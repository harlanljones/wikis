# Case

> **In one sentence:** pick a case your parts fit in, with a mesh front so air can get through, and after that choose on looks and size.

## What it does

The case holds the parts, directs air over them and keeps dust and fingers out. It has no direct effect on speed, but a case that starves the parts of air makes them hotter, louder and eventually slower.

It is also the part you will look at every day and the one most likely to be reused in your next build, so it is reasonable to choose one you like.

## What to buy

| You want | Look for |
| --- | --- |
| The easy default | A mid-tower with a mesh front and two or three included fans, $70 to $110 |
| A tight budget | A Micro-ATX mesh case with at least two fans, $50 to $70 |
| A showpiece | A "dual chamber" or panoramic glass case, and budget for extra fans |
| A small PC | A Mini-ITX case of 10 to 20 liters. Check every clearance twice |
| Silence | A case with sound-damped panels and large, slow fans |

## The specs, explained

### Motherboard support (form factor)

**Matters: essential.**

A case lists the [[parts/motherboard|motherboard]] sizes it accepts. A larger case accepts smaller boards: an ATX case takes ATX, Micro-ATX and Mini-ITX. A Micro-ATX case does not take ATX.

Case size names are loose. Roughly:

| Name | Takes | Typical volume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full tower | E-ATX and below | 60 liters or more |
| Mid-tower | ATX and below | 40 to 55 liters |
| Micro-ATX tower | Micro-ATX and below | 25 to 40 liters |
| Small form factor (SFF) | Mini-ITX | under 20 liters |

### Clearances

**Matters: essential. Check all three.**

- **Maximum GPU length.** Compare with your [[parts/gpu|graphics card]]'s length. Also check thickness on very large cards.
- **Maximum CPU cooler height.** Compare with your [[parts/cooling|air cooler]].
- **Radiator support.** Which sizes fit, and where (front, top, side).

Also check **PSU length** in compact cases.

### Airflow

**Matters: a lot.**

Air needs a way in. A **mesh** or perforated front panel lets fans breathe. A solid glass or plastic front with thin side slots can raise temperatures by 5 to 10°C and makes fans work harder and louder. This is the most important practical difference between cases.

### Included fans

**Matters: for value.**

A case with three decent fans included is worth $20 to $30 more than one with a single fan. Check how many are included and whether they are PWM (speed-controllable).

### Front panel ports

**Matters: day to day.**

Look for at least two USB-A ports, a USB-C port and a headphone jack on the front or top. The front USB-C port only works if your motherboard has the matching internal header.

### Drive bays

**Matters: only if you use them.**

M.2 SSDs mount on the motherboard and need no bay. Bays matter if you want 3.5-inch hard drives for bulk storage or 2.5-inch SATA SSDs. Many modern cases have room for only one or two.

### Dust filters

**Matters: for upkeep.**

Removable filters on the front, top and under the power supply make cleaning a two-minute job.

## Specs you can mostly ignore

- **Tempered glass thickness.**
- **The number of fan mounts**, beyond the handful you will use.
- **Built-in RGB controllers and fan hubs**, unless you need them.
- **"Tool-less" claims.** You will need one Phillips screwdriver whatever the box says.
- **Vertical GPU mounts.** They look good and usually run the card warmer.

## Going deeper

- **Dual-chamber cases** put the power supply and drives behind the motherboard tray. They are wide, show the parts off well, and have room for many fans. Glass-fronted ones rely on side and bottom intake, so they need those fans populated to cool well.
- **Fish-tank cases need fans.** Many panoramic cases ship with none. Budget for six.
- **Back-connect compatibility.** Motherboards with rear-facing connectors need a case with matching cutouts in the tray.
- **Small form factor builds** are rewarding and unforgiving. Everything has a clearance, parts go in a set order, SFX power supplies and low-profile coolers cost more, and temperatures run higher. Not recommended as a first build.
- **GPU sag.** Heavy cards droop over time. A support bracket costs a few dollars, and many cards and cases include one.
- **Noise.** Mesh cases let more fan noise out and need less fan speed. Solid damped cases block noise and need more fan speed. For a quiet PC, large slow fans in a mesh case usually win.
- **Cable management.** Look for 20mm or more of space behind the motherboard tray, rubber grommets or cutouts near the board's edges, and tie-down points. Tidy cables are for airflow at the front and for your sanity later.
- **Reusing an old case** is fine if it has front intake and fits modern long graphics cards. Very old cases often lack both.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a sealed glass-front case for a hot build.
- Not checking GPU length.
- Buying a panoramic case and forgetting it comes without fans.
- Buying a Mini-ITX case for a first build to "save space".
- Forgetting motherboard standoffs, or installing extra ones where the board has no hole.
- Removing every expansion slot cover instead of just the ones the graphics card needs.
