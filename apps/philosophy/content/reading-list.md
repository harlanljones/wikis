# Reading list

Where to start with the original texts. The classic works of political philosophy are shorter and more readable than their reputation suggests, and most of them are in the public domain and free online.

## How to read them

- **Start with the short ones.** A reader with a free weekend can finish the first list below.
- **Read selections.** Nobody begins by reading *Leviathan* cover to cover. Each thinker page ends with the chapters to read first.
- **Read in pairs.** These books answer each other. Reading two sides of an argument teaches more than reading either alone, and the [[debates]] pages set out six such pairs.
- **Use a modern edition for translations.** Old public-domain translations of Plato, Rousseau, Kant, Hegel and Marx are often stiff or misleading. Recent editions from Hackett, Penguin, Oxford World's Classics and Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought are reliable.
- **Expect to disagree.** Every author here says things that are wrong, and several say things that are ugly. The point is to see how the argument works.

## Introductions and reference works

These are widely used and reliable.

- **Jonathan Wolff, *An Introduction to Political Philosophy*.** The best short first book, organised by question.
- **Will Kymlicka, *Contemporary Political Philosophy*.** The standard guide to the debates since Rawls, tradition by tradition.
- **Alan Ryan, *On Politics*.** A long, readable history from Herodotus to the present by a single author.
- **Michael Sandel, *Justice: What's the Right Thing to Do?*** Based on his Harvard course, built around cases.
- **The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** (plato.stanford.edu). Free, written by specialists and regularly revised. The first place to check anything on this wiki.
- **The *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** (iep.utm.edu). Free and often more approachable.
- **Steven B. Smith's "Introduction to Political Philosophy,"** a Yale lecture course available free online, covers Plato to Tocqueville.

## Free texts

Works by authors who died before the mid-twentieth century are generally out of copyright. Project Gutenberg, the Online Library of Liberty, the Marxists Internet Archive and the Perseus Digital Library (for Greek and Latin authors) hold most of them. Check the translator and date. A translation from the 1880s is free but may not be the one to learn from.
