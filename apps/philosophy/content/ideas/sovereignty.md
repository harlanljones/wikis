# Sovereignty

Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory: the power to make the final decision, with no higher authority to appeal to. Much of modern political thought is an argument about who holds it, whether it can be limited or divided, and whether the idea still makes sense.

## Where the idea came from

Medieval Europe had no sovereign in the modern sense. Authority was shared and overlapping among kings, nobles, towns, the Church and the Empire. The concept was forged in the religious wars of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, as an answer to chaos.

The French jurist **Jean Bodin**, writing in 1576 during France's wars of religion, defined sovereignty as "the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth." Its mark is the power to give law to all without their consent. Bodin held that sovereignty cannot be divided, though the sovereign remains under divine and natural law.

[[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] pushed the logic through. If there are two powers in a state, each claiming the last word, there will be war between them. So the sovereign must hold all the powers of government and cannot be bound by the laws it makes. For Hobbes this was the lesson of the English Civil War.

The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 is conventionally taken as the start of a system of sovereign states, each supreme at home and recognising no superior abroad. Historians now regard this as a simplification, but the label "Westphalian" has stuck.

## Who is sovereign?

| Answer | Thinker | In short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ruler | Bodin, [[thinkers/hobbes\|Hobbes]] | One will must decide, or there is no state |
| The legislature, in trust | [[thinkers/locke\|Locke]] | Supreme while it keeps its trust. The people keep a final power to remove it |
| The people | [[thinkers/rousseau\|Rousseau]] | Sovereignty is the general will. It cannot be handed over, represented or divided |
| No one in particular | [[thinkers/montesquieu\|Montesquieu]], [[thinkers/madison\|Madison]] | Divide power so that no single body has the last word on everything |
| Whoever decides the exception | [[thinkers/schmitt\|Schmitt]] | The real sovereign appears in an emergency, when the rules run out |

**Popular sovereignty**, the doctrine that all authority comes from the people, won the argument in principle. Almost every constitution now claims it. It raises its own questions. Who counts as "the people"? And does the people's will have limits? [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] and [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] feared that a sovereign people could be as oppressive as a king.

## Can it be limited or divided?

Hobbes thought limited sovereignty a contradiction. Whoever enforces the limit is the real sovereign. The constitutional tradition replies that a system can work without any single final authority. In the United States power is divided between the federal government and the states, and among three branches. Asking where sovereignty "really" lies there gets no clean answer, and the system has lasted. See [[ideas/separation-of-powers|separation of powers]].

Schmitt took Hobbes's side. Constitutions conceal the sovereign but cannot abolish it. In a real crisis someone will decide. Critics answer that building a theory on the extreme case mistakes the breakdown of law for its foundation.

## Two faces

- **Internal sovereignty** is supremacy over everyone within the territory. [[thinkers/weber|Weber's]] definition of the state as holding a monopoly of legitimate force is a version of it.
- **External sovereignty** is independence from outside powers, and the equal standing of states in international law.

## Is it out of date?

Since 1945, external sovereignty has been qualified by human rights treaties, international courts, and bodies such as the European Union, whose members have pooled some of their powers. The doctrine of a "responsibility to protect," endorsed by the United Nations in 2005, holds that a state which fails to protect its people from mass atrocities loses its claim to non-interference.

Economic globalisation limits what any government can do in practice. And [[thinkers/foucault|Foucault]] argued that the whole picture of power as a sovereign's command hides the ways modern power actually works.

Sovereignty still matters to those who lack it. Anti-colonial movements fought to gain it, and arguments over immigration, trade and international institutions are often arguments about who decides. See [[ideas/power|power]] and [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].
