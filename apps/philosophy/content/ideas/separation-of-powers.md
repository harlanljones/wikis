# Separation of powers

The separation of powers is the principle that the main functions of government, making law, carrying it out and judging disputes under it, should be in different hands, so that no person or body holds all of them. Its purpose is to protect liberty by making power check power.

## Two ideas often run together

- The **mixed constitution** shares power among social classes or forms of rule: the one, the few and the many.
- The **separation of powers** divides government by function: legislative, executive and judicial.

The first is ancient and the second modern. The American Constitution draws on both, and adds a third device, **checks and balances**, which gives each branch some hold over the others.

## The mixed constitution

[[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] recommended a blend of oligarchy and democracy as the most stable constitution. The Greek historian Polybius, explaining Rome's rise in the second century BC, argued that each pure form decays in a cycle and that Rome had escaped by combining all three: consuls, Senate and people, each able to obstruct the others. [[thinkers/cicero|Cicero]] and later [[thinkers/machiavelli|Machiavelli]] took over the argument. English writers applied it to King, Lords and Commons.

## The modern doctrine

[[thinkers/locke|Locke]] distinguished the legislative power from the executive and argued that they should usually be in separate hands, because it is too great a temptation for those who make the laws also to execute them and so exempt themselves.

[[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] gave the doctrine its lasting form in 1748. Anyone with power tends to abuse it, and will go on until they meet a limit. So "power should be a check to power." He named three powers and warned that liberty is lost if the same person or body holds any two. Above all the power of judging must be separate, since a judge who is also legislator or enforcer holds the citizen's life and liberty at will.

[[thinkers/madison|Madison]] turned the principle into engineering. A paper declaration that the branches are separate is not enough. Each must have "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others. … Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." Hence the American arrangements: a president who can veto laws, a Congress that can override the veto and remove the president, a Senate that confirms judges, and courts that can strike down laws.

## Two systems

| | Presidential (United States) | Parliamentary (United Kingdom) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive | Elected separately from the legislature | Drawn from the legislature and dependent on its confidence |
| Separation | Strict, with shared powers as checks | Legislature and executive are fused |
| Main check | Branches blocking each other | Opposition, elections and convention |
| Courts | May strike down legislation | Traditionally may not, under parliamentary sovereignty |

The English journalist Walter Bagehot wrote in 1867 that the "efficient secret" of the English constitution was the opposite of what [[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] had seen in it: the near-complete fusion of executive and legislative power in the cabinet. Montesquieu's England was partly imagined. The fact that parliamentary systems have protected liberty suggests that an independent judiciary matters more than a strict division of the other two.

## Federalism

Power can also be divided by territory, between a central government and states or provinces. [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] called the combination of federalism with the separation of branches a "double security" for rights. [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] saw local self-government as the school of liberty.

## Objections

- **Sovereignty.** [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] held that divided power means civil war, and [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] that sovereignty cannot be cut into pieces. [[thinkers/schmitt|Schmitt]] argued that in an emergency someone decides, whatever the constitution says.
- **Gridlock.** A system built to stop bad laws also stops good ones. Woodrow Wilson, before he became president, criticised the American system on these grounds.
- **Parties.** If one disciplined party holds every branch, ambition no longer counteracts ambition. If opposed parties hold different branches, the result may be paralysis.
- **Democracy.** Checks on government are checks on majorities. Whether that is a defect depends on one's view of [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].
- **The administrative state.** Modern agencies make rules, enforce them and judge violations, which fits none of Montesquieu's boxes.

## Why it matters

The underlying thought is older and simpler than any institutional scheme. No one should be judge in their own case, and anyone who holds power needs someone able to say no. The same idea lies behind an independent press, auditors and central banks, and the **rule of law**: government bound by known rules that apply to rulers as well as ruled.
