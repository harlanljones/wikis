# Power

Power is the capacity to get things done, and in politics above all the capacity to affect what other people do. Theories of power differ over whether it is something that individuals hold, something built into institutions, or something that runs through all social relations.

## Power over and power to

Two senses are worth separating at the start.

- **Power over** is the ability to make others do what they would not otherwise do. This is domination or control.
- **Power to** is the ability to achieve something, alone or together. This is capacity.

Most political theory has been about the first. [[thinkers/arendt|Arendt]] insisted on the second.

## The classic view

[[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] defined a person's power as their "present means to obtain some future apparent good," and saw in everyone a restless desire for more of it, since no one can secure what they have without gaining more. Political order is the gathering of everyone's power into one sovereign.

[[thinkers/weber|Weber]] gave the standard modern definition. Power is the chance of a person or group to carry out their own will in a social relationship, even against resistance. The state is the body that holds a monopoly of legitimate force. He distinguished raw power from **authority**, which is power that those subject to it accept as rightful. See [[ideas/authority-and-obligation|authority and obligation]].

On this view power is a possession. Someone has it and uses it on someone else.

## Three faces of power

American political scientists argued in the 1950s and 1960s over how to tell who has power in a community. The British theorist Steven Lukes summed up the debate in *Power: A Radical View* (1974) as three dimensions.

| Face | Power is | How to find it | Associated with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First | Winning open conflicts over decisions | See whose side prevails | Robert Dahl |
| Second | Keeping issues off the agenda, so that no decision is ever taken | See what is never discussed | Peter Bachrach and Morton Baratz |
| Third | Shaping what people want, so that they accept their situation | Ask what they would want if they could choose freely | Lukes |

The third face is the most troubling. The most effective power prevents conflict from arising at all, by forming people's beliefs and desires. It draws on [[thinkers/marx|Marx's]] idea of **ideology** and on the Italian Marxist Antonio Gramsci's idea of **hegemony**: a ruling group leads not only by force but by making its outlook the common sense of society. The difficulty is knowing what people's "real" interests are without simply imposing one's own view.

## Foucault: power without a king

[[thinkers/foucault|Foucault]] rejected the whole picture of power as a thing held by a sovereign and used to forbid. Power, on his account, is exercised from countless points. It works through the routines of schools, hospitals, prisons and offices. It does not only repress. It **produces** skills, norms, forms of knowledge and kinds of people. And it comes with resistance wherever it appears.

Critics, including [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]], ask how Foucault can object to any use of power if nothing lies outside it.

## Arendt: power is not violence

[[thinkers/arendt|Arendt]] turned the usual view around. Power is what arises when people act together. It belongs to the group and lasts as long as the group holds. **Violence** is a different thing: a tool, used by one against many. "Power and violence are opposites; where the one rules absolutely, the other is absent." A government that must rely on violence alone has already lost its power. The sudden collapse of regimes when soldiers refuse to fire is her evidence.

## Domination

[[traditions/republicanism|Republican]] theorists such as Philip Pettit define **domination** as the capacity to interfere arbitrarily in another's choices, whether or not the capacity is used. An employee who can be dismissed at will, or a wife who is economically dependent on her husband, is dominated even if never mistreated. See [[ideas/liberty|liberty]].

[[traditions/feminism|Feminist]] theorists extended the analysis of power from the state to the household, the workplace and intimate life. Iris Marion Young listed five "faces of oppression": exploitation, marginalisation, powerlessness, cultural imperialism and violence.

## Soft power and hard power

In international relations, [[traditions/realism|realists]] treat power, measured mainly in military and economic strength, as the currency of politics among states. The American scholar Joseph Nye added the notion of **soft power**: getting others to want what you want, through the attraction of a country's culture and values.

## What to do about power

The traditions answer differently.

- [[traditions/liberalism|Liberals]] and [[traditions/republicanism|republicans]] seek to limit and divide it. Lord Acton's line is the motto: "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely." See [[ideas/separation-of-powers|separation of powers]].
- [[traditions/socialism|Socialists]] hold that political equality means little while economic power is concentrated.
- [[traditions/anarchism|Anarchists]] aim to do away with coercive power as far as possible.
- [[traditions/realism|Realists]] hold that power can be balanced and restrained but never removed from politics.

See also [[ideas/sovereignty|sovereignty]].
