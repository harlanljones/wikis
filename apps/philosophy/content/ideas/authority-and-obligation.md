# Authority and obligation

Political authority is the right to rule: to make laws and have them obeyed. Political obligation is the other side of it, the duty of citizens to obey. The central question is whether there is any such duty, and if so what grounds it and where it ends.

## Power, authority and legitimacy

A gunman has power over you. He does not have authority. Authority is power that claims to be **rightful**, and a government differs from a gang of robbers, if it does, by having a right to command and not merely the means. [[thinkers/augustine|Augustine]] asked what kingdoms are without justice but great bands of robbers.

Two senses of **legitimacy** should be kept apart.

- In the **descriptive** sense, used by [[thinkers/weber|Weber]], a government is legitimate when its subjects believe it has the right to rule. He named three sources of such belief: tradition, charisma and legal rules.
- In the **normative** sense, a government is legitimate when it really has that right, whatever people believe.

Philosophers mostly ask the second question.

## The problem

The law does not just advise. It commands, and it claims that you should obey because it is the law, even when you disagree. Yet we normally think adults should act on their own judgement. What could give anyone that kind of standing over another adult? And the duty is supposed to be owed to one's own state in particular, not to just governments in general.

## Proposed answers

| Ground | The argument | The difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Consent** | You agreed to obey ([[thinkers/locke\|Locke]]) | Almost no one has actually agreed |
| **Tacit consent** | By staying and using the roads, you agreed | [[thinkers/hume\|Hume]]: a poor man has no real choice to leave |
| **Gratitude** | The state has raised and protected you, as parents do (Socrates in the *Crito*) | Benefits you did not ask for do not create debts |
| **Fair play** | You take the benefits of others' obedience, so you owe your share (H. L. A. Hart, [[thinkers/rawls\|Rawls]]) | [[thinkers/nozick\|Nozick]]: others cannot put you in debt by giving you benefits unasked |
| **Utility** | Obedience generally has better results than disobedience ([[thinkers/bentham\|Bentham]]) | Then the duty disappears whenever disobeying would do more good |
| **Natural duty** | Everyone has a duty to support just institutions ([[thinkers/rawls\|Rawls]], after [[thinkers/kant\|Kant]]) | Why the institutions of my country in particular? |
| **Membership** | Obligations come with belonging, as in a family (Ronald Dworkin) | Not every group one is born into deserves loyalty |
| **Democracy** | The law was made by a process in which you had an equal say | Does losing a vote bind the loser? |

The earliest discussion is [[thinkers/plato|Plato's]] *Crito*. Socrates, condemned to death, refuses to escape. He imagines the Laws of Athens telling him that they gave him birth and education, that he was free to leave and stayed, and that to break them now would be to destroy the city so far as he can.

## The anarchist reply

**Philosophical anarchists** conclude that none of these arguments works and that there is no general duty to obey the law. Robert Paul Wolff argued in 1970 that authority is incompatible with moral autonomy, since a responsible person never does something just because they are told to. A. John Simmons argues, more modestly, that most citizens have simply not done anything that would bind them.

This position need not mean lawbreaking. There are good moral reasons to do most of what the law requires: not to kill, not to steal, to drive on the agreed side. The claim is that the law's say-so adds nothing. See [[traditions/anarchism|anarchism]].

The legal philosopher Joseph Raz offered a way between. Authority is justified when a person does better at acting on the reasons that apply to them by following its directives than by working things out alone. Authorities, on this view, serve their subjects. Where they do not, they have no claim.

## Disobedience and resistance

Few have held that the duty to obey is absolute. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] came close, but allowed that no one is bound to submit to being killed. [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] denied any right of revolution.

- [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] held that an unjust law does not bind in conscience.
- [[thinkers/locke|Locke]] defended a right of the people to resist and replace a government that breaks its trust.
- **Civil disobedience** is the public, non-violent breaking of a law, with acceptance of the penalty, to protest an injustice. Henry David Thoreau, who went to jail in 1846 for refusing a tax that supported slavery and war, gave it its name. Mohandas Gandhi made it a mass method, and Martin Luther King Jr. defended it in his "Letter from Birmingham Jail": one who breaks an unjust law openly and lovingly, and accepts the penalty, shows the highest respect for law.
- [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] treated civil disobedience as an appeal to the majority's sense of justice within a nearly just society. Others ask what is permitted when a society is not nearly just, and whether disobedience must always be non-violent and penalty-accepting.
