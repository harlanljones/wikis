# Toleration

Toleration is the practice of putting up with beliefs and ways of life one thinks are wrong, when one has the power to suppress them. It grew out of Europe's wars of religion and became a founding principle of liberalism.

## What it is

Toleration has three parts.

1. **Objection.** One disapproves of the belief or practice. People do not "tolerate" what they like or do not care about.
2. **Power.** One could interfere.
3. **Restraint.** One chooses not to, for reasons that outweigh the objection.

This makes toleration an odd virtue. It asks people to allow what they believe to be error. The arguments for it try to explain why that can be right.

## The background

For most of European history, enforcing the true religion was thought to be a duty of rulers. [[thinkers/augustine|Augustine]], after first opposing coercion, defended the use of state penalties against heretics as a harsh kindness. [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] held that heretics could be handed over for execution, as forgers of money were.

The Reformation split Western Christianity and led to more than a century of persecution and war. The first settlements were practical. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) let each German prince choose the religion of his territory. The Edict of Nantes (1598) gave French Protestants limited rights, which Louis XIV took away in 1685. Arguments of principle followed.

## The classic arguments

**Belief cannot be forced.** [[thinkers/locke|Locke's]] *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) holds that true religion is inward conviction, and that force can produce outward conformity but not belief. Persecution is therefore irrational even on the persecutor's own terms. He adds that the state exists to protect civil goods such as life, liberty and property, that no one has handed the care of their soul to the magistrate, and that churches are voluntary societies.

Locke set limits. He would not tolerate atheists, since "promises, covenants, and oaths" could have no hold on them, or churches whose members owe obedience to a foreign prince, by which he meant Catholics.

**Fallibility.** We might be wrong. [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] argued in *On Liberty* that to silence an opinion is to assume one's own infallibility, and that even false opinions keep true ones alive by challenging them.

**Peace.** Whatever the truth, attempts to impose it bring endless conflict. This was the lesson drawn by the *politiques* of sixteenth-century France, and it is close to [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes's]] concern, though he drew the opposite conclusion and gave the sovereign control of public doctrine.

**Conscience.** Pierre Bayle, a French Protestant exile, argued in the 1680s that even a mistaken conscience must be respected, and that this covers Jews, Muslims and atheists too. [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] went beyond toleration altogether. In drafting Virginia's declaration of rights he had "toleration" replaced with the "free exercise" of religion, because toleration implies a favour granted by those in charge, where religious liberty is a right.

**Respect.** [[thinkers/kant|Kantian]] arguments hold that people are entitled to form and follow their own view of how to live. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] built his later theory on "the fact of reasonable pluralism": free people reasoning in good faith will arrive at different religions and philosophies, and this is a permanent feature of a free society, not a failure to be cured.

## Problems

**The paradox of tolerance.** Karl Popper, writing in 1945, argued that unlimited tolerance defeats itself. If a society tolerates those who would destroy tolerance, the tolerant will be destroyed. A tolerant society may therefore claim the right not to tolerate the intolerant, at least when they turn from argument to force. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] took a more cautious line: the intolerant should be restricted only when the constitution is really in danger.

**Is toleration enough?** To be tolerated is to be put up with. Goethe wrote that "to tolerate is to insult." Many minorities ask not for grudging permission but for equal respect and **recognition**. The Canadian philosopher Charles Taylor made this the centre of his account of multicultural societies.

**Is it a mask?** The Marxist philosopher Herbert Marcuse argued in "Repressive Tolerance" (1965) that in an unequal society the tolerance of all views in practice favours the powerful, whose views already dominate. Critics replied that his remedy, withdrawing tolerance from the right, assumes someone can be trusted to decide.

**Minorities within minorities.** Should a liberal state tolerate groups that restrict the freedom of their own members, especially women and children? The feminist Susan Moller Okin asked whether multiculturalism is bad for women. Others, such as Chandran Kukathas, reply that toleration must extend to illiberal communities so long as members are free to leave.

**Where the line falls.** Disputes over religious dress, religious exemptions from general laws, and offensive or hateful speech are arguments about the boundary between what must be tolerated and what may be restricted. See [[debates/harm-principle|the harm principle]].
