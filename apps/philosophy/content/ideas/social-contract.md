# The social contract

The social contract is the idea that political authority is legitimate because the people subject to it have agreed to it, or would agree to it if asked under fair conditions. It is the main modern answer to the question of why anyone has the right to rule.

## The basic move

Contract theories share a three-step shape.

1. Describe people without government, in a [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]].
2. Show what goes wrong there.
3. Work out what such people would agree to in order to fix it. The terms of that agreement tell us what a legitimate government is and where its limits lie.

Underneath is a moral claim. People are naturally free and equal, so no one is born with authority over anyone else. Authority has to be created, and only the consent of the governed can create it. That claim is what sets the contract tradition apart from the divine right of kings before it and from [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle's]] view that political life is natural.

## The main versions

| Thinker | Who agrees to what | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[thinkers/hobbes\|Hobbes]] | Each gives up the right to govern themselves to a sovereign, who is not a party to the deal | Near-absolute government, for the sake of peace |
| [[thinkers/locke\|Locke]] | People entrust a government with protecting their life, liberty and property | Limited government, which can be removed if it breaks the trust |
| [[thinkers/rousseau\|Rousseau]] | Each gives everything to the whole community and becomes part of the sovereign people | Direct self-rule under the general will |
| [[thinkers/kant\|Kant]] | No actual agreement. The contract is "an idea of reason" | A test for laws: could a whole people have consented? |
| [[thinkers/rawls\|Rawls]] | Parties behind a veil of ignorance choose principles of justice | Equal liberties and the difference principle |

See [[debates/hobbes-locke-rousseau|Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau]] for a closer comparison of the first three.

## Actual or hypothetical?

The oldest objection is that the contract never happened. [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] pointed out that nearly all governments began in force, and that most people have never consented to anything. Locke's reply, that people consent "tacitly" by living in a country and using its roads, assumes they have a real choice to leave.

Later theorists gave up the claim of history. For Kant and Rawls the contract is hypothetical: a way of working out what would be fair. That change has a cost, which the legal philosopher Ronald Dworkin put sharply. A hypothetical contract is not a pale form of an actual contract. It is no contract at all. If you would have agreed to something, that does not mean you are bound as if you had.

The defenders' answer is that the hypothetical contract was never meant to bind in that way. It is a device for thinking impartially. Its force comes from the fairness of the conditions under which the imagined choice is made.

## Two families of modern contract theory

Present-day versions fall into two groups, named after their ancestors.

- **Hobbesian** (contractarian) theories start from self-interest. Rules are justified if rational people, each seeking their own advantage, would accept them as a bargain. David Gauthier is the leading example.
- **Kantian** (contractualist) theories start from equal respect. Rules are justified if no one could reasonably reject them. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] and T. M. Scanlon belong here.

## Who is left out

A second line of criticism asks who the parties to the contract are.

- **Carole Pateman** argued in *The Sexual Contract* (1988) that the classic theories assume a prior agreement among men about access to women. The "individuals" who contract are heads of households.
- **Charles Mills** argued in *The Racial Contract* (1997) that the actual historical contract was among white people, and that it set the terms on which non-white people were excluded and exploited.
- [[thinkers/nussbaum|Martha Nussbaum]] argued that a contract for mutual advantage among rough equals has no place for people with severe disabilities, for foreigners or for animals.

Other traditions reject the starting point. [[thinkers/burke|Burke]] and [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]] deny that society is a deal that individuals make. [[traditions/communitarianism|Communitarians]] say that people are shaped by their communities before they can choose anything. [[traditions/utilitarianism|Utilitarians]] such as [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]] say the contract is an unnecessary fiction, since what justifies government is the good it does.

## Why it lasts

For all that, the contract remains the usual way of asking whether a set of rules is fair. Would the people who must live under them accept them, if they chose freely and on equal terms? See [[ideas/authority-and-obligation|authority and obligation]] and [[ideas/justice|justice]].
