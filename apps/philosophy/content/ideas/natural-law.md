# Natural law

Natural law is the idea that there are moral principles built into human nature or reason, which hold everywhere and which the laws of any state can be measured against. On this view a law that violates them is defective as law, however properly it was passed.

## The core claims

1. There is a moral order that does not depend on anyone's decision.
2. Human beings can know it by reason, without special revelation.
3. Human laws get their authority from agreeing with it.

The third claim is the political one. It gives a standard above the ruler. It is what a person appeals to when they say that a law is legal but wrong, and that the wrongness matters.

## A short history

**The ancient world.** In Sophocles' play *Antigone*, the heroine buries her brother against the king's order and appeals to "unwritten and unfailing" laws of the gods. [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] distinguished what is just by nature from what is just by convention. The Stoics taught that the universe is ordered by reason and that all humans, sharing in reason, are citizens of one world. [[thinkers/cicero|Cicero]] gave this its classic Latin form: "True law is right reason in agreement with nature."

**The Christian synthesis.** [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] placed natural law within a larger scheme. It is the part of God's eternal law that human reason can grasp, starting from the precept that good is to be done and evil avoided. A human law contrary to it is "a perversion of law."

**The modern turn to rights.** In the seventeenth century the emphasis moved from duties to rights. The Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius argued that natural law would hold even if, as he said one could not grant without wickedness, there were no God. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] reduced the "right of nature" to self-preservation. [[thinkers/locke|Locke]] made natural law the basis of rights to life, liberty and property that governments exist to protect. The American Declaration of Independence and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man are written in this language. See [[ideas/rights|rights]].

## The attack

Three objections did lasting damage.

- **Is and ought.** [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] observed that one cannot get a conclusion about what ought to be from premises about what is. Facts about human nature do not by themselves yield duties.
- **Disagreement.** If natural law is knowable by reason, why do sincere people disagree about it? Slavery, the subordination of women and the persecution of heretics have all been defended as natural.
- **Positivism.** [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]] and his follower John Austin argued that law is simply what the sovereign commands. "The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another." Mixing the two confuses thought and invites either anarchy or complacency.

## The return

Natural law revived after 1945. The Nuremberg trials convicted Nazi officials for acts that were lawful under German law at the time. That seemed to require a standard above positive law.

- The German jurist Gustav Radbruch, a former positivist, concluded that a statute which is intolerably unjust loses its character as law.
- The **Hart and Fuller debate** of 1958 set the positivist H. L. A. Hart, who held that an evil law is still law though it may be right to disobey it, against Lon Fuller, who argued that law has an "inner morality": rules must be general, public, clear, consistent and not retroactive, or there is no legal system at all.
- **Martin Luther King Jr.**, writing from Birmingham jail in 1963, cited [[thinkers/augustine|Augustine]] and Aquinas: "An unjust law is a human law that is not rooted in eternal law and natural law."
- **John Finnis** restated the theory in *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980), basing it on a set of basic human goods instead of on facts about nature.

International human rights law is, in practice, a natural law idea: rights that people hold simply as human beings, whatever their state says.

## Where it stands

The dispute between natural lawyers and positivists is partly about a definition. Almost everyone now agrees that laws can be judged morally and that very bad laws should sometimes be disobeyed. What remains at issue is whether morality is part of what makes something a law in the first place.
