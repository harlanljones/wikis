# Property

Property is the set of rules that say who may use, control and dispose of things. Political thinkers have asked what could justify one person's right to exclude everyone else from a piece of the world, and whether private ownership protects freedom or threatens it.

## What property is

Lawyers describe ownership as a **bundle of rights**: to use a thing, to keep others from it, to earn income from it, to sell it, give it away or leave it to heirs. The rights can be separated and limited. A person may own land without the right to build on it or to pollute from it.

Property can be held in different ways.

- **Private** property belongs to individuals or companies.
- **Common** property is open to all members of a group, such as a village pasture.
- **State** or collective property is controlled by public authority.

The philosophical question is mostly about private property in land and productive resources. Hardly anyone objects to people owning their toothbrushes.

## Arguments for private property

### It works better

[[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] made the first sustained case, against [[thinkers/plato|Plato's]] proposal that the guardians hold everything in common. What belongs to everyone is cared for by no one. People quarrel less and work harder when each has their own. But he added that the use of property should be generous: private ownership, common use among friends.

[[thinkers/hume|Hume]] and the [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarians]] took the same line. Property is a convention, and it is justified by its results. Stable possession lets people plan, invest and trade. [[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]] and [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] added that markets, which coordinate the knowledge of millions, depend on it.

### Labour makes it mine

[[thinkers/locke|Locke]] gave the classic natural rights argument. The earth was given to humankind in common. But each person owns themselves and their labour, and whatever they mix their labour with becomes theirs, provided there is "enough, and as good" left for others and nothing is left to spoil.

The puzzles are well known. [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick]], who accepted the theory, asked why mixing what I own with what I do not own is a way of gaining the second and not of losing the first. If I pour my can of tomato juice into the sea, do I own the sea? And once all the land is taken, what is left of "enough, and as good"?

### It makes a person

[[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]] argued that people need property in order to be persons at all. By owning and shaping things, the will gains an existence in the outside world. This argument suggests that everyone should own something, which is not what markets guarantee.

### It protects freedom

An old [[traditions/republicanism|republican]] thought, shared by many [[traditions/liberalism|liberals]], is that property gives independence. A person who owns their means of living does not need anyone's permission to speak or vote as they choose. Widely spread property is a check on the state.

## Arguments against

[[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] located the origin of inequality, and of most human misery, in the first person who fenced a piece of land and said "this is mine." He did not propose abolishing property, but he held that no citizen should be rich enough to buy another.

The French anarchist Pierre-Joseph Proudhon gave the most famous answer to the question in the title of his 1840 book *What Is Property?* It is theft. He meant ownership that lets a person live off others' work through rent and interest, and he defended a person's possession of what they use. See [[traditions/anarchism|anarchism]].

[[thinkers/marx|Marx]] distinguished personal belongings from private ownership of the **means of production**: land, factories, machines. Those who own them can make everyone else work for them on their terms. The freedom of contract between an owner and a worker who must sell their labour or starve is, on this view, a formal freedom that hides a real compulsion. See [[traditions/socialism|socialism]].

## The middle positions

Most modern thought sits between full private ownership and its abolition.

- [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] held that private property is a useful human arrangement, limited by the claims of those in need.
- Thomas Paine, in *Agrarian Justice* (1797), argued that since the earth was originally common, landowners owe everyone else a ground rent, to be paid as a lump sum to each person on coming of age. This is an ancestor of proposals for a basic income.
- [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] defended property in what one has produced but questioned inheritance and private ownership of land.
- [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] counted the right to personal property among the basic liberties but not the right to own means of production. He favoured a "property-owning democracy" in which capital is widely held.
- **Left-libertarians** combine full self-ownership with the view that natural resources belong to everyone equally.

## Newer questions

Intellectual property raises the question of why ideas, which can be shared without loss, should be owned at all. The economist Elinor Ostrom showed that communities often manage common resources well without either private owners or the state, against the assumption of an inevitable "tragedy of the commons." And claims by indigenous peoples to land taken under colonial rule test every theory of just acquisition. Locke's own theory was used to deny those claims.
