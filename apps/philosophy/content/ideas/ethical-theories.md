# Ethical theories

Moral philosophy has three main families of theory about what makes an action right: its consequences, its agreement with duty, or its place in a good character. Most political arguments rest on one of them, so it helps to know the shapes.

## The three families

| | Consequentialism | Deontology | Virtue ethics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The right act is the one that | Produces the best results | Follows a rule or respects a right, whatever the results | A person of good character would do |
| Central question | What will happen? | What is my duty? | What kind of person should I be? |
| Key idea | The good to be promoted | The right, which limits what may be done for the good | Flourishing and the virtues |
| Classic source | [[thinkers/bentham\|Bentham]], [[thinkers/mill\|Mill]] | [[thinkers/kant\|Kant]] | [[thinkers/aristotle\|Aristotle]] |
| In politics | Cost-benefit analysis, welfare economics | Rights, constitutions, human dignity | Civic virtue, character, community |

### Consequentialism

Consequentialists judge actions by their outcomes alone. The best-known version is [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarianism]], which says the right action is the one that produces the greatest total happiness or welfare, counting everyone equally.

Its appeal is plain. It is impartial, it gives a method for settling questions, and it keeps attention on whether people's lives actually go better. Its difficulty is that it seems to permit anything if the sums come out right, including punishing an innocent person to calm a mob.

### Deontology

Deontological theories (from the Greek for duty) hold that some things may not be done whatever the consequences. [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] argued that morality is a matter of acting on principles that could hold for everyone, and of treating persons always as ends and never merely as means. Theories of natural and human [[ideas/rights|rights]] are deontological in structure. A right is a limit on what may be done to a person for the sake of others.

The appeal is that it protects individuals. The difficulties are what to do when duties conflict, and whether any rule should hold "though the heavens fall." Kant held that one may not lie even to a murderer asking where one's friend is hiding, and few have followed him.

### Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics begins from character. [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] asked what a flourishing human life is and answered that it is a life of virtues such as courage, justice, generosity and practical wisdom, which are acquired by practice. Right action is what a person with those virtues would do, and no rulebook can replace their judgement.

After long neglect, the approach was revived by Elizabeth Anscombe in 1958, and then by Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre and [[thinkers/nussbaum|Martha Nussbaum]]. Its critics say it gives little guidance in hard cases. "Do what the virtuous person would do" is not much help to someone who is not sure who the virtuous person is.

## The trolley problem

A thought experiment devised by Philippa Foot and developed by Judith Jarvis Thomson shows the families pulling apart. A runaway trolley will kill five people on the track. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one. Most people say you should.

Now you are on a bridge above the track. The only way to stop the trolley is to push a large man off the bridge into its path. He will die and the five will live. Most people say you should not.

The numbers are the same. A consequentialist sees no difference, or must explain it away. A deontologist says that in the second case you use a person as a means. The virtue ethicist asks what it would say about someone that they could do it.

## Other approaches

- **Contractualism.** Right and wrong are fixed by principles that everyone could agree to, or that no one could reasonably reject. This is the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] applied to morality. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] and T. M. Scanlon are the main sources.
- **Natural law.** Morality follows from the goods that fulfil human nature. See [[ideas/natural-law|natural law]] and [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]].
- **The ethics of care.** Developed by Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings in the 1980s, it starts from relationships of dependence and care instead of from rules or sums. See [[traditions/feminism|feminism]].
- **Moral sentiment.** [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] and [[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]] held that moral judgement is rooted in feeling and sympathy, not in reason alone.

## Questions about morality itself

A further set of questions, called metaethics, asks what moral claims are. Are there moral facts? [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] pointed out the gap between "is" and "ought." [[thinkers/nietzsche|Nietzsche]] argued that moral systems are historical creations that serve the needs of those who made them. [[thinkers/weber|Weber]] and [[thinkers/berlin|Berlin]], in different ways, held that ultimate values conflict and that reason cannot choose among them for us.

## How this bears on politics

- [[traditions/utilitarianism|Utilitarianism]] leads to judging institutions by total welfare.
- Deontology supports rights-based [[traditions/liberalism|liberalism]], whether in [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls's]] egalitarian form or [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick's]] libertarian one. Both call themselves Kantian.
- Virtue ethics underlies [[traditions/republicanism|republican]] concern with civic virtue and the [[traditions/communitarianism|communitarian]] view that politics should form character.

[[thinkers/machiavelli|Machiavelli]] and [[thinkers/weber|Weber]] raise a last possibility: that political leadership has its own ethic, which private morality does not fit. See [[traditions/realism|realism]].
