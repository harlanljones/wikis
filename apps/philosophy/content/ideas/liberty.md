# Liberty

Liberty, or freedom, is the condition of being able to act and live as one chooses. Nearly everyone values it, and political thinkers disagree deeply about what it is: being left alone, being one's own master, or not being under anyone's thumb.

## Three concepts

| Concept | A person is free when | Its typical enemy | Champions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Negative** liberty | No one interferes with what they could otherwise do | Coercion, by the state or anyone else | [[thinkers/hobbes\|Hobbes]], [[thinkers/bentham\|Bentham]], [[thinkers/mill\|Mill]], [[thinkers/berlin\|Berlin]], [[thinkers/hayek\|Hayek]] |
| **Positive** liberty | They govern themselves, by their own reason or as part of a self-governing people | Being ruled by passion, ignorance or others' will | [[thinkers/rousseau\|Rousseau]], [[thinkers/kant\|Kant]], [[thinkers/hegel\|Hegel]] |
| **Republican** liberty | No one has the power to interfere with them arbitrarily | Domination: having a master, even a kind one | [[thinkers/cicero\|Cicero]], [[thinkers/machiavelli\|Machiavelli]], Philip Pettit |

### Negative liberty

On the simplest view, freedom is the absence of obstacles put there by other people. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] defined a free man as one who "is not hindered to do what he has a will to." Law always reduces liberty to some degree, though it may be worth it. The more doors are open to me, the freer I am, whatever I do with them.

### Positive liberty

On the second view, an absence of interference is not enough. A person driven by addiction, or kept ignorant, or with no say in the laws they live under, is not free in any sense worth wanting. Freedom is self-rule. For [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] it is "obedience to the law one has prescribed for oneself."

[[thinkers/berlin|Isaiah Berlin]] drew this distinction in 1958 and warned that the positive concept is easily abused. Once freedom means rule by one's "true" self, others may claim to know that self better and to force people for their own good.

### Republican liberty

A third view was revived in the 1990s by the historian Quentin Skinner and the philosopher Philip Pettit, from Roman and Renaissance sources. Their test case is the slave of a kind master. The master happens never to interfere, so by the negative test the slave is free. That seems wrong. The slave lives at another's mercy and must flatter and tread carefully to stay in favour.

Freedom, on this view, is **non-domination**: not being subject to anyone's arbitrary power. Two things follow. A person can be unfree without being interfered with, as the slave is. And interference that is controlled by those it affects, such as a fair law they have a voice in, need not reduce freedom at all. See [[traditions/republicanism|republicanism]].

## Ancients and moderns

In a lecture of 1819 the Swiss-French writer Benjamin Constant compared two kinds of liberty. For the citizens of ancient Athens or Rome, liberty meant taking a direct part in collective power: voting on war and peace, judging, holding office. In private life they were closely controlled. For moderns, liberty means the peaceful enjoyment of private independence: the right to speak, work, worship and come and go without asking permission.

The error of [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] and the French revolutionaries, Constant argued, was to try to give modern people the liberty of the ancients. But he ended with a warning that points the other way. The danger of modern liberty is that, absorbed in private life, we give up our share in political power too easily. [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] made that danger his subject.

## Formal and effective freedom

A different dispute cuts across the first. Is a person free to do something when no law forbids it, or only when they are actually able to do it? The law, as the French writer Anatole France said, forbids rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges.

- Classical [[traditions/liberalism|liberals]] and [[traditions/libertarianism|libertarians]] hold that poverty is a lack of means, not a lack of freedom. To call it unfreedom confuses liberty with power or wealth.
- [[traditions/socialism|Socialists]] and egalitarian liberals reply that property rules are themselves enforced by coercion. Someone without money is stopped by the law from taking what they need. [[thinkers/sen|Sen's]] capabilities are a measure of freedom in this fuller sense.

## The limits of liberty

Even those who agree on what liberty is must say when it may be restricted. [[thinkers/mill|Mill's]] answer is the best known: only to prevent harm to others. See [[debates/harm-principle|the harm principle]].

A further question is how liberty ranks against other values. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] gives the basic liberties priority over economic gain. [[thinkers/berlin|Berlin]] thought liberty one value among several that cannot all be had together. See [[ideas/equality|equality]] for the supposed conflict between the two.
