# Rights

A right is a claim that a person is entitled to make on others: to be left alone, to be given something, or to be treated in a certain way. To say that someone has a right is to say that their interest or choice is protected even when overriding it would be convenient for everyone else.

## What rights do

The legal philosopher Ronald Dworkin described rights as **trumps**. In ordinary policy we weigh costs and benefits. A right takes a matter out of that weighing. If I have a right to speak, it is not enough to show that silencing me would make most people happier.

Rights come with **duties**. My right not to be assaulted is your duty not to assault me. This makes rights different from mere interests or wishes, and it is why loose talk of rights invites the question: who exactly has the duty?

## Useful distinctions

| Distinction | One side | The other |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | **Legal** rights, created by law | **Moral** or natural rights, held whatever the law says |
| Content | **Negative** rights, to be free from interference | **Positive** rights, to be provided with something |
| Holder | **Individual** rights | **Group** rights, such as a people's right to self-determination |

Historians of human rights sometimes speak of three generations: civil and political rights (speech, religion, fair trial, the vote), then social and economic rights (education, health care, an adequate standard of living), then collective rights (development, a healthy environment). The Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 includes the first two kinds.

## Where rights come from

**Natural rights.** The seventeenth century turned [[ideas/natural-law|natural law]] into natural rights. [[thinkers/locke|Locke]] held that people have rights to life, liberty and property before any government exists, and that governments are set up to protect them. The American Declaration of Independence (1776) calls such rights "unalienable," and the French Declaration of 1789 calls them "natural, inalienable and sacred."

**Dignity.** [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] grounded rights in the status of persons as ends in themselves. Modern human rights documents speak this language.

**Utility.** [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] argued that rights are rules which protect people's most vital interests, and that society does best overall by treating them as nearly inviolable.

**Agreement.** [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] derived basic liberties from what people would choose in a fair position.

**Capability.** [[thinkers/nussbaum|Nussbaum]] bases entitlements on what is needed for a life worthy of human dignity.

## The critics

Rights have been attacked from every side of politics.

- [[thinkers/burke|Burke]], from the right, held that the "rights of man" proclaimed in France were abstractions that ignored circumstances. Real rights are inherited and specific: the rights of Englishmen.
- [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]], a radical, called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts." Rights are made by law. To say a right exists where no law gives it is only to say that one wishes it did.
- [[thinkers/marx|Marx]], from the left, saw the rights of man as the rights of the isolated, self-interested individual, with property as the model. They free people politically while leaving them unequal in real life.
- [[traditions/communitarianism|Communitarians]] argue that a culture of rights crowds out talk of duty and the common good.
- Some [[traditions/feminism|feminists]] argue that rights assume a separate, self-sufficient individual and overlook relations of care and dependence. Others reply that women have needed rights precisely against those they depend on.

## Hard questions

**Are positive rights real rights?** Critics say a right to health care or housing has no definite duty-bearer and depends on resources. Defenders, such as Henry Shue, answer that negative rights are costly too. The right to a fair trial requires courts, and the right to security requires police. Every right needs institutions.

**Are human rights universal?** Critics from several traditions have called them a Western idea imposed on the rest of the world. Defenders reply that the demand not to be tortured, starved or silenced is not peculiar to any culture, and that those who invoke "culture" against rights are usually the powerful. [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] and [[thinkers/nussbaum|Nussbaum]] have argued this at length.

**What good are rights without a state?** [[thinkers/arendt|Arendt]] observed that people who lost their citizenship between the world wars found that their human rights were unenforceable. The most basic right is the **right to have rights**, which means the right to belong to a political community.

**When rights conflict.** Free speech and privacy, liberty and security, one person's religion and another's equality. Rights do not come with instructions for settling their own conflicts.

**Who has them?** The circle has widened over time, from propertied men to all adults. The same arguments are now made for animals, for future generations and, in some legal systems, for rivers and ecosystems.
