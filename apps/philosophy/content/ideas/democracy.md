# Democracy

Democracy means rule by the people. For most of the history of political thought it was a term of abuse, and it became the standard of legitimate government only in the last two centuries. The main arguments are about why the people should rule, how they can, and what protects minorities when they do.

## Ancient and modern

The word is Greek: *demos* (people) and *kratos* (power). In Athens in the fifth and fourth centuries BC, citizens met in an assembly to vote on laws and on war and peace, and most offices were filled by lot, which the Greeks thought more democratic than election. Citizens were a minority. Women, slaves and resident foreigners were excluded.

Modern democracy is a different arrangement. It is **representative**: citizens elect those who govern. It covers millions of people, not thousands. And it is usually **liberal** or constitutional: majorities are limited by rights, courts and a written constitution.

## The case against

The philosophers of Athens were democracy's first critics.

- [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] argued that ruling is a skill, like navigation or medicine, and that the many do not have it. Democracy flatters appetite and ends in tyranny.
- [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] classed democracy as a corrupt form, rule by the poor in their own interest, though he granted that a crowd may judge better than any one expert, as a feast to which many contribute is better than one man's dinner.
- [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] distinguished a "republic," with representation, from a "pure democracy," which he thought prone to turbulence and to majority faction.
- [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] and [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] accepted democracy and feared the **tyranny of the majority**, over opinion as much as through law.

## The case for

Arguments for democracy are of two kinds.

**It is right in itself.** If people are free and equal, no one has a natural title to rule anyone else, so each must have an equal say. For [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]], only those who make the law they obey are free. Democracy expresses equal respect.

**It works better.** Rulers who can be removed must attend to the governed. [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] argued that people's interests are only safe when they can defend them, and that taking part in public life educates citizens. [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] found that famines do not occur in functioning democracies. The philosopher Karl Popper put the case at its simplest: democracy is the system in which governments can be changed without bloodshed.

## Models of democracy

| Model | Democracy is | Associated with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct or participatory | Citizens deciding for themselves, and being formed by doing so | [[thinkers/rousseau\|Rousseau]], Carole Pateman |
| Minimal or competitive | A method for choosing leaders, by competition for votes | Joseph Schumpeter |
| Pluralist | Bargaining among many organised groups, none always winning | Robert Dahl |
| Deliberative | Public reasoning among equals, in which arguments count | [[thinkers/habermas\|Habermas]], [[thinkers/rawls\|Rawls]] |
| Agonistic | An unending contest between opponents that cannot be argued away | Chantal Mouffe |

The economist Joseph Schumpeter argued in 1942 that the "classical" picture, in which the people have a will and representatives carry it out, is a myth. There is no common good that all can agree on, and voters know little. Real democracy is a competition among elites for the people's vote. Much political science still works with this model.

**Deliberative** democrats reply that this leaves out what gives democratic decisions their authority. Votes are not just counted. They follow public argument in which citizens give each other reasons. Experiments with citizens' assemblies chosen by lot, an Athenian device, come from this school.

## Standing problems

**Representation.** Should a representative do what voters want or use their own judgement? [[thinkers/burke|Burke]] gave the classic answer for judgement. [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] held that the will cannot be represented at all.

**Majorities and rights.** A majority vote can violate the rights of a minority. Constitutions restrain majorities with entrenched rights and judicial review. Critics such as Jeremy Waldron ask why a few unelected judges should have the last word on questions about which citizens reasonably disagree.

**The will of the people.** Kenneth Arrow proved in 1951 that no method of combining individual preferences meets all of a short list of reasonable conditions. Different fair-seeming voting rules can give different winners from the same votes. This makes it hard to say that an election reveals what "the people" want. See [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] for work that softened the result.

**Competence.** Voters are often badly informed, as [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] complained. Some writers have revived the case for giving more weight to those who know more. Defenders of democracy reply with evidence that diverse groups can decide better than experts, and with the question of who would choose the knowers.

**The boundary problem.** Democracy says the people should decide. It cannot say who the people are, since that would need a prior vote.

**Democracy and liberalism.** [[thinkers/schmitt|Schmitt]] argued that the two are at odds. In recent years the phrase "illiberal democracy" has been used for governments that win elections and then weaken courts, the press and the opposition. Whether such a system remains a democracy is disputed, since fair elections depend on those very freedoms.
