# About this wiki

This page explains what the wiki covers, how its thinkers were chosen, how the facts were checked and what is missing. Read it before relying on anything here for more than a first orientation.

## What it is

An introduction to political philosophy for readers new to the subject, organised around thirty-five thinkers. It also covers the moral philosophy that political arguments rest on. It is a guide to the books, not a replacement for them.

The pages were drafted by an AI model, Claude, from its knowledge of the standard literature, and have not yet been reviewed by a specialist. They aim to summarise mainstream scholarly views. Where scholars disagree about how to read a thinker, the page says so.

## How the thinkers were chosen

The list follows the canon as it is usually taught in university courses on the history of political thought in the English-speaking world, from Plato to the present. A thinker has a page if they meet most of these tests.

- They wrote a work that later political thinkers had to answer.
- They stand for a position or tradition that needs a representative.
- They are regularly assigned in introductory courses.
- For the twentieth century, their influence has lasted beyond their own generation.

Any such list could be different. Some choices were close. Spinoza, Paine, Constant, Du Bois, Gramsci, Popper and several living philosophers could each have had a page and may get one.

## What is missing

**Most of the world.** This is the Western canon. Chinese political thought (Confucius, Mencius, Han Feizi, Mozi), the Indian tradition (Kautilya's *Arthashastra*, Ashoka, Gandhi, Ambedkar), Islamic political philosophy (al-Farabi, Ibn Rushd, Ibn Khaldun) and African political thought are not covered. That reflects the limits of the standard curriculum, not a judgement of their worth.

**Most women and most non-white thinkers** before the twentieth century. The canon was formed when few of them could publish or be read. Four of the thirty-five pages are on women, and only [[thinkers/fanon|Fanon]] and [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] wrote from the colonised or formerly colonised world. The [[reading-list|reading list]] has a path called "Who was left out," and the pages on [[traditions/feminism|feminism]] and [[ideas/social-contract|the social contract]] discuss the exclusions directly.

**Whole subjects.** There are no pages yet on nationalism, fascism, religion and politics, environmental political thought, multiculturalism, global justice, or the political philosophy of technology.

**The medieval period.** The chart on the [[timeline]] jumps eight centuries from Augustine to Aquinas.

## Including is not endorsing

Several thinkers on this wiki defended slavery, the subordination of women, empire or dictatorship. [[thinkers/schmitt|Carl Schmitt]] was a member of the Nazi Party. They are here because their arguments shaped the subject and still have to be answered. Each page states the view first in its own terms, as fairly as possible, and then gives the main criticisms and the relevant facts about the author's conduct.

On questions that remain disputed, such as how much the state should redistribute or what freedom is, the wiki tries to give each side its strongest case and does not take one.

## How far to trust the facts

- **Dates, titles and summaries** come from the model's general knowledge of the subject, which is drawn from the standard literature. They were reviewed for consistency across pages, and the automated tests check that the dates on each thinker page match the timeline. They were not checked one by one against printed sources.
- **Quotations** are given as they appear in the standard English translations or original English texts, from memory of those texts. Wording varies between translations, so a quotation here may differ from the edition in front of you. Check the original before citing one.
- **Recent facts were checked on the web in November 2026.** Jürgen Habermas died on 14 March 2026 and Alasdair MacIntyre in May 2025. [[thinkers/sen|Amartya Sen]] and [[thinkers/nussbaum|Martha Nussbaum]] were living when their pages were written.
- For anything that matters, confirm it in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* or in the text itself. The [[reading-list|reading list]] says where to find both.

## Known simplifications

Every summary simplifies. These are the ones most likely to mislead.

- **Dates for ancient authors are approximate.** Plato's dates are given as c. 428–348 BC. Sources vary by a year either way. The dates of his dialogues are scholarly estimates.
- **"Thesis, antithesis, synthesis"** is not [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel's]] own formula, and the page on him compresses a notoriously difficult writer.
- **[[thinkers/nietzsche|Nietzsche's]] politics** are contested. The page reports the main readings without choosing.
- **The "waves" of feminism** and the "generations" of rights are teaching devices that scholars increasingly question.
- **The traditions** are tidier on these pages than in history. Liberalism, conservatism and socialism each contain rival schools, and the labels mean different things in different countries.
- **The tables that compare thinkers** flatten real subtleties in order to make contrasts visible. The thinker pages are more careful than the tables.
