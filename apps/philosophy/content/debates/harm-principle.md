# The harm principle

The harm principle says that the only good reason to restrict an adult's liberty is to prevent harm to other people. John Stuart Mill stated it in 1859, and it has been at the centre of arguments about drugs, sex, speech and the limits of law ever since.

## Mill's claim

In *On Liberty*, [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] wrote that power may rightfully be exercised over a member of a civilised community against their will only "to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant."

The principle rules out two kinds of law.

- **Paternalism**: restricting people for their own good.
- **Legal moralism**: restricting conduct because it is thought immoral, though it harms no one else.

It also does not say that harm to others is always enough to justify a law. It says only that without such harm there is no case at all. And Mill did not think people should be indifferent to each other's choices. We may advise, argue, warn and avoid. We may not compel.

His reasons were broadly [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarian]]. Each person is the best judge of their own interests. Society's interventions in private conduct are usually wrong. And a people that is allowed to make its own mistakes grows in character, while one that is managed stays childish.

## First round: Stephen

The judge **James Fitzjames Stephen** replied in *Liberty, Equality, Fraternity* (1873). Mill's line between acts that affect only oneself and acts that affect others cannot be drawn, he argued, since no one is an island. And society is held together by a shared morality, which law and public opinion rightly enforce. Compulsion in matters of morals is no worse in principle than compulsion in matters of contract. Most people, in Stephen's view, are not improved by free discussion. They need to be governed.

## Second round: Hart and Devlin

The argument revived in England in 1957. A government committee chaired by Sir John Wolfenden recommended that homosexual acts between consenting adults in private should no longer be a crime, on the ground that there must remain "a realm of private morality and immorality which is, in brief and crude terms, not the law's business."

**Lord Devlin**, a senior judge, objected in a lecture later published as *The Enforcement of Morals*. A society is a community of ideas, including moral ideas. A shared morality is part of what holds it together, and society may defend it as it defends itself against treason. The test of what must be suppressed is the reaction of the ordinary person, "the man on the Clapham omnibus." If conduct arouses in him real intolerance, indignation and disgust, the law may forbid it.

The legal philosopher **H. L. A. Hart** answered in *Law, Liberty, and Morality* (1963), in the spirit of Mill.

- Devlin offers no evidence that societies fall apart when their sexual morality changes. Morality shifts over time without the society ceasing to exist.
- Popular disgust is not a reason. It has been directed at people of other races and religions.
- The misery caused by enforcing such laws is real and certain. The benefit is speculative.

Hart parted from Mill on one point. He accepted some **paternalism**, such as laws against selling dangerous drugs, since people do not always know their own interests best.

Parliament decriminalised homosexual acts in England and Wales in 1967. Later debates on the same lines have included abortion, pornography, assisted dying and drug policy.

## What counts as harm?

The principle is only as clear as the idea of harm. If harm means any bad effect, almost everything is harmful and the principle forbids nothing.

The American philosopher **Joel Feinberg** gave the most careful treatment in a four-volume work, *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law* (1984 to 1988).

- **Harm** is a wrongful setback to someone's interests. Mere dislike or disapproval is not harm.
- He added an **offence principle**. Seriously offensive conduct that people cannot reasonably avoid, such as indecency in public, may be restricted, though only by lighter penalties.
- He rejected hard paternalism but allowed **soft paternalism**: stopping someone whose choice is not truly voluntary, because they are ignorant, coerced or not in their right mind. Mill gave the example himself. One may stop a person about to cross an unsafe bridge long enough to warn them.

Other pressure points:

- **Consent.** Can a person consent to being harmed? Mill thought one could not validly sell oneself into slavery, since that would end the freedom the principle protects.
- **Harm by omission.** Does failing to rescue someone harm them? Mill thought there are duties to help.
- **Remote and collective harms.** No single car harms the climate. Many together do.
- **Harm to society.** Devlin's claim returns in arguments that drug use or family breakdown damages everyone.

## Speech

Mill gave speech stronger protection than action, limiting it only at the point of direct incitement. The modern debate over **hate speech** asks whether that is enough. Jeremy Waldron argues that hate speech harms its targets by attacking their standing as equal members of society. Catharine MacKinnon made a similar argument about pornography. Others, in the tradition of Mill and of American free speech law, hold that offence and insult are not harm, and that giving the state power to decide which opinions wound is the greater danger.

## Nudges

The economist Richard Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein popularised "libertarian paternalism" in their 2008 book *Nudge*. Institutions can steer people towards better choices, for instance by making saving for retirement the default, while leaving them free to choose otherwise. Supporters see a way to help without forcing. Critics see manipulation, and a paternalism that is harder to resist for being hard to notice.

## Why the principle lasts

Almost no legal system follows it strictly. Seat-belt laws are paternalist, and many laws rest partly on moral feeling. But it has shifted the burden of proof. Anyone who wants to forbid something is now expected to say who is harmed.
