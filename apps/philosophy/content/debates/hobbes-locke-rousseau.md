# Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau

Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau are the three classic social contract theorists. Each begins with people in a state of nature and asks what they would agree to, and each reaches a different kind of state: absolute, limited or directly democratic. Comparing them is the standard introduction to modern political thought.

## Why the answers differ

The method is the same and the results are far apart. The reason is that the [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]] is where each author puts his view of what human beings are like and what most threatens them.

**Hobbes** wrote in fear of civil war. He looked at people without a common power and saw distrust turning into violence. Almost any government is better than that, so people should accept almost any government, and must not weaken it by dividing it or setting conditions.

**Locke** wrote against a king who seemed bent on absolute rule. He looked at people without government and saw them getting by, owning things, keeping promises, but lacking an impartial judge. Government is a convenience, and so a ruler who becomes more dangerous than the inconveniences he was meant to cure has lost his title.

**Rousseau** wrote as a critic of a brilliant and unequal civilisation. He thought the other two had described people already spoiled by society. The real problem was not violence or insecurity but dependence: the rich on the labour of the poor, the poor on the favour of the rich, everyone on the opinion of others. Only a society in which each obeys laws that all have made can end it.

## Three arguments among them

### Is absolute power the cure or the disease?

Hobbes holds that a limited sovereign is a contradiction. Whoever judges the limits is the true sovereign, and if no one does there will be war.

Locke's reply is among the most quoted in political thought. To think that people would give a ruler absolute power to escape each other "is to think that men are so foolish that they take care to avoid what mischiefs may be done them by polecats or foxes, but are content, nay, think it safety, to be devoured by lions."

Hobbes's defenders answer that Locke assumes what Hobbes denies: that people can keep order well enough to hold a ruler to account without falling back into conflict.

### What is property?

For Hobbes there is no "mine" and "thine" until the sovereign makes rules. For Locke property exists before government, and protecting it is government's main purpose. For Rousseau that is exactly what is wrong with existing governments. They were set up by the rich to protect what they had seized. In a legitimate state, property is held under the community's rules and is kept moderate.

See [[ideas/property|property]].

### Can the people be represented?

Hobbes says that the sovereign represents the people, and that the people only exists as a unity through that representation. Locke says an elected legislature governs for them, on trust. Rousseau says that will cannot be represented. A people that hands lawmaking to deputies is free only on election day.

See [[ideas/sovereignty|sovereignty]] and [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].

## What they share

All three reject the idea that anyone is born with a right to rule. All three begin with individuals who are free and equal and treat political authority as something made by agreement, which therefore needs to be justified to those who live under it. That shared premise is what [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]], [[thinkers/burke|Burke]] and [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]] reject, and what [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] thought a fiction.

All three also assume that the parties to the contract are men who head households. See [[ideas/social-contract|the social contract]] for that criticism and for what [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] and [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] later made of the idea.

## A way to remember them

- For Hobbes, the worst thing is **disorder**.
- For Locke, the worst thing is **tyranny**.
- For Rousseau, the worst thing is **dependence**.

Most political disagreements can still be sorted by asking which of the three a person fears most.
