# Burke vs Paine

In 1790 Edmund Burke attacked the French Revolution, and within months Thomas Paine replied. Their exchange is the original argument between conservatism and radicalism: whether a people should respect what it has inherited or has the right to remake its government from first principles.

## The two men

[[thinkers/burke|Edmund Burke]] (1729–1797) was an Irish-born member of the British Parliament, a Whig who had supported the American colonists' complaints.

**Thomas Paine** (1737–1809) was an English corset-maker's son and dismissed tax officer who sailed to America in 1774. His pamphlet *Common Sense* (January 1776) made the case for independence in plain language and sold in huge numbers. He returned to Europe in 1787, was elected to the French National Convention, and was imprisoned during the Terror for opposing the king's execution. His later attack on organised religion, *The Age of Reason*, cost him most of his friends. He died poor in New York.

The two had been on friendly terms. Each had defended the Americans. France divided them.

## The exchange

- **November 1789.** The dissenting minister Richard Price preaches a sermon in London welcoming the French Revolution and claiming that the English Revolution of 1688 had established the people's right to choose their governors and to dismiss them for misconduct.
- **November 1790.** Burke answers with *Reflections on the Revolution in France*.
- **Late 1790.** [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Mary Wollstonecraft]] publishes *A Vindication of the Rights of Men*, among the first replies.
- **1791 and 1792.** Paine publishes *Rights of Man* in two parts. It far outsells Burke. Paine is charged with seditious libel and convicted in his absence, having left for France.

## The disagreement

| Question | Burke | Paine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where do rights come from? | Inheritance. The rights of Englishmen, handed down like an estate | Nature. The rights of man, the same for all and in every age |
| What is a constitution? | The slow growth of a people's laws and customs | A written act of the people, made before any government and above it |
| Authority of the past | The dead and the unborn are partners with the living | "The vanity and presumption of governing beyond the grave is the most ridiculous and insolent of all tyrannies" |
| Method of change | Repair, with reverence, preserving what works | Begin again from sound principles when the old is rotten |
| Guide to politics | Experience, prudence and "prejudice" | Reason, available to every person |
| Monarchy and aristocracy | Useful, tested institutions that give stability | Frauds. Hereditary rulers make as much sense as hereditary mathematicians |
| The French Revolution | A catastrophe that will end in tyranny | The dawn of a new age |

### Generations

The sharpest point of conflict is about time. Burke describes society as a partnership between the dead, the living and those yet to be born. No generation has the right to waste the inheritance.

Paine answers that every age must be free to act for itself. "It is the living, and not the dead, that are to be accommodated." The Parliament of 1688 had no more right to bind posterity for ever than a Parliament today has to decree that people shall live for ever. Jefferson agreed: "the earth belongs in usufruct to the living."

### Feeling

Burke's most famous passage mourns the humiliation of Marie Antoinette and the passing of "the age of chivalry." Paine's retort is that Burke weeps for a queen and has no tears for the prisoners of the Bastille. "He pities the plumage, but forgets the dying bird."

### What government is for

Paine's second part goes beyond politics to sketch something like a welfare state, paid for by taxing large estates: grants for poor families and for children's schooling, old-age pensions as a right and not a charity, and public works for the unemployed. His later *Agrarian Justice* proposed a payment to every person on reaching adulthood. Burke, in his own late writing, held that it is not within the competence of government to supply the poor with necessities.

## Who was right?

**On France, Burke.** Writing in 1790, when the Revolution still looked moderate, he predicted bloodshed and the rise of a popular general who would make himself master of the country. The king was executed and the Terror began in 1793, and Napoleon took power in 1799. Paine himself was nearly guillotined.

**On the long run, more often Paine.** Written constitutions, universal suffrage, the end of hereditary privilege, public education and old-age pensions are now common ground. Most were denounced in Burke's terms when first proposed.

**On the question itself, both.** Burke saw that institutions hold knowledge no one can fully state, and that tearing them down has costs that plans do not foresee. Paine saw that inherited arrangements favour those who inherited the most, and that "we have always done it this way" has been the argument for every abuse.

## The lasting pattern

The American writer Yuval Levin has argued that this debate is the origin of the modern right and left: a politics of gratitude for what exists against a politics of indignation at what is wrong with it. See [[traditions/conservatism|conservatism]] and [[traditions/liberalism|liberalism]].

The questions carry over directly. Should a constitution be read as its authors meant it, or by each generation for itself? How much should one generation bind the next with debt or with entrenched law? When is an institution a tested inheritance and when an old injustice?
