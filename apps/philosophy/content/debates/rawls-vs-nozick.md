# Rawls vs Nozick

John Rawls and Robert Nozick were colleagues at Harvard who published, three years apart, the two most influential books of modern political philosophy. Rawls argued that justice requires arranging society to benefit the worst off. Nozick argued that justice forbids taking from some to give to others. Their disagreement defines the modern argument between left and right liberalism.

## What they share

Both are liberals. Both reject [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarianism]] for the same reason: it would sacrifice some people for the greater good of others. Rawls says it "does not take seriously the distinction between persons." Nozick says there is no social entity whose good could justify the sacrifice, only "different individual people, with their own individual lives." Both claim [[thinkers/kant|Kant's]] principle that persons are ends and not merely means.

They draw opposite conclusions from it. For Rawls, treating people as ends means not letting their life chances be settled by luck. For Nozick, it means not using some people as resources for others, which is what redistribution does.

## Rawls's case

Nobody deserves the family they are born into or the abilities they are born with. These are "arbitrary from a moral point of view." Yet in an unregulated market they decide most of how a person's life goes.

To find fair terms, imagine choosing the rules of society without knowing who you will be. Rawls argues that you would secure equal basic liberties and fair opportunity, and would allow inequalities of income and wealth only if they improve the position of the worst off (the **difference principle**). Higher rewards for scarce skills are fine if they are needed to draw out effort that benefits everyone.

The picture is of society as "a cooperative venture for mutual advantage." What people produce, they produce within a system of cooperation, and the terms of that system must be ones all can accept.

## Nozick's objections

**Things come already owned.** Rawls writes as if the social product were a pie to be divided. But goods do not fall from heaven like manna. They are made by particular people, who have claims to them.

**Liberty upsets patterns.** The Wilt Chamberlain argument: begin with any distribution you think just. Let a million people each freely pay twenty-five cents to watch Chamberlain play. He ends with far more than anyone else. Each step was voluntary, so where is the injustice? To preserve any pattern, the state must keep interfering with what people choose to do with their fair shares.

**Self-ownership.** If I own myself, I own my labour and what I earn by it. To take part of my earnings is to make others part-owners of me.

**Why this group's viewpoint?** The difference principle looks at society from the position of the least advantaged. Nozick asks why the better endowed, whose cooperation is equally needed, should accept those terms.

**The original position is rigged.** People choosing behind a veil can only consider end-state principles, since they know nothing of anyone's history. A historical theory like his could not even be chosen there.

## The Rawlsian replies

**The basic structure.** Rawls's principles apply to the rules of the system: property law, taxation, inheritance, education. They do not apply to each transaction. Within fair rules, people may make whatever exchanges they like, and Chamberlain may keep what the rules leave him after tax. No one is constantly interfered with. They simply know in advance that they are playing under rules which include taxes.

**Accumulation.** Individually harmless transactions add up. Over time they concentrate wealth and power so that the next generation starts from grossly unequal positions and fair opportunity disappears. Background justice has to be maintained.

**Property is not natural.** Nozick assumes strong property rights that exist before any social arrangement. But what counts as property, how it is acquired and what may be done with it are fixed by law. One cannot appeal to property rights to settle what the property rules should be. Thomas Nagel called Nozick's view "libertarianism without foundations."

**Acquisition and history.** Nozick's theory needs holdings to trace back to just original acquisitions. His account of that first step is thin, and real history is full of conquest and theft. Nozick concedes that rectifying past injustice might justify extensive redistribution in the short run.

**Liberty for whom?** G. A. Cohen argued, from the left, that Nozick's minimal state protects the freedom of owners and restricts the freedom of everyone else, who may not use what others own.

## Criticism of both

- From the left, **G. A. Cohen** held that Rawls concedes too much. If the talented demand extra pay before they will use their gifts for the common good, they are not acting on the principle of justice themselves.
- [[traditions/communitarianism|Communitarians]] objected that both treat society as a scheme among separate individuals and ignore shared ends.
- [[traditions/feminism|Feminists]] noted that both take the family for granted.
- [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] agreed with Nozick's conclusions but for different reasons, and thought the search for "social justice" confused from the start.
- [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] and [[thinkers/nussbaum|Nussbaum]] argued that the debate looks at the wrong thing. What matters is neither primary goods nor property titles but what people are able to do.

## Why it still matters

Arguments over taxes, inheritance, health care and the welfare state usually reduce to this pair. One side asks whether the system as a whole is fair to those who do worst under it. The other asks by what right anyone takes what a person has honestly earned. Each question has force, and no later theory has made either go away.
