# Markets vs planning

Can an economy be run by a central plan, or does it need markets and prices? The "socialist calculation debate" of the 1920s to 1940s was an argument among economists that turned into one of the central questions of twentieth-century political thought, and it changed what both sides believed.

## Before the debate

[[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]] had argued in 1776 that free exchange coordinates production without anyone directing it. [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] replied that the market's "anarchy of production" brings crises, waste and exploitation, and that a society of associated producers would plan production consciously for human need. He left few details.

By 1920 the question was practical. The Bolsheviks held power in Russia and were trying to abolish markets. The Austrian philosopher Otto Neurath argued that an economy could be administered "in kind," without money, as Germany's had been during the war.

## Mises: calculation is impossible

In 1920 the Austrian economist **Ludwig von Mises** published "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth." His argument:

1. Under socialism the means of production (factories, land, machines) are owned in common, so they are not bought and sold.
2. What is not exchanged has no market price.
3. Without prices for the means of production, a planner cannot compare the cost of different ways of making something. Should a railway be built over the mountain or around it, with more steel or more labour? There is no common unit in which to reckon.
4. So rational economic calculation is impossible, and socialism would mean "groping in the dark."

## Lange: the planners can imitate the market

Socialist economists took the challenge seriously. The Polish economist **Oskar Lange**, in 1936, joked that a statue of Mises should stand in the planning ministry of the future socialist state, for having forced socialists to solve the problem.

His solution was **market socialism**. Consumers and workers would choose freely in real markets. For capital goods, the planning board would announce prices and instruct managers of state firms to minimise costs and produce up to the point where cost equals price. If a shortage or surplus appeared, the board would adjust the price, by trial and error, as a market does. It could reach the right prices, and without capitalism's inequality and slumps.

On paper, in the terms of the economic theory of the day, this seemed to work. Many economists concluded that Lange had won.

## Hayek: the knowledge problem

[[thinkers/hayek|Friedrich Hayek]], a student of Mises, shifted the ground. In essays from 1935 to 1945, most famously "The Use of Knowledge in Society," he argued that Lange had assumed the hard part away.

The models took the facts as given: what resources exist, what methods are available, what people want. But these facts are not given to anyone. They are scattered across millions of minds, they change every day, and much of this knowledge is of local circumstances that could never be reported to a ministry. Some of it is skill that its holders could not put into words.

A market is a way of using that dispersed knowledge. A price sums up what countless people know and signals to everyone else how to adjust, without anyone needing to know the reason. Competition is a **discovery procedure**. It finds out things that no one knew in advance, including which products and methods are worth trying. Managers told to follow rules, risking nothing of their own, would not discover what entrepreneurs discover.

Hayek added a political argument in *The Road to Serfdom* (1944). A central plan needs agreement on what to produce for whom. Free people do not agree, so the plan must be imposed, and economic control becomes control over all of life.

## What history showed

The Soviet Union industrialised fast and at terrible human cost, and beat Nazi Germany. For a time its growth alarmed the West. But its economy matched Mises's and Hayek's predictions closely: chronic shortages alongside unwanted goods, poor quality, little innovation outside the military, managers who hid capacity from planners, and a huge informal economy. Collectivised farming in the Soviet Union and China brought famines that killed millions.

From 1978 China turned to markets, and in 1989 to 1991 the Soviet bloc collapsed. Most economists, including many socialists, concluded that Hayek had been right about comprehensive central planning.

## What it did not settle

The debate was about one question: whether a whole economy can be planned from the centre. Its outcome leaves a good deal open.

- **Markets fail too.** Pollution, monopoly, financial crises and unemployment are problems the price system does not solve unaided. John Maynard Keynes argued that market economies can settle into long slumps and need governments to manage demand. He wrote to Hayek that he was in "deeply moved agreement" with *The Road to Serfdom* in moral terms, and added that the real question was where to draw the line, which Hayek had not said.
- **Mixed economies did not become tyrannies.** After 1945 the democracies combined markets with public ownership of some industries, regulation and welfare states. Hayek's road did not lead where he feared.
- **Markets need rules.** The Hungarian thinker Karl Polanyi argued in *The Great Transformation* (1944) that free markets were themselves created by state action, and that societies always act to protect themselves from them.
- **Ownership is a separate question.** Hayek's argument is for prices and competition. It does not show that firms must be owned by capitalists. Market socialists such as Alec Nove and John Roemer have proposed worker-owned or publicly owned firms that compete.
- **Planning is everywhere.** A large company is a planned economy inside. The economist Ronald Coase asked in 1937 why firms exist at all if markets are so efficient. His answer was that using the market has costs too.
- **Computing.** Some argue that modern data and processing power weaken the knowledge argument. Others reply that Hayek's point was never about arithmetic. It was about knowledge that does not exist until people act on it.
- **Efficiency is not justice.** [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] and [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] accept markets as tools and deny that the incomes they produce are thereby fair.
