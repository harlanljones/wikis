# Plato vs Aristotle

Plato and his student Aristotle agreed that politics should aim at the good life, and disagreed about almost everything else: whether to reason from an ideal or from experience, whether experts should rule, and how much unity a city can bear. Their disagreement set a pattern that later political thought keeps repeating.

Raphael's fresco *The School of Athens* shows the two at its centre. [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] points up, towards the Forms. [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] holds his hand out flat, towards the world.

## The disagreement in brief

| Question | Plato (in the *Republic*) | Aristotle (in the *Politics*) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Method | Design the best city in thought, then measure real ones against it | Collect and compare real constitutions, then ask what works |
| Political knowledge | Like mathematics: exact, and held by a few | Practical wisdom: inexact, learned by experience |
| Who should rule | Philosophers, trained for decades | Citizens in turn, in a mixed constitution with a strong middle class |
| Unity | The more a city is one, the better | A city is by nature a plurality. Too much unity destroys it |
| Property and family | Abolished for the guardians | Private ownership, generous use. Keep the household |
| Democracy | The second-worst regime, a step from tyranny | A defective form, but the many together can judge well |
| The best regime | One model, the rule of the wise | Depends on the people and circumstances |

## Four arguments

### Ideal or actual?

[[thinkers/plato|Plato]] has Socrates admit that the city of the *Republic* may exist nowhere on earth. It is "a pattern laid up in heaven" for anyone who wishes to see it and to order themselves by it. Its value is as a standard.

[[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] thinks this leaves out most of what a student of politics needs. One must know the best constitution in the abstract, and also the best for a given people, how any constitution may be preserved, and which form suits most cities. A trainer who knew only the regimen for a perfect athlete would be of little use.

### Is ruling a science?

Plato's case for philosopher-rulers rests on an analogy with crafts. We do not vote on how to steer a ship or cure a patient. We ask someone who knows.

Aristotle doubts the analogy in two ways. Ethics and politics do not admit of mathematical precision, and it is the mark of an educated person to look for only as much exactness as a subject allows. And the user of a thing may judge it better than the maker: "the guest will judge better of a feast than the cook." A crowd, each contributing a share of sense and virtue, may collectively judge better than a single expert.

### How much unity?

Plato wants citizens to say "mine" and "not mine" of the same things, so that the city feels pleasure and pain as one body. Hence the guardians share property, wives and children.

Aristotle replies in Book II of the *Politics*. A city is made of different kinds of people, and to unify it completely would turn it into a household and then into a single person. And shared property and families would not work. "That which is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it." A man with a thousand "sons" will have none in particular, and love will be watered down, like a little sweet wine mixed in a great deal of water.

### One good or many?

For Plato, there is a Form of the Good, and those who grasp it know what is good for everyone. Aristotle says "good" is used in many ways and that no single Form explains them. This is the root of his greater tolerance of variety, in lives and in constitutions.

## What they share

The contrast can be overdrawn.

- Both hold that the city exists for the sake of **living well**, not just for security or wealth, and that it should form the character of its citizens. Modern [[traditions/liberalism|liberals]] part from both on this.
- Both accept natural hierarchy. Aristotle defends slavery and the subordination of women. Plato's city is a rigid order of classes.
- Both rank the life of the mind highest.
- The later Plato, in the *Laws*, moves towards Aristotle: a mixed constitution, private property within limits and the rule of law. Aristotle spent twenty years in Plato's Academy, and much of the *Politics* develops themes from the *Laws*.

## The pattern repeats

Later thinkers line up on either side.

| The Platonic temper | The Aristotelian temper |
| --- | --- |
| Start from the ideal and ask what justice requires | Start from practice and ask what can be made to work |
| [[thinkers/rousseau\|Rousseau]], [[thinkers/kant\|Kant]], [[thinkers/rawls\|Rawls]] in his method | [[thinkers/montesquieu\|Montesquieu]], [[thinkers/burke\|Burke]], [[thinkers/tocqueville\|Tocqueville]], [[thinkers/sen\|Sen]] |

[[thinkers/sen|Sen's]] criticism of Rawls, that we need to compare real alternatives and not describe perfect institutions, is Aristotle's criticism of Plato in modern dress. So is the [[traditions/realism|realist]] complaint about "ideal theory."

Karl Popper cast the argument as one between the closed society and the open one, with Plato as the first enemy of freedom. And the dispute over whether experts or citizens should decide returns in every argument about [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].
