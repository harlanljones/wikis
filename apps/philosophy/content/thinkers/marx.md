# Karl Marx

Karl Marx was the German philosopher, economist and revolutionary who argued that the history of every society is driven by the way it produces what it needs and by the conflict of classes that results. He held that capitalism, for all its power, rests on the exploitation of workers and would be replaced by a society without classes.

## Life

Marx was born in 1818 in Trier, in the Prussian Rhineland, to a family of Jewish descent that had converted to Protestantism so his father could keep practising law. He studied in Bonn and Berlin, where he joined the Young Hegelians, and wrote a doctoral thesis on ancient Greek philosophy. With an academic career closed to radicals, he became a journalist. Prussia shut down his newspaper, and he moved to Paris in 1843. There he met Friedrich Engels, the son of a textile manufacturer, who became his collaborator and financial support for forty years.

Expelled from Paris, then Brussels, then Germany after the failed revolutions of 1848, he settled in London in 1849 and lived there, often in poverty, for the rest of his life. He spent his days in the British Museum reading room working on *Capital*. He helped lead the International Working Men's Association from 1864. He died in 1883 and is buried in Highgate Cemetery. Only the first volume of *Capital* appeared in his lifetime. Engels edited the second and third.

## The main ideas

### Alienation

In manuscripts written in Paris in 1844, unpublished until 1932, the young Marx describes what wage labour does to the worker. Human beings are creative, social producers by nature. Under capitalism workers are **alienated**, or estranged, in four ways: from the product, which belongs to someone else; from the activity of work, which is a mere means to survive; from their own human nature; and from one another. The worker "only feels himself outside his work, and in his work feels outside himself."

### Historical materialism

Marx's theory of history starts from the fact that people must produce in order to live. Each society has **forces of production** (tools, skills, technology) and **relations of production** (who owns what and who works for whom). Together these form an economic base, on which rises a "legal and political superstructure" with matching forms of thought. "It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but, on the contrary, their social being that determines their consciousness."

When the forces of production outgrow the relations that contain them, an era of revolution begins. So slavery gave way to feudalism and feudalism to capitalism. The engine is conflict between those who own and those who labour:

> The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles.

The dominant ideas of an age are the ideas of its ruling class. **Ideology** presents a particular class's interests as the natural and general interest.

### Capitalism and exploitation

The *Manifesto* contains a remarkable tribute to capitalism, which in a century created "more massive and more colossal productive forces than have all preceding generations together." *Capital* sets out to explain how it works.

Workers own nothing but their capacity to work, so they must sell it. The employer pays a wage that covers the worker's upkeep, but the worker produces more value in a day than that wage. The difference is **surplus value**, the source of profit. Nothing is stolen in the market, where equals trade freely. The exploitation happens in "the hidden abode of production." Competition forces capitalists to accumulate, replace workers with machines and drive down costs, which produces recurring crises and a growing, organised working class. The system creates "its own grave-diggers."

### State and rights

The modern state, according to the *Manifesto*, is "but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie." In "On the Jewish Question" (1844) Marx argues that the rights of man proclaimed by the French and American revolutions are the rights of "egoistic man," separated from other people and from the community. Political emancipation is real progress, but it leaves the inequalities of civil society untouched. Human emancipation requires changing those too. See [[ideas/rights|rights]] and [[ideas/equality|equality]].

### Communism

Marx wrote little about the future, on principle. He refused to write "recipes for the cook-shops of the future." A few points are clear. The working class must win political power and bring the means of production under common ownership. In his notes on the Gotha Programme (1875) he separates a first phase, in which people are still rewarded according to their work, from a higher phase in which society can write on its banners: "From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!" Classes having gone, the state as an instrument of class rule would lose its function.

## Criticism and influence

The revolutions Marx expected in the advanced industrial countries did not come. Wages rose, the vote spread and the welfare state softened the conflicts he described. The regimes that later ruled in his name, in Russia, China and elsewhere, were dictatorships responsible for mass death. Whether they followed from his ideas or betrayed them is fiercely argued. Critics such as [[thinkers/berlin|Berlin]] and Popper see the danger in his claim to know the direction of history. [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] and Ludwig von Mises argued that central planning cannot work at all. See [[debates/markets-vs-planning|markets vs planning]]. Economists have largely abandoned the labour theory of value.

His influence on the study of society is another matter. The ideas that economic life shapes politics and culture, that formal equality can coexist with real domination, and that ideas serve interests are now common property. [[thinkers/weber|Weber]] wrote in dialogue with him. The Frankfurt School, [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]], [[thinkers/fanon|Fanon]], [[thinkers/beauvoir|Beauvoir]] and [[thinkers/foucault|Foucault]] all worked out their positions in relation to his.

## Where to start

*The Communist Manifesto*, parts 1 and 2. Then the "Estranged Labour" section of the 1844 manuscripts, the "Theses on Feuerbach" (two pages, ending with the line that philosophers have only interpreted the world and the point is to change it), and the 1859 preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*.
