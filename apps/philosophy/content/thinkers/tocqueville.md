# Alexis de Tocqueville

Alexis de Tocqueville was the French aristocrat who travelled to the United States to see what a democratic society looked like and came back with the most searching book ever written about one. He believed the spread of equality could not be stopped, and that the question was whether it would lead to freedom or to a new kind of despotism.

## Life

Tocqueville was born in Paris in 1805 into an old Norman noble family. His parents had been imprisoned during the Terror and narrowly escaped the guillotine, and his great-grandfather, who had defended Louis XVI at his trial, did not. He grew up knowing that the world of his class was finished.

In 1831, aged twenty-five, he and his friend Gustave de Beaumont went to the United States, officially to study its prisons. They travelled for nine months, from New York to the frontier in Michigan and down the Mississippi to New Orleans. The two volumes of *Democracy in America* made him famous. He sat in the French parliament, warned of revolution weeks before it came in 1848, helped write the constitution of the Second Republic, and was briefly foreign minister. He left politics after Louis-Napoleon's coup in 1851 and died of tuberculosis in 1859.

## The main ideas

### Equality of conditions

By "democracy" Tocqueville means more than a form of government. He means a kind of society, one without fixed ranks, where people see each other as basically alike. He saw the gradual levelling of conditions as the great movement of the last seven hundred years of European history, "a providential fact." America showed the result in its purest form because it had never had an aristocracy to overthrow.

He was neither for it nor against it. Democratic society, he thought, is less brilliant than aristocracy and more just.

### The tyranny of the majority

In America the majority rules without rival, and its power is not only legal. It is moral. Tocqueville wrote that he knew of no country with "less independence of mind and true freedom of discussion." No one is burned for an opinion. The dissenter is simply left alone, shut out and made to feel it. The old despots struck the body. Democratic majorities go straight for the soul. [[thinkers/mill|John Stuart Mill]], who reviewed the book, built *On Liberty* around this danger.

### Individualism and soft despotism

Aristocracy linked everyone in a chain from peasant to king. Democracy breaks the chain. Each person, equal and independent, tends to withdraw into a small circle of family and friends and leave the wider society to look after itself. Tocqueville gave this new condition a new name: **individualism**.

Isolated citizens absorbed in private comfort are easy to rule. Near the end of the second volume he imagines the despotism democratic nations have to fear. It would not be cruel. Above a crowd of similar and equal people, each a stranger to the fate of the rest, would stand "an immense tutelary power" that provides for their security and pleasures, manages their affairs, and would gladly spare them "the trouble of thinking and the pain of living." It does not break wills. It softens and guides them, until the nation is "a flock of timid and industrious animals, of which the government is the shepherd."

### What keeps Americans free

Tocqueville found the remedies in American practice.

- **Local self-government.** Town meetings are to liberty what primary schools are to knowledge. They teach people to use it.
- **Associations.** Americans "of all ages, all conditions, and all minds" constantly form associations, and in doing so learn to act together without waiting for the state.
- **Self-interest well understood.** Americans explain their public spirit by enlightened self-interest. It does not produce heroes, but it produces a great many orderly, moderate citizens.
- **Religion.** Separated from the state, the churches moderate the restless materialism of a commercial people.
- A free press, juries and an independent legal profession.

The modern study of **civil society** and "social capital" begins here.

### The old regime

*The Old Regime and the Revolution* argues that the French Revolution did not break with the past as much as it claimed. The kings had already destroyed local liberties and gathered power in Paris. The Revolution completed their work. It also offers a much-cited paradox: revolutions break out not when conditions are worst but when they have begun to improve and expectations rise.

## Criticism and influence

Tocqueville saw American slavery clearly and predicted that it, and the treatment of Native Americans, would be the country's lasting wound. Yet in his own country's affairs he was an advocate of the French conquest and colonisation of Algeria, and he defended harsh methods there. His liberalism stopped at Europe's edge.

His reading of America also has gaps. He underestimated economic inequality in the North and said little about the party system.

His fears of conformity, withdrawal into private life and paternalist government remain live questions, and he is quoted by left and right alike. [[thinkers/arendt|Arendt]] and the [[traditions/communitarianism|communitarians]] drew on his account of associations.

## Where to start

*Democracy in America*, volume 1, the author's introduction and Part 2, chapter 7 (the omnipotence of the majority). Volume 2, Part 2, chapters 2 to 8 (individualism and associations) and Part 4, chapter 6 (soft despotism).
