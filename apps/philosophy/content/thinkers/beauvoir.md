# Simone de Beauvoir

Simone de Beauvoir was the French existentialist philosopher and novelist whose book *The Second Sex* asked what a woman is and answered that femininity is not a fact of nature but a situation imposed by society. It is the founding work of modern feminist theory.

## Life

Beauvoir was born in Paris in 1908 into a Catholic middle-class family that had lost its money, which meant she would have to earn her living. In 1929, at twenty-one, she became the youngest person to pass the *agrégation* in philosophy, the fiercely competitive national teaching examination, placing second to Jean-Paul Sartre. The two formed a lifelong partnership. They never married or lived together, and each had other relationships by agreement.

She taught in schools until 1943, when she published her first novel. After the war she and Sartre founded the journal *Les Temps modernes* and became the public faces of existentialism. *The Second Sex* appeared in two volumes in 1949 and was placed on the Vatican's Index of banned books. Her novel *The Mandarins* won the Prix Goncourt in 1954. She wrote four volumes of memoirs, opposed the French war in Algeria, and in the 1970s joined the women's movement, signing the 1971 manifesto of 343 women who declared they had had illegal abortions. She died in 1986.

Her conduct towards some of her young female students in the 1930s and 1940s, whom she drew into relationships with herself and Sartre, has been strongly criticised.

## The main ideas

### Freedom and ambiguity

Existentialism holds that human beings have no fixed nature or given purpose. We are free, and we make ourselves by what we choose. Beauvoir's *The Ethics of Ambiguity* draws an ethics from this. To be human is to be both a free consciousness and a body in a world we did not choose, and it is tempting to flee that tension by pretending our values are simply given. Real freedom accepts responsibility. It also requires the freedom of others: "To will oneself free is also to will others free." Oppression is the attempt to cut people off from their own future.

Where Sartre at first treated freedom as absolute in any circumstances, Beauvoir stressed **situation**. A person's real possibilities depend on their material and social conditions. A woman shut in a harem, she wrote, is not free in the way her master is.

### Woman as the Other

*The Second Sex* begins with a question no man, she notes, would think to ask about himself: what is a woman? Her answer is that humanity is taken to be male, and woman is defined in relation to him. "He is the Subject, he is the Absolute. She is the Other."

The idea comes from [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel's]] account of master and slave. Every group sets itself up as the One against others. But where other oppressed groups can answer back as a "we," women have lived scattered among men, tied to them by family, class and affection more closely than to each other.

Volume 1 examines the explanations offered by biology, psychoanalysis and Marxism, and finds each incomplete. The body is real, and it matters. But biology is not destiny. Facts take their meaning from the society that interprets them.

### Becoming a woman

Volume 2 opens with her most famous sentence: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." It follows a girl through childhood, adolescence, sexual initiation, marriage, motherhood and old age, showing how at each stage she is taught to see herself as an object for others. A boy is urged outward into projects. A girl is taught to please.

Beauvoir describes the result with two terms. **Transcendence** is the reaching forward into freely chosen projects that marks a human life. **Immanence** is confinement to repetition and maintenance. Women have been assigned to immanence, most of all in housework, which she compares to the torture of Sisyphus.

She also examines the ways women cooperate with their condition. Dependence has its comforts, and the roles of the narcissist, the woman in love and the mystic are ways of making a vocation out of one's own confinement.

The way out is economic independence above all, along with control over childbearing and a transformation of how children are raised. She called for a world in which men and women recognise each other as equals and peers. This later distinction between biological **sex** and socially made **gender** grows from her work, though she did not use those terms.

## Criticism and influence

*The Second Sex* has been criticised for treating male activities as the measure of a full human life and for a cold view of the female body and of motherhood. Later feminists pointed out that its "woman" is mostly white, Western and middle class, and Black feminists in particular objected to its analogies between women and other oppressed groups. Judith Butler pushed its central sentence further, asking whether "sex" itself is as natural as Beauvoir assumed.

The first English translation, from 1953, cut a good part of the text and blurred its philosophical terms. A complete translation appeared only in 2009.

The book gave the women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s its theoretical basis, and it stands in a line from [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Wollstonecraft]] and [[thinkers/mill|Mill]]. See [[traditions/feminism|feminism]].

## Where to start

*The Second Sex*: the introduction, then the opening of volume 2 ("Childhood") and the conclusion. *The Ethics of Ambiguity* is short and is the best statement of her moral philosophy.
