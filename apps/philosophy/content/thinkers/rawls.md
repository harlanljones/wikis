# John Rawls

John Rawls was the American philosopher whose *A Theory of Justice* revived political philosophy in the English-speaking world. He asked what rules free and equal people would choose for their society if they did not know what place they would hold in it, and argued that they would choose equal liberties and an economy arranged to benefit the worst off.

## Life

Rawls was born in Baltimore in 1921. Two of his younger brothers died in childhood of infections they caught from him, which he later said gave him a sense of how much in life is unearned luck. He studied at Princeton and considered the priesthood. He then served as an infantryman in the Pacific in the Second World War, passed through the ruins of Hiroshima, and lost his religious faith.

He taught at Cornell and MIT and, from 1962, at Harvard. *A Theory of Justice* took him about twenty years and appeared in 1971. He was a private man who avoided publicity and gave few interviews, and spent the next thirty years revising and defending the theory. He died in 2002.

## The main ideas

### The question

"Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought." Rawls's subject is the **basic structure** of society: the constitution, the legal system, the economy and the family, which together shape each person's prospects from birth. People are born into different positions through no merit or fault of their own. What would make the basic structure fair?

The dominant answer in his day was [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarian]]: arrange things to produce the greatest total welfare. Rawls objected that this could justify sacrificing some for the gain of others. It treats society as if it were one big person and "does not take seriously the distinction between persons."

### The original position

Rawls revives the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] in a new form. Imagine people choosing the principles for their society in an **original position**, behind a **veil of ignorance**. They do not know their class, race, sex, talents, religion or idea of the good life. They know general facts about human society, and they want more "primary goods" (rights, opportunities, income, the social bases of self-respect) for themselves.

Since no one knows who they will be, no one can rig the rules in their own favour. The veil turns self-interested choice into fair choice. This is why Rawls calls his theory **justice as fairness**.

### Two principles

He argues that the parties would choose two principles.

1. **Equal liberty.** Each person has an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of basic liberties: freedom of conscience, speech and association, political rights, the rule of law.
2. Social and economic inequalities must meet two conditions.
   - **Fair equality of opportunity.** Positions must be open to all, with real and not merely formal chances for those with similar talents.
   - **The difference principle.** Inequalities must be to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society.

The first has priority over the second. Liberty may not be traded for economic gain.

The difference principle is the most original part. It does not demand equal shares. Higher pay for doctors or entrepreneurs is allowed if, and only if, the arrangement makes the worst off better off than they would be under equality. Why would people choose it? Because behind the veil, with one life at stake and no knowledge of the odds, it is rational to make sure the worst position is as good as possible.

A further argument runs under the contract. Natural talents and the family one is born into are "arbitrary from a moral point of view." Nobody deserves their starting place. So a just society treats the distribution of talents as, in a sense, a common asset, and lets the lucky gain only on terms that help the unlucky. See [[ideas/justice|justice]] and [[ideas/equality|equality]].

Rawls thought these principles ruled out both laissez-faire capitalism and a welfare state that merely tops up low incomes. They require either a "property-owning democracy," in which wealth and capital are widely spread, or a liberal form of socialism.

### Reflective equilibrium

How do we know the principles are right? Rawls's method is to move back and forth between general principles and our firm judgements about cases (that slavery is unjust, for instance), adjusting each until they fit. He calls the result **reflective equilibrium**.

### Political liberalism

In his later work Rawls faced a problem in his own theory. A free society will always contain many reasonable religious and philosophical outlooks. How can it be stable, and its coercive laws be legitimate, if citizens do not share one?

His answer is that the principles of justice should be **political**, not part of any complete philosophy of life. Citizens with different outlooks can each support them for their own reasons, forming an **overlapping consensus**. And when debating fundamental questions, citizens and officials should offer **public reasons**, ones that others could reasonably accept, and not appeal only to their own creed.

*The Law of Peoples* extends the theory to international relations, with a more modest set of principles that disappointed readers who had hoped for a global difference principle.

## Criticism and influence

- [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick]] argued that any pattern of distribution can only be kept up by constant interference with free choices. See [[debates/rawls-vs-nozick|Rawls vs Nozick]].
- [[traditions/communitarianism|Communitarians]] such as Michael Sandel objected that the parties behind the veil are selves stripped of everything that makes a person who they are.
- Feminists such as Susan Moller Okin argued that he said too little about justice within the family.
- G. A. Cohen argued from the left that the difference principle lets the talented demand incentives they have no right to.
- [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] argued that we need ways of comparing actual injustices, not a blueprint of a perfectly just society.
- Charles Mills argued that an ideal theory which leaves out race cannot guide a society built on racial injustice.

Nozick himself wrote that "political philosophers now must either work within Rawls's theory or explain why not." Half a century on, that is still roughly the case.

## Where to start

*A Theory of Justice*, sections 1 to 4, 11 to 17 and 24 to 26. Or the shorter and clearer *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001), parts I and II.
