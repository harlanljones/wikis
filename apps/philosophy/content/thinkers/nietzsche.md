# Friedrich Nietzsche

Friedrich Nietzsche was the German philosopher who asked where our moral values come from and what they are worth. He argued that the morality of equality, pity and humility, which Europe took for morality as such, began as the revenge of the weak upon the strong, and that with the "death of God" its foundations had gone.

## Life

Nietzsche was born in 1844 in Röcken, Saxony, the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when he was four. He was a brilliant student of classical languages, and at twenty-four he was appointed professor at the University of Basel before he had finished his doctorate. His first book, *The Birth of Tragedy*, shocked his colleagues. He was for some years a devoted friend of the composer Richard Wagner and then broke with him.

In 1879 poor health forced him to resign. For ten years he lived alone in boarding houses in the Swiss Alps and on the Italian and French coasts, writing a book a year that almost no one read. In January 1889 he collapsed in a street in Turin. He never recovered his mind and died in 1900.

His sister Elisabeth, a German nationalist married to an antisemitic agitator, took control of his papers. She edited his notes into a book called *The Will to Power* and promoted him as a prophet of the new Germany. The Nazis later claimed him. Nietzsche himself had despised antisemites and German nationalism, and said so often.

## The main ideas

### The death of God

In *The Gay Science* a madman runs into the marketplace crying that he seeks God. "God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him." The point is not an argument for atheism. Educated Europe has already stopped believing. What it has not understood is the consequence. Its morality, its faith in truth and its sense that life has a purpose all rested on that belief, and they cannot simply carry on without it. The danger is **nihilism**, the sense that nothing matters.

### Master and slave morality

*On the Genealogy of Morality* tells a story of where "good" and "evil" came from. The first values were coined by the strong: nobles and warriors who called themselves and their way of life **good**, meaning powerful, healthy and proud, and called the common people **bad**, meaning merely low. This is master morality.

The weak, unable to act on their hatred, took an imaginary revenge. In what Nietzsche calls the "slave revolt in morality," which he traces to ancient Judaism and to Christianity, they declared the strong to be **evil** and themselves, the meek and the suffering, to be **good**. The feeling behind this reversal is *ressentiment*, a festering resentment turned creative. Humility, pity, equality and selflessness are its products.

Nietzsche's claim is that modern secular morality, including democracy, socialism and utilitarianism, keeps Christian values while dropping the Christian God. The belief that all are equal and that suffering should be abolished is "herd" morality in a new coat.

### Will to power and perspectivism

Nietzsche suggests that what living things basically seek is not survival or pleasure but growth and the overcoming of resistance, which he calls **will to power**. It can take crude forms, as domination, or refined ones, as the self-mastery of the artist or the ascetic.

He also holds that there is no view from nowhere. "There is only a perspective seeing, only a perspective 'knowing'." Every claim to truth comes from some standpoint and serves some form of life. [[thinkers/foucault|Foucault]] took over this method and the name genealogy.

### The higher human being

Against the "last man," who wants only comfort and blinks, Nietzsche sets the *Übermensch* (overman), a figure who creates new values and says yes to life as it is. The test is the thought of **eternal recurrence**: could you will to live your life again, exactly the same, an infinite number of times?

## Nietzsche and politics

Nietzsche wrote no political theory, and his remarks on politics are scattered and often violent. He praised rank, distance and hardness, called democracy a form of decay, and dismissed the equality of persons as a lie. He called the state "the coldest of all cold monsters."

Readers divide over what to do with this.

- Some take the aristocratic statements at face value. On this reading he is the most radical enemy of [[ideas/equality|equality]] in the modern canon.
- Others hold that his concern is cultural and personal: the self-creation of exceptional individuals, not a programme for government.
- A third group, many on the left, separates his methods from his opinions and uses genealogy to expose how power hides in claims to morality and truth.

What is not in doubt is that he fits no party. He attacked nationalism, militarism and antisemitism as fiercely as he attacked socialism.

## Criticism and influence

The usual objection to the genealogy is that the origin of a value does not settle its worth. Even if pity began in resentment, it might still be good. Others point out that Nietzsche offers little to anyone who is not a potential genius.

His influence spread across the twentieth century: on [[thinkers/weber|Weber's]] picture of a disenchanted world, on existentialism, on [[thinkers/foucault|Foucault]], and on the moral philosophy of Bernard Williams and Alasdair MacIntyre, whose *After Virtue* frames the modern choice as "Nietzsche or [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]]".

## Where to start

*On the Genealogy of Morality*, first essay, is the clearest and most sustained argument. Then *Beyond Good and Evil*, parts 5 and 9, and *The Gay Science*, sections 125 and 341.
