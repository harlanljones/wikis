# Hannah Arendt

Hannah Arendt was the German-Jewish political thinker who set out to understand totalitarianism, the new form of rule that had made her a refugee. She went on to defend politics itself, as the activity in which free and different people act together in public, against everything that would reduce it to administration or violence.

## Life

Arendt was born in 1906 near Hanover and grew up in Königsberg, [[thinkers/kant|Kant's]] city. She studied philosophy with Martin Heidegger, with whom she had an affair, and with Karl Jaspers, under whom she wrote a thesis on the concept of love in [[thinkers/augustine|Augustine]]. In 1933, after being arrested and briefly held by the Gestapo for collecting evidence of antisemitic propaganda, she fled to Paris, where she worked for organisations sending Jewish children to Palestine.

When Germany invaded France in 1940 she was interned as an "enemy alien" in the camp at Gurs, escaped, and reached New York in 1941. She was stateless for eighteen years until she became an American citizen in 1951. She wrote, taught at Chicago and at the New School in New York, and died in 1975 with the first page of the last part of *The Life of the Mind* in her typewriter.

She refused the title "philosopher". Philosophy, she said, is about man in the singular. Her subject was politics, which exists because "men, not Man, live on the earth and inhabit the world."

## The main ideas

### Totalitarianism

*The Origins of Totalitarianism* argues that Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union were a new kind of regime, different from the tyrannies and dictatorships known before. Ordinary tyranny wants obedience. Totalitarian rule wants to remake human nature, by an ideology that explains all of history and by terror that strikes the innocent as readily as the guilty. Its typical institution is the concentration camp, where human beings are made superfluous.

It grew, on her account, from the breakdown of the nineteenth-century order: antisemitism, imperialism, and the appearance of masses of isolated, uprooted people who belonged nowhere. Loneliness is its soil.

Her chapter on refugees contains one of her most lasting ideas. People who lost their citizenship between the wars found that their "human rights" protected them nowhere. Rights turn out to depend on membership in a political community. The most basic right is therefore **the right to have rights**. See [[ideas/rights|rights]].

### Labour, work and action

*The Human Condition* separates three kinds of activity.

| Activity | What it is | What it produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Labour | Meeting the needs of the body, over and over | Things consumed as soon as made |
| Work | Making a lasting world of objects and buildings | Things that outlive their makers |
| Action | Speaking and acting among others, starting something new | Stories, institutions, a shared public world |

**Action** is the properly political activity. It depends on **plurality**, the fact that people are equal and yet each unique, and on **natality**, the capacity of each newcomer to begin something. Its outcomes are unpredictable and cannot be undone, which is why promising and forgiving are political virtues.

Arendt thought the modern age had inverted the old ranking. It treats politics as housekeeping on a national scale, a matter of managing production and consumption. She called this "the rise of the social" and regretted it. Her model of real politics was the Greek city and the moments when ordinary people create public freedom together: the American founding, the workers' and citizens' councils that spring up in revolutions.

### Power and violence

Against [[thinkers/weber|Weber]] and almost everyone else, Arendt denied that power is a matter of command backed by force. **Power** "corresponds to the human ability not just to act but to act in concert." It belongs to a group and lasts only while the group holds together. **Violence** is a tool, and it can destroy power but never create it. Rule by pure violence comes into play where power has been lost. See [[ideas/power|power]].

### The banality of evil

In 1961 Arendt reported for *The New Yorker* on the trial in Jerusalem of Adolf Eichmann, who had organised the transport of Jews to the death camps. She expected a monster. She found, by her account, a mediocre official who spoke in clichés and seemed unable to think from anyone else's point of view. Her phrase for this was "the banality of evil." Great crimes do not require demonic motives. They can be committed by people who simply do not think about what they are doing.

The book caused a storm, both for this thesis and for her harsh remarks about the Jewish councils that had been forced to cooperate with the Nazis. Later historians have shown that Eichmann was a far more committed antisemite than he appeared in court. The wider point about thoughtlessness and obedience has outlived the dispute over the man.

## Criticism and influence

Arendt's sharp line between the "political" and the "social" seems to push poverty, work and welfare out of politics. In "Reflections on Little Rock" (1959) she applied that line to oppose federally enforced school desegregation, a position widely judged a serious misreading of American racism. Her idealised Greek city rested on slaves and excluded women.

Her account of public freedom shaped the thinking of dissidents in Eastern Europe before 1989 and of theorists of civil society and deliberative democracy, including [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]]. *The Origins of Totalitarianism* returns to bestseller lists whenever democracies feel insecure.

## Where to start

*Eichmann in Jerusalem* is the most readable. In *The Origins of Totalitarianism*, read the chapter "The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man." In *The Human Condition*, read the prologue and chapter 5, on action. Then the essay *On Violence*.
