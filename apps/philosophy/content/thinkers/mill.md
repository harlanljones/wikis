# John Stuart Mill

John Stuart Mill was the English philosopher who gave liberalism its classic defence of individual freedom. In *On Liberty* he argued that society may interfere with a person only to prevent harm to others, and he spent his life applying that principle to speech, to ways of living and to the position of women.

## Life

Mill was born in London in 1806 and was the subject of a famous experiment. His father, James Mill, a follower of [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]], taught him Greek at three and Latin at eight, logic at twelve and economics at thirteen, to make him the perfect utilitarian reformer. At twenty he fell into a deep depression. He asked himself whether he would be happy if all his reforms were achieved, and the honest answer was no. Poetry, especially Wordsworth, helped him recover, and he concluded that his education had starved the feelings.

He worked for thirty-five years at the East India Company, the private body that governed British India. In 1830 he met Harriet Taylor, who was married. They kept up a close friendship for twenty years and married in 1851 after her husband died. Mill credited her with a large share in his major works. She died in 1858, and *On Liberty* is dedicated to her. From 1865 to 1868 he sat in Parliament, where he proposed giving women the vote. He died in Avignon in 1873.

## The main ideas

### The harm principle

*On Liberty* is not mainly about tyrannical governments. Following [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]], Mill fears the "tyranny of the prevailing opinion and feeling," the pressure of society on anyone who lives differently. Against it he asserts "one very simple principle":

> The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.

We may argue with a person, plead with them or avoid them, but we may not force them for their own sake. The principle applies to adults in full possession of their faculties. See [[debates/harm-principle|the harm principle]] for the arguments it started.

### Freedom of thought and discussion

Chapter 2 gives the classic case for free speech. Silencing an opinion is always a loss.

- The opinion may be **true**. To deny this is to assume we cannot be wrong.
- It may be **partly true**, and only the collision of opinions brings out the rest of the truth.
- Even if it is **false**, a true belief that is never challenged becomes a "dead dogma," held by habit and no longer understood.

The limit comes where speech turns into instigation of harm. One may write in the press that corn dealers starve the poor. One may not say it to an angry mob outside a corn dealer's house.

### Individuality

Mill wants more than non-interference. He wants a society of strong and varied characters. People should try "experiments in living," since no single pattern suits everyone and custom is not proof. Someone who lets the world choose their plan of life needs no faculty but "the ape-like one of imitation." Human nature is "not a machine to be built after a model" but a tree that needs to grow on all sides.

### A revised utilitarianism

Mill never gave up the principle that actions are right as they promote happiness, but he changed [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham's]] version of it. Pleasures differ in **quality** as well as amount. Those who have known both prefer the pleasures of mind and feeling. "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."

He also tried to show that utility supports justice and rights. Rules of justice protect security, the most vital of all interests, and so carry special weight. Whether a utilitarian can hold an almost absolute harm principle is the oldest question about Mill. His answer is that liberty serves "utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being."

### Representative government

Mill held that the best government is representative, because taking part in public affairs educates citizens and because each person is the safest guardian of their own interests. He also feared the rule of an uneducated majority. He therefore supported proportional representation, so that minorities would be heard, and proposed giving extra votes to the better educated. See [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].

### The subjection of women

*The Subjection of Women* argues that the legal subordination of one sex to the other is wrong in itself and one of the chief obstacles to human improvement. What is called the nature of women is "an eminently artificial thing," the result of forced repression in some directions and unnatural stimulation in others. No one can know what women are capable of until they are free to try. Marriage as the law then stood was, he said, the only actual bondage known to English law.

In later life Mill also moved towards a form of socialism based on worker-owned cooperatives.

## Criticism and influence

The harm principle has been attacked from both sides. Conservatives such as James Fitzjames Stephen argued that society has every right to enforce a shared morality. Others ask what counts as harm, since almost any act affects someone.

Mill explicitly excluded "barbarians" from the principle of liberty, saying that despotism is a legitimate way of governing them if it improves them. He defended British rule in India on those terms, and critics see in this the standing temptation of liberal empire.

For all that, *On Liberty* remains the first text anyone reaches for in arguments about free speech, drugs, censorship and paternalism.

## Where to start

*On Liberty* is short, and chapters 1 to 3 are essential. Then *Utilitarianism*, chapters 2 and 5, and *The Subjection of Women*, chapter 1.
