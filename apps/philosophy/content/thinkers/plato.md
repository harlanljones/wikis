# Plato

Plato was the Athenian philosopher who turned the conversations of his teacher Socrates into the first great body of Western philosophy. His *Republic* asks what justice is and answers with a picture of a city ruled by philosophers, an answer that almost every later political thinker has felt the need to accept, repair or attack.

## Life

Plato was born into a wealthy Athenian family during the Peloponnesian War, which Athens lost to Sparta in 404 BC. Two things in his youth shaped everything he wrote. The first was the brief, violent rule of the Thirty Tyrants, an oligarchy that included his own relatives. The second was the restored democracy's trial and execution of Socrates in 399 BC, on charges of impiety and corrupting the young.

Plato concluded that neither the rule of the few rich nor the rule of the many could be trusted with justice. Around 387 BC he founded the Academy, a school that lasted in some form for centuries. He also travelled to Syracuse in Sicily, where he tried and failed to educate its ruler, Dionysius II, into a philosopher. He died in Athens around 348 BC.

Socrates wrote nothing. Almost everything we know of him comes from Plato's dialogues, in which Socrates is usually the main speaker. Scholars generally read the early dialogues (such as the *Apology*) as closer to the historical Socrates and the later ones (such as the *Republic*) as Plato's own thought.

## The main ideas

### Justice in the soul and in the city

The *Republic* begins with a challenge. Thrasymachus, a sophist, claims that justice is only "the advantage of the stronger": rules made by those in power for their own benefit. Glaucon sharpens it with the story of the ring of Gyges, which makes its wearer invisible. If you could do wrong and never be caught, why be just at all?

Plato's answer is to look at justice "writ large" in a city and then read it back into the person. A city needs three kinds of work: ruling, defending and producing. A soul has three parts: reason, spirit (the seat of anger and pride) and appetite. In both, justice is each part doing its own job, with reason in charge. The unjust person is not a clever winner but a soul at war with itself, and so injustice is bad for the one who commits it.

### Philosopher-rulers

If ruling is a skill, Plato argues, it should be done by those who have it. The rulers of his ideal city are philosophers, selected by ability and trained for decades in mathematics and dialectic until they grasp the Form of the Good. To keep them from ruling for private gain, the guardian class owns no property and has no private families. Women with the right abilities are to be guardians too, a remarkable position for its time.

The famous image of the **cave** explains why such rulers are needed. Most people are like prisoners watching shadows on a wall and taking them for reality. The philosopher is the one who has climbed out, seen the sun, and must be made to go back down and govern.

### The case against democracy

Plato compares a democratic city to a ship whose crew, knowing nothing of navigation, fight over the helm and flatter the shipowner, while the true navigator is dismissed as a stargazer. In Book VIII he describes how regimes decay: rule of the best declines into rule by honour, then by wealth (oligarchy), then democracy, whose love of unlimited freedom ends by handing power to a tyrant who poses as the people's champion.

### The later Plato

In the *Statesman* and the *Laws*, his last and longest work, Plato is more cautious. Since true philosopher-rulers are unlikely to appear, the "second-best" city is one governed by fixed laws that bind the rulers themselves. This is one of the first statements of the rule of law.

## Criticism and influence

[[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] objected that Plato's city seeks too much unity and that shared property and families would weaken care, not spread it. The sharpest modern attack is Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), which read the *Republic* as a blueprint for totalitarian rule: a closed caste society held together by a "noble lie". Defenders reply that the *Republic* is a thought experiment about the soul as much as a political programme.

Either way, the questions are Plato's. Is ruling a kind of knowledge? Should experts or citizens decide? Can a city be good if its citizens are not? See [[debates/plato-vs-aristotle|Plato vs Aristotle]] for how the argument began.

## Where to start

Read the *Apology* first. It is short and is Socrates' defence speech at his trial. Then the *Crito*, on whether a citizen may disobey an unjust verdict (an early source for [[ideas/authority-and-obligation|political obligation]]). Then the *Republic*, especially Books I, II, IV, VII and VIII.
