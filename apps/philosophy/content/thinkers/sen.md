# Amartya Sen

Amartya Sen is the Indian economist and philosopher who changed the way poverty, development and justice are measured. He argues that what matters is not how much money or happiness people have but what they are actually able to do and be, and that the pursuit of justice should begin with removing plain injustices.

## Life

Sen was born in 1933 in Santiniketan, Bengal, on the campus of the school founded by the poet Rabindranath Tagore, who is said to have suggested his first name. Two childhood experiences stayed with him. In 1943 he saw the Bengal famine, in which some two to three million people died. And during the riots before the partition of India, a Muslim labourer named Kader Mia, who had come to a Hindu area because his family needed the wages, was stabbed and staggered into the Sens' garden. He died in hospital. Sen took from it a lesson about how poverty removes other freedoms, and about the violence of reducing people to a single identity.

He studied in Calcutta and at Cambridge and has taught at Delhi, the London School of Economics, Oxford and Harvard. From 1998 to 2004 he was Master of Trinity College, Cambridge. He won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics in 1998 for work on welfare economics and social choice. He helped design the United Nations' Human Development Index. He holds Indian citizenship.

## The main ideas

### Social choice

Sen's early work is in the mathematical theory of collective decisions. The economist Kenneth Arrow had proved that no voting rule can meet a short list of reasonable conditions at once, a result that seemed to make "the will of the people" incoherent. Sen showed that the problem eases when we allow richer information, such as comparisons of how well off different people are.

He also proved the "liberal paradox." Even a minimal commitment to individual liberty can conflict with the principle that if everyone prefers one outcome to another, it should be chosen. Respecting rights and satisfying preferences are different things.

### Famines

*Poverty and Famines* overturned the common belief that famines happen when there is not enough food. Studying Bengal in 1943 and later famines in Ethiopia and Bangladesh, Sen found that food supply was often no lower than in normal years. People starved because they lost their **entitlement** to food, meaning their ability to command it through wages, trade or production. Rural labourers whose wages fell behind soaring prices died while food was available nearby.

He later added a political finding: no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press. Governments that face elections and criticism have strong reasons to act. A free press and an opposition are an early warning system. See [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].

### Capabilities

In a 1979 lecture, "Equality of What?", Sen pointed out that every theory of justice wants equality of something. The question is what.

- **Welfare** or happiness is a poor measure, because people adapt to deprivation. Someone poor and oppressed may learn to want little and be content with it.
- **Resources**, such as income or [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls's]] primary goods, are a poor measure for another reason. People differ in how well they can turn resources into living. A person with a disability, a pregnant woman and a person in a region with malaria need different amounts to reach the same level.

Sen's answer is **capabilities**. A person's **functionings** are the things they manage to do and be: being nourished, being literate, taking part in the life of the community, appearing in public without shame. Their capability is the range of functionings they are really able to choose. It is a kind of freedom. Someone who fasts and someone who starves have the same functioning, but only one has the capability to eat.

*Development as Freedom* draws the conclusion for poor countries. Development is the expansion of people's real freedoms, not the growth of national income, which is only a means. Freedom is both the goal of development and its main instrument.

Applying this, Sen calculated in 1990 that more than a hundred million women were "missing" in Asia and North Africa, the result of neglect of girls' health and nutrition and of the abortion of female fetuses.

### The idea of justice

*The Idea of Justice* is dedicated to the memory of Rawls and is an argument with him. Rawls, like [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]], [[thinkers/locke|Locke]], [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] and [[thinkers/kant|Kant]], asks what perfectly just institutions would be. Sen calls this **transcendental institutionalism** and thinks it is neither necessary nor sufficient. To know that slavery or the neglect of famine victims is unjust, we do not need to agree on the ideal society, any more than we need to know the tallest mountain in the world to compare two smaller ones.

He places himself in a different line, running from [[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]] and Condorcet through [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Wollstonecraft]], [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] and [[thinkers/mill|Mill]], which compares the actual lives people lead and asks how to reduce injustice. He uses two Sanskrit words for the contrast: *niti*, the rightness of rules and institutions, and *nyaya*, the justice that is actually realised in the world.

Reasoning about justice should be public and open to distant voices, as with Smith's impartial spectator, and should be expected to leave several reasonable answers standing.

### Identity

In *Identity and Violence* Sen argues that every person belongs to many groups at once, by nationality, religion, language, profession, politics and taste. Violence is encouraged by the illusion that one of these, usually religion or civilisation, defines a person completely.

## Criticism and influence

Sen declines to give a fixed list of the capabilities that matter or to say how to weigh them, holding that each society should decide through public discussion. [[thinkers/nussbaum|Martha Nussbaum]], who developed the approach with him, argues that without a list it cannot ground firm entitlements. Rawlsians reply that comparing injustices still requires some idea of what justice is. Some historians have questioned whether the link between democracy and the absence of famine is as tight as he claims.

His influence on practice has been unusually direct. International bodies now measure development by health, education and freedom as well as income.

## Where to start

*Development as Freedom*, the introduction and chapters 1 to 4, is written for general readers. Then the preface and introduction to *The Idea of Justice*.
