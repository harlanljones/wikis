# Frantz Fanon

Frantz Fanon was the psychiatrist and revolutionary from Martinique who analysed what colonial rule does to the minds of the colonised and to those who rule them. His books became the central texts of the movements for decolonisation and of later thinking about race and empire.

## Life

Fanon was born in 1925 in Fort-de-France, Martinique, a French colony in the Caribbean, into a middle-class Black family. At eighteen he left the island to join the Free French forces and was wounded fighting in France in 1944. The racism he met in the army and afterwards, from the people he had fought to liberate, shaped his thinking.

He studied medicine and psychiatry in Lyon. In 1953 he became head of a ward at the psychiatric hospital of Blida-Joinville in Algeria, where he treated both Algerians and French soldiers and policemen, including men who tortured for a living. The Algerian war of independence began in 1954. In 1956 he resigned, writing that it was absurd to try to cure people in a society that systematically dehumanised them. He was expelled from Algeria and worked for the National Liberation Front (FLN) from Tunis as a writer, doctor and ambassador.

In 1960 he was found to have leukaemia. He dictated *The Wretched of the Earth* in a few months and died in December 1961 in a hospital near Washington, aged thirty-six. Algeria became independent the following year.

## The main ideas

### The lived experience of race

*Black Skin, White Masks* is a study of the psychology of racism, written from the inside. In a colonial world, to be human is to be white. The colonised person is taught to see their own language, colour and culture as inferior, and tries to escape by putting on a "white mask": speaking perfect French, adopting French manners, seeking white approval.

The escape fails. In the book's best-known scene, a child on a train points at him: "Look, a Negro! ... Mama, I'm frightened!" In that moment he is fixed from outside, made into an object and loaded with every story told about his race. He is, he writes, "overdetermined from without."

Fanon uses [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel's]] master and slave to say what is missing. In Hegel, each side seeks **recognition** from the other. The colonial master wants no recognition from the slave, only work. And freedom handed down by the master, without a struggle, leaves the old relation in place.

The book ends not with a call to racial pride but with a hope to get beyond race altogether. "The Negro is not. Any more than the white man."

### Colonialism and violence

*The Wretched of the Earth* opens with the chapter that made it notorious. The colonial world is "a world cut in two," with the settlers' town of stone and light on one side and the native quarter on the other, the border marked by barracks and police stations. It was created by violence and is kept in place by violence. It "is not a thinking machine" and will only yield to greater force.

So decolonisation "is always a violent phenomenon." Fanon goes further and claims that for the colonised person violence is "a cleansing force" that frees them from their sense of inferiority and binds the people together.

Readers disagree about how to take this. Sartre's preface celebrated the violence in terms more extreme than Fanon's own. [[thinkers/arendt|Arendt]], in *On Violence*, criticised Fanon and still more his admirers. But the book's last chapter consists of case notes from his psychiatric practice, describing the mental wreckage the war left in torturers and victims alike. Fanon knew what violence costs.

### The pitfalls of national consciousness

The chapters that followed proved prophetic. Fanon warned that independence could simply replace the foreign rulers with a local elite. The "national bourgeoisie" of the new states, having no industry of its own, would take over the old colonial positions and act as agent for foreign business. The party that led the struggle would harden into a single-party state around a leader, and national unity would break down into tribal and regional rivalry.

Real liberation would need to involve the rural poor, whom Fanon, departing from [[thinkers/marx|Marx]], saw as the revolutionary class in colonial countries, and would need a national culture that is made in the struggle, not dug up from the past.

### A new humanism

The book's conclusion tells the new nations not to imitate Europe, which talks endlessly of Man while murdering men everywhere. The task is to "work out new concepts" and "set afoot a new man." Fanon's universalism is a demand that the humanism Europe preached be made true for everyone.

## Criticism and influence

The main objection is to the claim that violence heals. The history of many states born in armed struggle suggests that it more often carries over into the new regime, as Fanon half foresaw. His picture of the peasantry has also been called romantic, and his account of gender, especially his chapter on Algerian women and the veil, has drawn feminist criticism.

His influence was immediate. He was read by the Black Panthers in the United States, by Steve Biko in South Africa and by liberation movements across Africa, Asia and Latin America. Postcolonial studies, through writers such as Edward Said and Homi Bhabha, takes much of its starting point from him, and his analysis of internalised racism remains a reference point in discussions of race.

## Where to start

*Black Skin, White Masks*, chapter 5 ("The Lived Experience of the Black Man"). *The Wretched of the Earth*, chapters 1 and 3 and the conclusion.
