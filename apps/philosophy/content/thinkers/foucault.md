# Michel Foucault

Michel Foucault was the French philosopher and historian who changed how power is understood. He argued that modern power works less through kings, laws and prohibitions than through the everyday shaping of bodies and minds in prisons, schools, hospitals and offices, and that it is bound up with the knowledge these institutions produce.

## Life

Foucault was born in 1926 in Poitiers, the son of a surgeon who expected him to follow the profession. He studied philosophy and psychology at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, suffered periods of severe depression, and worked in Sweden, Poland and Tunisia before returning to France. *The Order of Things* was an unexpected bestseller in 1966. In 1970 he was elected to the Collège de France, the summit of French academic life, taking a chair he named "History of Systems of Thought."

After 1968 he became a political activist. He helped found a group that gathered and published prisoners' own accounts of conditions in French jails, and he campaigned for migrants, dissidents and refugees. His enthusiastic newspaper reports on the Iranian Revolution in 1978 and 1979 are the most criticised episode of his career. He died in Paris in June 1984 of an illness related to AIDS, one of the first prominent public figures in France to do so. His lectures at the Collège de France, published since, have added greatly to his work.

## The main ideas

### Histories of the present

Foucault wrote histories, but with a philosopher's purpose. He took things that seem natural and timeless, such as madness, illness, crime and sexuality, and showed that the ways we think about them have a recent and contingent origin. He called his later method **genealogy**, after [[thinkers/nietzsche|Nietzsche]]. Its aim is to show that what is could have been otherwise.

### Discipline

*Discipline and Punish* opens with two scenes. In 1757 a man who tried to kill the king of France is publicly tortured and torn apart. Eighty years later a prison timetable sets out the inmates' day, minute by minute. Punishment has stopped being a spectacle on the body and become a routine aimed at the soul.

The usual story calls this progress in humanity. Foucault calls it a new technique of power, which he names **discipline**. It works by distributing people in space, breaking down their movements, training them by drill, watching them, examining them and ranking them against a **norm**. It spread from monasteries and armies to schools, factories, hospitals and prisons. Its product is the "docile body," useful and obedient.

His emblem for it is [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham's]] **Panopticon**, the prison where inmates can always be seen and never know when they are being watched. They end by watching themselves. "Visibility is a trap." Foucault asks whether it is surprising that "prisons resemble factories, schools, barracks, hospitals, which all resemble prisons."

### Power and knowledge

Foucault's account of power overturns the picture inherited from [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]], in which power is something a sovereign holds and uses to forbid. In political theory, he said, "we still have not cut off the king's head." His alternative has four parts.

- Power is **not a possession**. It is a relation, exercised from countless points, running through the whole social body.
- Power is **productive**. It does not only say no. It creates skills, desires, categories and identities.
- Power and knowledge **imply one another**. The human sciences (psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy) grew up inside institutions of control, and their classifications, such as "the delinquent" or "the homosexual," make up the kinds of people they claim to describe.
- "Where there is power, there is resistance." Power relations are never total, and they can always be contested.

### Biopower and governmentality

*The History of Sexuality* argues that the Victorian age did not simply repress sex. It made people talk about it endlessly, in confessionals, clinics and courtrooms, and made it the key to who a person is. This belongs to a wider shift. The old sovereign power was the right to take life or let live. Modern power takes charge of life itself: birth rates, health, hygiene, longevity. Foucault calls this **biopower**, the management of populations.

In his lectures he coined a wider term, **governmentality**, for the many ways of directing people's conduct, by states and by others. His 1979 lectures gave an early analysis of neoliberalism as a way of governing that asks each person to act as an "entrepreneur of himself."

### The care of the self

His last books turn to ancient Greek and Roman ethics, in which people worked on themselves to give their lives a certain form. He seems to have been looking for a kind of freedom that is not release from power but a practice within it.

## Criticism and influence

The best-known objection comes from [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]] and Nancy Fraser. Foucault's histories are plainly written to make us object to the things he describes. But if every claim to truth and justice is itself an effect of power, on what ground does he object? He seems to use standards he will not defend. Charles Taylor added that without some notion of truth and freedom, the idea of liberation from power makes no sense.

Others find his picture so total that resistance looks hopeless, and historians have disputed his dates and sources.

Foucault replied that his point was "not that everything is bad, but that everything is dangerous," which calls for alertness, not despair. His concepts are now basic equipment across the social sciences and humanities, in the study of prisons, medicine, sexuality, colonialism and surveillance.

## Where to start
