# Aristotle

Aristotle was the Greek philosopher who made ethics and politics into systematic subjects of study. He argued that human beings are political animals who can only live a full life in a community, and that the purpose of a city is not merely to keep people alive but to help them live well.

| At a glance | |
| --- | --- |
| **Lived** | 384–322 BC, Stagira and Athens |
| **Wrote** | *Nicomachean Ethics*, *Politics*, *Rhetoric*, and works on logic, biology and physics |
| **Big idea** | The good life is a life of virtue lived among fellow citizens, and good government serves the common good |
| **Belongs with** | Virtue ethics, [[traditions/republicanism\|republicanism]] and, much later, [[traditions/communitarianism\|communitarianism]] |
| **Argued with** | His teacher [[thinkers/plato\|Plato]] |

## Life

Aristotle was born in Stagira in northern Greece, the son of a physician to the Macedonian court. At seventeen he went to Athens and studied at Plato's Academy for about twenty years. After Plato's death he left, and from about 343 BC he was tutor to the young Alexander of Macedon, later Alexander the Great. He returned to Athens in 335 BC and founded his own school, the Lyceum.

He was never an Athenian citizen. As a resident foreigner with Macedonian ties he was exposed when Alexander died in 323 BC and anti-Macedonian feeling rose. He left for Chalcis, reportedly so that Athens would not "sin twice against philosophy", and died there the next year.

His surviving works read like lecture notes: dense, careful, and full of distinctions. Where Plato reasons from an ideal, Aristotle starts from what exists. He and his students are said to have collected the constitutions of 158 Greek cities. One, the *Constitution of the Athenians*, survives.

## The main ideas

### The good life

The *Nicomachean Ethics* asks what the highest human good is. Everyone agrees on the word: *eudaimonia*, usually translated "happiness" but better understood as flourishing or living well. Aristotle argues that the good for a human being lies in doing well what is distinctively human, which is living by reason. Flourishing is therefore "activity of the soul in accordance with virtue" over a whole life.

A **virtue** is a settled disposition of character, and it usually lies in a mean between two extremes. Courage sits between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between meanness and waste. Virtues are acquired by habit: we become just by doing just acts, as we become builders by building. Knowing where the mean lies in a particular case takes **practical wisdom** (*phronesis*), which cannot be reduced to rules. This approach is the root of virtue ethics, one of the three main [[ideas/ethical-theories|ethical theories]].

### The political animal

The *Politics* opens with a claim about human nature. The household exists for daily needs and the village for more than daily needs, but only the city (*polis*) is complete. It "comes into being for the sake of living, but exists for the sake of living well." A human being is by nature a **political animal**, because humans alone have speech and can argue about what is just and unjust. Someone who can live without a city is "either a beast or a god."

This is a direct contrast with later [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] thinkers such as [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]], for whom political society is an artificial device that naturally separate individuals agree to build.

### Six kinds of constitution

Aristotle classifies governments by two questions: who rules, and in whose interest.

| Who rules | For the common good | For the rulers' own good |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | Kingship | Tyranny |
| Few | Aristocracy | Oligarchy |
| Many | Polity | Democracy |

By "democracy" he means rule by the poor majority in their own interest, and by "oligarchy" rule by the rich. The best constitution that most cities can hope for is **polity**, a mixture of the two, resting on a large middle class. Those who are neither very rich nor very poor are most likely to listen to reason and least likely to plot against each other. This is an early form of the mixed constitution later developed by [[thinkers/cicero|Cicero]] and [[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]].

A **citizen**, for Aristotle, is not just someone who lives in a place. It is someone who shares in ruling and being ruled in turn, by sitting in the assembly and the courts.

### Justice

Book V of the *Ethics* separates **distributive justice**, which gives out honours and goods in proportion to merit, from **corrective justice**, which restores the balance after a wrong. The idea that justice means treating equals equally and unequals unequally comes from here, as does the point that people fight over what counts as merit. See [[ideas/justice|justice]].

## Criticism and influence

Aristotle defended slavery, arguing that some people are "slaves by nature", and held that women are naturally fit to be ruled by men. Both views were used for centuries to justify domination, and both are rejected by modern readers, including those who admire the rest. His citizens are a leisured minority.

His influence is hard to overstate. Medieval thinkers called him simply "the Philosopher", and [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] rebuilt Christian thought around him. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] wrote *Leviathan* in large part against him. In the twentieth century [[thinkers/arendt|Arendt]], Alasdair MacIntyre and [[thinkers/nussbaum|Nussbaum]] all returned to him for an alternative to modern theories built on rights and utility.

## Where to start

In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, read Books I and II (the good life and virtue) and Book V (justice). In the *Politics*, read Book I (the political animal), Book III (citizens and constitutions) and Book IV, chapter 11 (the middle class). Then see [[debates/plato-vs-aristotle|Plato vs Aristotle]].
