# Max Weber

Max Weber was the German sociologist who defined the modern state by its monopoly of legitimate force and asked why people obey. His accounts of authority, bureaucracy and the ethics of political leadership are starting points for the study of politics as it is practised.

| At a glance | |
| --- | --- |
| **Lived** | 1864–1920, Germany |
| **Wrote** | *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904–05), "Politics as a Vocation" (1919), *Economy and Society* (1922) |
| **Big idea** | Modern life is ruled more and more by calculation and bureaucracy, which bring efficiency and threaten freedom |
| **Belongs with** | Political [[traditions/realism\|realism]] and the founders of sociology |
| **Argued with** | [[thinkers/marx\|Marx]], and the revolutionaries and pacifists of 1918 to 1919 |

## Life

Weber was born in 1864 in Erfurt and grew up in Berlin, where his father was a National Liberal politician and his mother a devout Calvinist. He trained in law and economic history and became a professor in his early thirties. In 1897, after a violent quarrel with his father, who died weeks later, he suffered a breakdown that kept him from teaching for most of two decades. He wrote his major works as a private scholar in Heidelberg.

A fierce nationalist in his youth, he supported the German war effort in 1914 and then became one of the sharpest critics of the Kaiser's government. After the defeat he helped found a liberal party and advised on the drafting of the Weimar constitution. He died of pneumonia in Munich in June 1920, aged fifty-six. His wife, Marianne Weber, a scholar and feminist, edited his unfinished manuscripts.

## The main ideas

### What a state is

"Politics as a Vocation" was a lecture given to students in Munich in January 1919, with Germany in revolution. Weber starts by defining the state, not by its aims, which vary, but by its means.

> A state is a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory.

Other groups may use force only so far as the state allows it. The word "legitimate" matters. A state lasts when those it rules accept its right to command. See [[ideas/sovereignty|sovereignty]] and [[ideas/power|power]].

### Three kinds of legitimate authority

Why do people accept commands? Weber names three pure types. Real cases mix them.

| Type | People obey because | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional | "It has always been so" | Kings, elders, patriarchs |
| Charismatic | They believe in the extraordinary gifts of a leader | Prophets, war heroes, demagogues |
| Legal-rational | The command follows from rules properly made | The modern official, obeyed for the office and not the person |

Charisma is the revolutionary force in history, but it is unstable. When the leader dies it must be "routinised" into tradition or rules. Note that Weber uses "legitimate" as a description of what people believe, not as a judgement of what they ought to believe. See [[ideas/authority-and-obligation|authority and obligation]].

### Bureaucracy and the iron cage

Legal-rational authority works through **bureaucracy**: trained officials in a hierarchy, applying written rules without regard to persons. Weber thought it technically superior to every other form of organisation, as a machine is to hand tools, and therefore unstoppable, in business and political parties as much as in government.

This is one face of a wider process he called **rationalisation**: the spread of calculation, method and expertise into every area of life. Its companion is the **disenchantment of the world**, the retreat of magic and mystery. *The Protestant Ethic* argues that the disciplined, calculating spirit of modern capitalism grew in part from Calvinist anxiety about salvation. The religious root has died, but the system remains and now compels everyone born into it, like a "shell as hard as steel," a phrase famously translated as the **iron cage**.

Against [[thinkers/marx|Marx]], Weber held that ideas can be causes in history and not only effects. He also predicted that socialism would bring more bureaucracy, not less, since it would join the bureaucracies of state and business into one.

### Two ethics of politics

The last part of "Politics as a Vocation" asks what kind of person may put a hand on the wheel of history. Politics works with power, backed by violence, and whoever takes it up "contracts with diabolical powers."

Weber contrasts two outlooks.

- The **ethic of conviction** acts on principle and leaves the results to God or blames them on the world.
- The **ethic of responsibility** answers for the foreseeable consequences of what one does.

The saint may follow the first. The politician must weigh the second. But Weber does not simply choose. A mature leader feels responsibility for consequences with heart and soul and yet reaches a point where he says, "Here I stand; I can do no other." Politics needs passion, a sense of responsibility and judgement. It is "a strong and slow boring of hard boards."

### Facts and values

In "Science as a Vocation" Weber argued that scholarship can tell us what is the case and what means serve which ends, but not which ends to choose. Ultimate values are in permanent conflict, like warring gods, and each person must decide which to serve. Teachers should not use the lecture hall as a pulpit.

## Criticism and influence

Weber wanted a strong, directly elected president to balance parliament and bureaucracy with charismatic leadership. Critics have linked this "leader democracy" to the weaknesses of Weimar, though Weber died thirteen years before Hitler took power. Leo Strauss argued that the fact and value split leads to nihilism, and [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]] that Weber's notion of reason was too narrow.

His definitions are still the working vocabulary of social science. [[thinkers/schmitt|Carl Schmitt]] attended his lectures, and the Frankfurt School combined his rationalisation with [[thinkers/marx|Marx's]] theory of capitalism.

## Where to start

"Politics as a Vocation" in full. It is the best single lecture on political ethics. Then "Science as a Vocation," and the final pages of *The Protestant Ethic*.
