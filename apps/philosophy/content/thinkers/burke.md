# Edmund Burke

Edmund Burke was the Irish-born British statesman whose attack on the French Revolution became the founding text of modern conservatism. He argued that a society is an inheritance to be improved with care, not a machine to be torn down and rebuilt from a theory.

## Life

Burke was born in Dublin in 1729 to a Protestant father and a Catholic mother, and moved to London to study law, which he gave up for writing. An early book on the sublime and the beautiful made his literary name. In 1765 he became secretary to the Whig leader Lord Rockingham and entered Parliament, where he sat for nearly thirty years, almost always in opposition.

He was not a reactionary by the standards of his day. He defended the American colonists' complaints against taxation from London, pressed for relief for Irish Catholics, attacked royal patronage, and spent years prosecuting Warren Hastings, the governor-general of Bengal, for abuses by the East India Company. Then, in 1790, he turned on the French Revolution with a fury that split his party. He died in 1797.

## The main ideas

### Against government by theory

The *Reflections* was written before the Terror, when most British opinion still admired events in France. Burke predicted that the Revolution would end in bloodshed and military dictatorship. His reason was its method. The revolutionaries were destroying the church, the nobility and the old provinces in the name of abstract "rights of man," and trying to build a state from first principles.

Burke did not deny that people have rights. He denied that they can be read off from a theory without regard to circumstances. "The science of constructing a commonwealth, or renovating it, or reforming it, is, like every other experimental science, not to be taught *a priori*." Liberty in the abstract is no more worth congratulating than a madman's escape from his cell. One must see what it is combined with.

### Inheritance, prejudice and prescription

The English, on Burke's account, treat their liberties as "an entailed inheritance" handed down from their forefathers, not as conclusions of an argument. Three of his terms carry the case.

- **Prejudice** means the settled, untaught feelings and habits of a people. "The individual is foolish," but the species is wise, and prejudice puts that stored wisdom to work at the moment of decision.
- **Prescription** means that long possession gives a title. Institutions earn authority by lasting.
- The **little platoon** is the family, neighbourhood or rank a person belongs to. To love it "is the first principle (the germ as it were) of public affections."

### A contract across generations

Burke keeps the language of the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] and transforms it. Society is indeed a contract, but not like a trade deal in pepper or coffee, to be dissolved at will. It is a partnership in all science, all art and every virtue, and since its ends cannot be reached in many generations,

> it becomes a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born.

The living are temporary holders of an estate. They have no right to waste it.

### Reform

Burke was not against change. "A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation." The Revolution of 1688, which he defended, was on his reading a repair made to preserve the constitution. Good reform works with existing materials, proceeds slowly and can be corrected. This is the difference between reform and revolution.

### The representative

In a 1774 speech to his voters in Bristol, Burke gave the classic statement of one view of representation. A member of Parliament owes his constituents his judgement, "and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion." Parliament is a deliberative assembly of one nation, not a congress of ambassadors from rival interests. Bristol did not re-elect him. See [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].

## Criticism and influence

Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man* answered that Burke had given the dead authority over the living, and that he "pities the plumage, but forgets the dying bird" by weeping for the queen and ignoring the poor. [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Wollstonecraft]] made a similar case within weeks of the *Reflections* appearing. See [[debates/burke-vs-paine|Burke vs Paine]].

The standing objection is that respect for inheritance can defend any injustice that is old enough. Burke's own record on India and Ireland suggests he did not mean it so, but the principle that separates a venerable institution from an ancient abuse is hard to state.

His insights into unintended consequences, the limits of planning and the value of institutions between individual and state return in [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]], [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] and the [[traditions/communitarianism|communitarians]].

## Where to start

The *Reflections* is a long open letter with no chapters. Good abridgements exist. Add the "Speech to the Electors of Bristol" (1774) and the "Speech on Conciliation with the Colonies" (1775).
