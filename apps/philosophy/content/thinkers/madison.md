# James Madison

James Madison was the Virginian statesman who did most to design the United States Constitution and, in *The Federalist*, gave the best explanation of it. He argued that a large republic with divided and competing powers could protect liberty without relying on the virtue of its rulers or citizens.

## Life

Madison was born in 1751 into a planter family in Virginia and studied at the College of New Jersey, now Princeton. He was small, frail and bookish, and a tireless committee man. Before the Constitutional Convention of 1787 he studied the history of ancient and modern confederacies and wrote a memo on the "vices" of the American system. He arrived in Philadelphia with a plan, took the fullest notes of the debates, and then co-wrote *The Federalist* to persuade New York to ratify.

In the first Congress he drafted the amendments that became the Bill of Rights. He broke with Hamilton, founded the Republican party with Jefferson, and served as Secretary of State and then as the fourth president, from 1809 to 1817. He died in 1836, the last of the framers.

He owned enslaved people all his life and freed none, while calling slavery a "dreadful calamity." That contradiction runs through the constitution he designed.

## The main ideas

### The problem of faction

*Federalist* No. 10 addresses the old disease of popular governments: faction. A faction is a group of citizens, "whether amounting to a majority or minority of the whole," united by some passion or interest that is opposed to the rights of others or the good of the community.

There are two ways to cure it. One can remove its causes, either by destroying liberty, which is worse than the disease, or by giving everyone the same opinions and interests, which is impossible. "The latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man," and the most common source is "the various and unequal distribution of property." So the only course is to control its effects.

A minority faction can be outvoted. The real danger is a **majority** faction, which can use the forms of popular government to oppress. See [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].

### The extended republic

[[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] had taught that republics must be small. Madison reverses the point, with help from [[thinkers/hume|Hume]]. In a small republic one interest can easily form a majority. "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests," so that a majority with a common motive to invade others' rights is less likely to form or to act together. Representation adds a second filter, by passing public views through a chosen body of citizens.

Size and variety, which the older tradition thought fatal to free government, become its protection.

### Checks and balances

*Federalist* No. 51 explains how to keep the branches of government in their places. Written limits are only "parchment barriers." Each branch must have the means and the motive to resist the others.

> Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place.

The reasoning behind this is frank about human nature. "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary." Since neither is the case, one must first enable the government to control the governed and then oblige it to control itself.

In the American system power is divided twice: between the federal government and the states, and within each among legislature, executive and courts. Madison calls this a "double security" for the rights of the people. See [[ideas/separation-of-powers|separation of powers]].

### Religious liberty

Madison's "Memorial and Remonstrance against Religious Assessments" opposed a Virginia tax to support Christian teachers. Religion, he argued, must be left to each person's conviction and conscience. It is a right towards other people because it is first a duty towards God, and the civil magistrate is not a competent judge of religious truth. This goes further than [[thinkers/locke|Locke's]] toleration, towards a full separation of church and state. See [[ideas/toleration|toleration]].

## Criticism and influence

The Anti-Federalists replied that so large a republic would put government out of the people's reach and in the hands of an elite. Later critics agree that the system was built to slow down majorities, and add that it has protected property and, for seventy years, slavery, better than it protected the weak. Others note that Madison did not foresee national political parties, which can unite the branches he meant to set against each other.

His approach is still the standard example of designing institutions for people as they are. It stands opposite [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau's]] hope of forming citizens who put the common good first.

## Where to start

*Federalist* Nos. 10 and 51 take half an hour and contain the core. Add Nos. 39 (what makes a government republican), 47 and 48 (separation of powers).
