# Augustine

Augustine of Hippo was the North African bishop whose *City of God* set the terms of Christian political thought for a thousand years. He taught that no earthly state can be truly just, that government exists mainly to restrain the damage done by human sin, and that a Christian's deepest loyalty lies elsewhere.

## Life

Augustine was born in Thagaste, in what is now Algeria, to a Christian mother, Monica, and a pagan father. He trained as a teacher of rhetoric, lived for years with a woman he never names, and passed through several philosophies before converting to Christianity in Milan in 386. The *Confessions*, written around 397 to 400, tells that story and is often called the first autobiography.

He returned to Africa, became bishop of the port city of Hippo, and spent the rest of his life preaching and writing. In 410 an army of Visigoths sacked Rome. Pagans blamed the disaster on the abandonment of the old gods. Augustine's reply, the *City of God*, took him from 413 to 426 and grew into a theory of all human history. He died in 430 while the Vandals were besieging Hippo.

## The main ideas

### Two cities

Augustine divides humanity not by nation or class but by love. "Two loves have made two cities": love of self, reaching as far as contempt of God, made the earthly city, and love of God, reaching as far as contempt of self, made the heavenly one. The two are mixed together in this life and will only be separated at the end of time. No empire or church on earth is simply identical with either.

The effect is to lower the stakes of politics. Rome was not eternal, and its fall was not the end of the world. Christians are pilgrims who use the peace of the earthly city without belonging to it.

### Why government exists

For [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]], political life completes human nature. For Augustine, coercive government is a result of the Fall. Had humans not sinned, no one would rule over another by force. As things are, people are driven by pride and the "lust for domination", and rulers, courts and punishment are a harsh remedy that keeps them from destroying each other. This is the sense in which political [[traditions/realism|realists]] have claimed him as an ancestor.

Even so, earthly peace is a real good. Augustine defines peace as "the tranquillity of order", and holds that even robbers and tyrants want a kind of peace on their own terms.

### Kingdoms and robber bands

Augustine takes up [[thinkers/cicero|Cicero's]] definition of a commonwealth as a people united by agreement about justice, and argues that by this standard Rome never was one, since true justice means giving God his due. He then asks a question that still stings:

> Take away justice, and what are kingdoms but great bands of robbers?

He tells the story of a captured pirate who told Alexander the Great that they did the same thing, only the pirate did it with one ship and was called a robber, while Alexander did it with a fleet and was called an emperor. Augustine offers a looser definition in its place: a people is a multitude united by agreement about the things it loves. Better loves make a better people.

### War and coercion

Augustine did not write a treatise on war, but his scattered remarks founded the just war tradition. War may be waged by a lawful authority to right a wrong, with the aim of peace, and without love of violence. [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] later put these points in order.

More troubling is his change of mind about religious coercion. At first he opposed forcing the Donatists, a breakaway African church, back into unity. Later he defended imperial penalties against them, citing the Gospel phrase "compel them to come in." That argument was used for centuries to justify persecution, and the case for [[ideas/toleration|toleration]] had to be made against it.

## Criticism and influence

Critics find Augustine's view of human nature too dark and his separation of the two cities a recipe for giving up on justice in this world. Admirers find in him a permanent warning against expecting salvation from politics. The twentieth-century theologian Reinhold Niebuhr drew on him for exactly that, and [[thinkers/arendt|Hannah Arendt]] wrote her doctoral thesis on his idea of love.

The medieval struggle between popes and emperors was fought largely in Augustine's vocabulary, as were the arguments of Luther and Calvin.

## Where to start

The *Confessions*, Books I to IX, for the person. In the *City of God*, read Book IV, chapter 4 (the robber bands), Book XIV, chapter 28 (the two loves) and Book XIX (peace, justice and the commonwealth), which is the centre of his political thought.
