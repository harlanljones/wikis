# Montesquieu

Montesquieu was the French judge and writer who taught that liberty depends on dividing power so that one part of government can check another. His *The Spirit of the Laws* also began the comparative study of politics, by asking why different societies have the laws they do.

| At a glance | |
| --- | --- |
| **Lived** | 1689–1755, France |
| **Wrote** | *Persian Letters* (1721), *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) |
| **Big idea** | To prevent the abuse of power, "power should be a check to power" |
| **Belongs with** | [[traditions/liberalism\|Liberalism]], constitutionalism and [[traditions/republicanism\|republican]] thought |
| **Influenced** | [[thinkers/madison\|Madison]] and the makers of the American Constitution |

## Life

Charles-Louis de Secondat, baron de La Brède et de Montesquieu, was born near Bordeaux in 1689 into the nobility of the law courts. He inherited from an uncle both his title and a senior judgeship in the Bordeaux *parlement*, one of the regional courts that could delay royal decrees. He sold the office after some years to write.

His first success, the *Persian Letters*, was a satire in which two Persian travellers describe France with puzzled honesty, a device that let him mock the king, the pope and Parisian manners. He then travelled for three years, spending about eighteen months in England, where he watched Parliament at work. *The Spirit of the Laws* took him some twenty years and appeared in 1748. The Catholic Church put it on the Index of banned books in 1751. He died in Paris in 1755.

## The main ideas

### Three kinds of government

Montesquieu sorts governments by their **nature** (who holds power) and by their **principle** (the passion that makes them work).

| Government | Who rules | What keeps it going |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Republic | The people, or a part of them | Virtue, meaning love of country and of equality |
| Monarchy | One person, by fixed laws | Honour, the ambition of ranks and orders |
| Despotism | One person, by whim | Fear |

A republic that loses its virtue is lost, which is why he thought republics must be small. A monarchy is kept moderate by "intermediate powers" such as the nobility, the towns and the courts, which stand between king and subject. Remove them and monarchy slides into despotism, the form he most dreaded and saw approaching in the France of Louis XIV's heirs.

### Liberty and the separation of powers

Political liberty, for Montesquieu, is not doing whatever one wants. It is "the right to do everything the laws permit," and the calm of mind that comes from each person's sense of security. That security exists only under moderate government, and experience shows that anyone with power is inclined to abuse it until they meet a limit.

Book XI, chapter 6, presented as a description of the English constitution, gives the remedy. There are three powers: making laws, executing them, and judging. "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person," there is no liberty, and none either if the power of judging is not separate from both. The powers should be placed in different hands, and each should be able to stop the others. His picture of England was not quite accurate, but it became the working model of constitutional design. See [[ideas/separation-of-powers|separation of powers]].

### Laws fit their setting

"Laws, in their most general sense, are the necessary relations arising from the nature of things." The laws of a people should suit its government, its climate and soil, its way of earning a living, its religion and its customs. Taken together these make up the "spirit" of the laws. Montesquieu's theory that hot climates produce servile peoples was crude and was later used to excuse empire, but the method of explaining institutions by their causes helped found sociology.

### Commerce

Montesquieu argued that trade "softens" manners. Nations that trade become dependent on each other and inclined to peace, and the spirit of commerce brings habits of thrift, moderation and order. This thesis, later called *doux commerce*, was taken up by [[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]] and [[thinkers/kant|Kant]].

## Criticism and influence

[[thinkers/madison|Madison]] called him "the oracle who is always consulted and cited" on the separation of powers, and then used the idea of a large federal republic to answer Montesquieu's claim that republics must be small. [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] took the opposite lesson, insisting that sovereignty cannot be divided. Montesquieu's defence of noble privileges as a barrier to despotism made him a hero to [[thinkers/burke|Burke]] and [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] and suspect to democrats.

His ironic chapter on the enslavement of Africans, which lists absurd arguments in its favour, was among the earlier Enlightenment attacks on slavery.

## Where to start

The *Persian Letters* for pleasure, especially the fable of the Troglodytes (letters 11 to 14). In *The Spirit of the Laws*, read Books II and III (kinds of government), Book VIII (how each is corrupted) and Book XI, chapter 6.
