# John Locke

John Locke was the English philosopher who argued that government is a trust, set up by free and equal people to protect rights they already have, and that a government which breaks that trust may be removed. His *Two Treatises of Government* is the founding text of liberalism.

## Life

Locke was born in Somerset in 1632, the son of a lawyer who fought on Parliament's side in the Civil War. He studied and taught at Oxford and trained in medicine. In 1666 he met Anthony Ashley Cooper, later Earl of Shaftesbury, and became his physician, secretary and adviser. Shaftesbury led the opposition to Charles II and the campaign to exclude the king's Catholic brother James from the throne.

When that campaign failed, Shaftesbury fled, and in 1683 Locke followed him into exile in Holland. He returned in 1689 after the Glorious Revolution had replaced James II with William and Mary. His three major works appeared within a year. The *Two Treatises* was published anonymously, and Locke admitted writing it only in his will. Scholars now agree that most of it was written around 1679 to 1683, as a call for resistance, and not afterwards as a defence of the settled revolution.

## The main ideas

### Against the divine right of kings

The *First Treatise* demolishes Sir Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha*, which claimed that kings inherit the fatherly authority God gave Adam. The *Second Treatise* builds the alternative.

### A state of nature with a law

Locke also starts from a [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]], but his is not [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes's]] war. It is "a state of perfect freedom" and equality, and it "has a law of nature to govern it." Reason teaches that, since all are equal and independent, "no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions." People are God's workmanship and not each other's to destroy.

What the state of nature lacks is a way of enforcing that law fairly. Everyone is judge in their own case. People are biased, punish too hard, and lack the power to carry out a just sentence. These "inconveniences" are the reason for government.

### Property

Locke uses "property" in a wide sense for a person's "life, liberty and estate". His account of how goods come to be owned starts from the claim that every person has a property in their own person. When someone "mixes their labour" with something in the common stock, by picking apples or tilling a field, it becomes theirs.

Two limits apply. There must be "enough, and as good, left in common for others," and no one may take more than they can use before it spoils. The invention of money, which does not spoil, lets people fairly pile up more than they can use, and Locke takes people to have agreed to the resulting inequality. See [[ideas/property|property]].

### Consent and trust

Political society begins when people agree to give up their private power to enforce the law of nature and to be bound by the majority. They do not hand over everything. They set up a legislature as a **trust**, limited to the purpose for which it was created: protecting life, liberty and estate. It must rule by known, standing laws, cannot take property without consent given through representatives, and cannot pass its power to anyone else.

Most people never sign anything, so Locke relies on **tacit consent**. Anyone who owns land, or even travels on the highway, has agreed to obey the laws. Critics from [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] onwards have found this the weakest part of the theory.

### The right to resist

When rulers act against the trust, by governing without law or plotting against the people's property, they put themselves into a state of war with the people, and power returns to the community. Who is to judge? "The people shall be judge." If there is no court on earth to settle it, they may "appeal to heaven," which means fight. Locke insists this will not cause constant upheaval, since people put up with a great deal before they stir.

### Toleration

The *Letter Concerning Toleration* argues that the state exists to protect civil interests and has no business saving souls. Belief cannot be forced, since "true and saving religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind." Churches are voluntary societies. Locke stopped short of tolerating atheists, whose oaths he thought could not be trusted, or those who owe allegiance to a foreign prince, by which he meant Catholics. See [[ideas/toleration|toleration]].

## Criticism and influence

Jefferson's Declaration of Independence, with its unalienable rights and its right of the people "to alter or to abolish" a destructive government, is Locke compressed. His influence runs through every later theory of limited government and through [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick's]] libertarianism.

His record has darker parts. He invested in the slave-trading Royal African Company and helped draft the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, which gave masters absolute power over slaves. His labour theory of property was used to argue that land the native peoples of America had not enclosed and farmed was free for the taking. Feminist critics note that the "free and equal" parties to the contract are heads of households, and Marxists that it protects existing property above all.

## Where to start

The *Second Treatise*, chapters 2 (state of nature), 5 (property), 8 and 9 (the beginning and ends of political society) and 19 (the dissolution of government). The *Letter Concerning Toleration* is short and clear. Then see [[debates/hobbes-locke-rousseau|Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau]].
