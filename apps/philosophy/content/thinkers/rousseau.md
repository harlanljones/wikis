# Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was the Genevan writer who argued that civilisation had made people unequal, dependent and unfree, and that the only legitimate political order is one in which citizens obey laws they have made for themselves. His *Social Contract* gave the modern world the idea of popular sovereignty.

## Life

Rousseau was born in 1712 in Geneva, a small Protestant republic, and signed his books "Citizen of Geneva." His mother died days after his birth and his father, a watchmaker, left when he was ten. At fifteen he ran away. He spent years as a servant, music copyist and tutor before arriving in Paris, where he befriended Diderot and wrote on music for the *Encyclopédie*.

In 1749, walking to visit Diderot in prison, he read an essay question asking whether the progress of the arts and sciences had improved morals. He answered no, won the prize, and became famous as the critic of the age of progress. In 1762 both *Emile* and *The Social Contract* were condemned in Paris and Geneva, and he fled arrest. Years of wandering followed, including an unhappy stay in England with [[thinkers/hume|Hume]]. He died near Paris in 1778. In 1794 the revolutionaries moved his remains to the Panthéon.

His life contradicted his books in ways his enemies enjoyed. The author of a great treatise on education left his own five children at a foundling hospital.

## The main ideas

### How inequality began

The *Discourse on Inequality* tells a speculative history of the human race. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]], Rousseau says, described civilised man and called him natural. Truly natural humans were solitary, healthy and peaceful, with few needs, a simple self-love (*amour de soi*) and an instinctive pity for suffering.

Things went wrong as people began to live together and compare themselves. A new passion appeared: *amour-propre*, the need to be thought well of, which makes people live "outside themselves" in the opinion of others. Then came farming, metalwork and private property.

> The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying "This is mine," and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society.

Property divided rich from poor, and the rich then proposed a government to protect everyone. The poor "ran headlong into their chains." The social contract of [[thinkers/locke|Locke]] is, on this account, a trick that made usurpation into right. See [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]] and [[ideas/equality|equality]].

### The legitimate contract

*The Social Contract* asks what a rightful order would look like. It opens: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." Rousseau does not promise to remove the chains but to say what could make them legitimate.

Force gives no right, and no one can sensibly give themselves into slavery. The solution is an agreement in which each person gives themselves and all their rights to the whole community. Since everyone gives everything, the terms are equal for all. And since each gives himself to all, he "gives himself to no one." Each person gains, as a member of the sovereign, the same authority over others that others gain over him.

### The general will

The community formed this way has a will of its own, the **general will**, which aims at the common good. It is not the same as the **will of all**, which is only the sum of private interests. When citizens vote on a law, they are not asked what they want for themselves but whether the proposal agrees with the general will.

Whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body, which, Rousseau adds, means only that he will be "forced to be free." Rousseau's point is that obeying the law one has helped make is what frees a person from dependence on other individuals. His critics hear something more sinister.

Several conditions follow:

- **Sovereignty cannot be handed over or divided.** The people must make the laws in person. Representatives are not allowed: "The English people thinks it is free. It is greatly mistaken. It is free only during the election of members of Parliament."
- **Government is only the people's agent**, an executive that applies the laws and can be changed at any time.
- **Citizens must be roughly equal**, so that no one is rich enough to buy another and no one so poor as to have to sell himself.
- **The state must be small**, with simple manners and a shared **civil religion** that teaches love of the laws.

See [[ideas/sovereignty|sovereignty]], [[ideas/democracy|democracy]] and [[ideas/liberty|liberty]].

### Education

*Emile* describes raising a child to be independent in a corrupt society, by letting him learn from things and not from authority. Its fifth book, on the education of Sophie as Emile's pleasing and dependent wife, provoked [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Mary Wollstonecraft's]] reply.

## Criticism and influence

Rousseau has been read as the prophet of democracy and as the ancestor of totalitarianism. Robespierre and the Jacobins revered him. Benjamin Constant, and later [[thinkers/berlin|Isaiah Berlin]], argued that the general will hands unlimited power to whoever claims to speak for the people, and that being "forced to be free" is the formula of every modern tyranny. Defenders reply that Rousseau demands unanimity on the contract, strict limits on what the sovereign may do, and conditions no large modern state meets.

[[thinkers/kant|Kant]] took from him the idea of freedom as self-given law. [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]] and [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] inherited his criticism of civil society, and [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] counted himself in his line. The questions he raised about inequality, authenticity and the price of progress have not gone away.

## Where to start

The *Discourse on Inequality*, Part Two, is the most gripping thing he wrote. Then *The Social Contract*, Books I and II. See [[debates/hobbes-locke-rousseau|Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau]] for the comparison.
