# Thomas Hobbes

Thomas Hobbes was the English philosopher who argued that without a government strong enough to overawe everyone, human life would be a war of all against all. His *Leviathan* founded modern social contract theory by deriving political authority from the consent of equal individuals, and then used that consent to justify nearly absolute power.

## Life

Hobbes was born in Malmesbury, Wiltshire, in April 1588, as the Spanish Armada approached England. He later joked that his mother gave birth to twins, "myself and fear". After Oxford he became tutor to the Cavendish family, later Earls of Devonshire, and stayed attached to them for most of his long life. Travels in Europe brought him into contact with Galileo and with the new mechanical science, and a chance reading of Euclid at about forty convinced him that politics could be reasoned out like geometry.

In 1640, as the conflict between Charles I and Parliament sharpened, Hobbes fled to Paris, "the first of all that fled" as he put it. There he wrote *Leviathan*, published in 1651, two years after the king's execution. The book pleased no one. Royalists disliked a defence of monarchy that rested on consent instead of divine right, and churchmen thought it atheist. Hobbes returned to England under Cromwell, and after the Restoration in 1660 he was protected by Charles II, his former pupil in mathematics. He died in 1679 at ninety-one.

## The argument of Leviathan

### Human beings as they are

Hobbes begins with a materialist account of human nature. People are bodies in motion, driven by appetites and aversions. There is no highest good that all pursue, as [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] thought. There is only "a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death," because no one can be sure of keeping what they have without getting more.

People are also roughly **equal**. The weakest can kill the strongest by stealth or by joining with others. Equal ability produces equal hope of getting what we want, and so competition.

### The state of nature

Imagine people with no common power over them. Hobbes finds three causes of quarrel: competition for gain, distrust (which makes it rational to strike first) and glory. The result is a condition of war "of every man against every man." War here means not constant fighting but the known readiness to fight. In such a condition there is no industry, no farming, no arts, no society,

> and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

Nothing is unjust in this condition, because where there is no common power there is no law. Everyone has a **right of nature** to do whatever they judge necessary to preserve themselves. See [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]].

### The way out

Reason suggests rules for escaping, which Hobbes calls **laws of nature**. The first is to seek peace where it can be had. The second is to be willing, when others are too, to lay down the right to all things. The third is to keep covenants. But "covenants, without the sword, are but words." Promises need an enforcer.

So each person agrees with every other to authorise one man or assembly to act for them all, "as if every man should say to every man, I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man, or to this assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy right to him." The multitude so united is a commonwealth: "that great Leviathan," a mortal god.

### The sovereign

Three features of this contract give Hobbes his conclusions.

- The contract is among the subjects, not between subjects and ruler. The sovereign promises nothing and so cannot break the agreement.
- The subjects have authorised whatever the sovereign does, so they cannot justly accuse it of injury.
- Power must be **undivided**. Splitting it between king and parliament, or between state and church, is what caused the Civil War.

The sovereign makes the law, judges disputes, controls doctrine and decides what is taught. Hobbes prefers monarchy but says the argument works for an assembly too. See [[ideas/sovereignty|sovereignty]].

Subjects keep one thing. Because the whole point of the contract is self-preservation, no one can be obliged to kill themselves or to submit quietly to being killed. And the duty to obey lasts only as long as the sovereign can actually protect. For the rest, liberty lies in "the silence of the law": whatever the sovereign has not forbidden.

## Criticism and influence

[[thinkers/locke|Locke]] gave the classic reply: to escape the dangers of the state of nature by submitting to an absolute ruler is to avoid "polecats or foxes" only to be "devoured by lions." Others have doubted that people as fearful as Hobbes's could ever trust each other enough to make the first agreement.

Yet nearly all later theory takes over his starting points. Individuals come first, they are equal, and authority must be justified to them. In that sense Hobbes, the defender of absolutism, is also an ancestor of [[traditions/liberalism|liberalism]]. Modern game theory restates his state of nature as a prisoner's dilemma, and writers on international relations describe a world with no world government as Hobbesian. Compare him with his successors in [[debates/hobbes-locke-rousseau|Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau]].

## Where to start

*Leviathan*, chapters 13 to 15 (the state of nature and the laws of nature), 17 and 18 (the commonwealth and the rights of sovereigns) and 21 (the liberty of subjects). The seventeenth-century English is vivid and worth reading in the original.
