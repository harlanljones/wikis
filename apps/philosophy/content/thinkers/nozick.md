# Robert Nozick

Robert Nozick was the American philosopher who gave libertarianism its most powerful statement. In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* he argued that individuals have rights so strong that only a minimal state, limited to protecting people against force, theft and fraud, can be justified.

## Life

Nozick was born in Brooklyn in 1938, the son of a Russian Jewish immigrant. As a student at Columbia he was a socialist. Arguments with libertarian friends in graduate school at Princeton changed his mind, to his own annoyance. He became a full professor at Harvard at thirty.

*Anarchy, State, and Utopia* appeared in 1974, three years after Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*, and won the National Book Award. It is witty, fast and full of puzzles, and it made him famous. He never wrote another book of political philosophy. He declined to reply to critics, saying he did not want to spend his life writing "The Son of Anarchy, State, and Utopia," and turned to knowledge, personal identity, free will and the meaning of life. He died of stomach cancer in 2002, the same year as Rawls.

## The main ideas

### Rights as side constraints

The book's first sentence states its premise: "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)."

These rights are not goals to be promoted, so that one violation could be justified by preventing two. They are **side constraints** on action. The reason is [[thinkers/kant|Kantian]]. People are ends and not merely means. There is no social entity with a good of its own that could justify sacrificing one person for others. "There are only individual people, different individual people, with their own individual lives."

Nozick assumes these rights and does not derive them, which critics have called "libertarianism without foundations." They are broadly those of [[thinkers/locke|Locke's]] state of nature: rights over one's own person and to property justly acquired.

### From anarchy to the minimal state

Part I answers the anarchist, who says that any state violates rights. Starting from a Lockean [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]], Nozick argues that people would hire protection agencies, that one agency would come to dominate in each territory, and that it could become a state by extending protection to everyone, without anyone's rights being violated. The **minimal state**, the "night-watchman state" of classical liberalism, is legitimate.

### The entitlement theory

Part II argues that nothing beyond the minimal state is legitimate. Its core is a theory of justice in holdings with three parts.

1. **Justice in acquisition.** How unowned things may come to be owned.
2. **Justice in transfer.** How holdings may pass from one person to another: by gift, sale or bequest, freely agreed.
3. **Rectification.** How past injustices in the first two are to be corrected.

A distribution is just if everyone is entitled to what they hold under these principles. The theory is **historical**. Justice depends on how a distribution came about, not on its shape. Nozick contrasts it with **patterned** theories, which say goods should be distributed according to need, merit, usefulness or, as in Rawls, the benefit of the worst off.

### Wilt Chamberlain

His best-known argument is aimed at every patterned theory. Suppose goods are distributed according to your favourite pattern, call it D1. Now the basketball star Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract giving him twenty-five cents from each ticket sold. A million fans happily pay to watch him. At the end of the season he has $250,000, far more than anyone else. Call the new distribution D2.

Is D2 unjust? Everyone was entitled to their share under D1, and each chose to give a quarter to Chamberlain. If D1 was just and people moved from it voluntarily, how can the result be unjust? The lesson Nozick draws is that "liberty upsets patterns." To keep any pattern in place a state would have to forbid "capitalist acts between consenting adults," or keep taking from some what others chose to give them.

### Taxation

The conclusion follows. "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor." Taking the earnings of some hours of a person's work is like forcing them to work those hours for someone else. It gives others a partial property right in that person. Redistribution, however well meant, violates self-ownership.

### A framework for utopia

Part III argues that the minimal state is also inspiring. No single way of life suits everyone. Nozick lists dozens of people from Wittgenstein to Elizabeth Taylor and asks whether there is one kind of life best for all of them. The minimal state is a framework within which people may form whatever communities they like, including socialist or religious ones, as long as joining and leaving are voluntary.

### The experience machine

A thought experiment from the book has had a life of its own. Suppose a machine could give you any experience you wanted, so that you would think and feel you were writing a great novel or making a friend, while floating in a tank. Would you plug in for life? Most people say no. That suggests we care about more than how our lives feel from the inside, and it is a standard objection to the hedonist form of [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarianism]].

## Criticism and influence

- **Acquisition.** The whole theory rests on how property first becomes privately owned, and Nozick's treatment is brief. He adopts a weak version of [[thinkers/locke|Locke's]] proviso: taking something is permitted if no one is made worse off. Critics such as G. A. Cohen argue this ignores what others could have done with the resource.
- **History.** Actual holdings come from centuries of conquest, slavery and fraud. Nozick admits that rectification might call for large transfers and even says a patterned principle could serve as a rough rule of thumb for it. That concession gives away a great deal in practice.
- **Chamberlain.** Rawlsians reply that their principles apply to the rules of the basic structure, not to individual transactions, and that the fans did not agree to the long-run effects of concentrated wealth on everyone's opportunities.
- **Self-ownership.** Many deny that owning oneself implies full ownership of what one earns within a system of social cooperation.

In *The Examined Life* (1989) Nozick wrote that the libertarian position he had once put forward now seemed to him "seriously inadequate," partly because it left no room for the symbolic importance of acting together through government. He later said he had not abandoned it altogether.

The book made libertarianism a position academic philosophers had to answer. See [[debates/rawls-vs-nozick|Rawls vs Nozick]] and [[ideas/property|property]].
