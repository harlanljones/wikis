# Adam Smith

Adam Smith was the Scottish moral philosopher who explained how a society of people pursuing their own interests could produce order and wealth without anyone directing it. He is remembered as the founder of economics, but he saw himself as a moral philosopher, and his defence of markets came with a sharp eye for their costs.

## Life

Smith was born in 1723 in Kirkcaldy, a small port across the water from Edinburgh. His father died before he was born. He studied at Glasgow and Oxford, and in 1752 became Professor of Moral Philosophy at Glasgow. *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* made his name. In 1764 he resigned to travel in France as tutor to a young duke, and there he met Voltaire and the French economists known as the Physiocrats.

Back in Kirkcaldy he spent about ten years writing *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, published in 1776. He ended his career, with some irony, as a Commissioner of Customs in Edinburgh. He was the closest friend of [[thinkers/hume|David Hume]]. Shortly before his death in 1790 he had most of his unpublished papers burned.

## The main ideas

### Sympathy and the impartial spectator

*The Theory of Moral Sentiments* asks how creatures who care most about themselves come to make moral judgements. Smith's answer is **sympathy**, by which he means our ability to imagine ourselves in another person's place and feel something of what they feel. We approve of feelings we can go along with.

We also learn to see ourselves as others see us. Over time we form an idea of an **impartial spectator**, a well-informed observer with no stake in the matter, and we judge our own conduct by asking what that figure would approve. Conscience is this "man within the breast." People want not only to be praised but to be worthy of praise.

### The division of labour

*The Wealth of Nations* opens in a pin factory. One worker alone could hardly make twenty pins in a day. Ten workers, splitting the job into about eighteen steps, make some forty-eight thousand. Wealth comes from this division of labour, and the division of labour grows as markets widen.

It arises from a human "propensity to truck, barter, and exchange." We do not depend on the kindness of others for our dinner:

> It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.

### The invisible hand

Smith uses the phrase "invisible hand" only once in *The Wealth of Nations*. A merchant who invests at home for his own security and gain is "led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention," the wealth of his society. The broader idea is that prices and competition steer labour and capital to where they are most wanted, with no central direction. [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] later developed this into the theory of spontaneous order.

Smith's target was the mercantile system of tariffs, monopolies and colonial trade rules, which he saw as a conspiracy of merchants against the public. Sweep it away and "the obvious and simple system of natural liberty establishes itself of its own accord."

### What government should do

Under natural liberty the sovereign has three duties: defence, the administration of justice, and "erecting and maintaining certain public works and certain public institutions" that no private person would profit from providing, such as roads, bridges and schools. See [[traditions/libertarianism|libertarianism]] for those who would stop there, and [[debates/markets-vs-planning|markets vs planning]] for the later argument.

### The costs of commerce

Smith was no simple cheerleader for business. Merchants and manufacturers, he warned, have an interest "to deceive and even to oppress the public," and "people of the same trade seldom meet together" without the talk ending in "a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices."

He also saw what repetitive work does to people. A man who spends his life on a few simple operations "generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become," unless government takes pains to prevent it through public education. This passage anticipates [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] on alienation.

And he favoured high wages. "No society can surely be flourishing and happy, of which the far greater part of the members are poor and miserable."

## Criticism and influence

German scholars once posed "the Adam Smith problem": how could the author of a book on sympathy write a book on self-interest? Most now think there is no contradiction. Markets work within a framework of justice and moral feeling that the first book describes.

[[thinkers/marx|Marx]] took over Smith's labour theory of value and turned it against capitalism. Free-market [[traditions/liberalism|liberals]] and [[traditions/libertarianism|libertarians]] claim Smith as a founder, and so do egalitarian liberals who point to his concern for workers. [[thinkers/sen|Amartya Sen]] has drawn heavily on the impartial spectator.

## Where to start

*The Wealth of Nations*, Book I, chapters 1 to 3 (division of labour), Book IV, chapter 2 (the invisible hand) and Book V, chapter 1 (the duties of government and the effects of factory work). *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part I and Part III.
