# Cicero

Marcus Tullius Cicero was a Roman statesman, lawyer and writer who carried Greek political philosophy into Latin and into the service of a republic. He argued that a commonwealth is the people's shared property, held together by law, and that above every city's law stands a natural law that binds all human beings.

## Life

Cicero was born in Arpinum, a hill town south-east of Rome, to a family that had never held high office. He rose by his skill as a courtroom speaker and became consul in 63 BC, the top office of the Roman Republic. That year he exposed and crushed the conspiracy of Catiline, and had several conspirators executed without trial, an act for which he was later exiled.

He lived through the collapse of the Republic: the alliance of Pompey, Crassus and Caesar, the civil war, and Caesar's dictatorship. Pushed out of politics, he wrote philosophy. After Caesar's assassination in 44 BC he returned to attack Mark Antony in a series of speeches called the *Philippics*. When Antony, Octavian and Lepidus took power together, Cicero's name went on their list of enemies. He was killed in December 43 BC, and his head and hands were displayed in the Forum.

## The main ideas

### The commonwealth belongs to the people

In *On the Republic*, written in the late 50s BC, Cicero defines a commonwealth (*res publica*) as *res populi*, "the property of the people." A people, he adds, is not any crowd gathered together. It is a multitude joined by agreement about justice and by partnership for the common good. Where a tyrant or a faction treats the state as its own, there is strictly no commonwealth at all.

### The mixed constitution

Following the Greek historian Polybius, Cicero held that each simple form of government decays. Kingship turns into tyranny, aristocracy into the rule of a clique, democracy into mob rule. The stable solution is to blend them. Rome, on his account, combined royal power in the consuls, the authority of the best men in the Senate and the liberty of the people in the assemblies and tribunes. This idea of balance passed to [[thinkers/machiavelli|Machiavelli]], [[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] and the American founders. See [[ideas/separation-of-powers|separation of powers]].

### Natural law

Cicero's most quoted passage comes from Book III of *On the Republic*:

> True law is right reason in agreement with nature. It is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting.

It goes on to say that this law cannot be repealed by Senate or people, and that there will not be one law at Rome and another at Athens. The idea came from the Stoics, who taught that the universe is ordered by reason and that all humans share in it. In Cicero's hands it became a standard for judging the laws of any state, and a basis for the claim that all people share a common nature. [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]] and [[thinkers/locke|Locke]] built on it. See [[ideas/natural-law|natural law]].

### Duties

*On Duties*, written in 44 BC as a letter to his son, asks what to do when what is honourable and what is useful seem to conflict. Cicero's answer is that they never truly do: nothing dishonourable is really useful. The book praises the active life of public service above private withdrawal and says that force and fraud are the methods of the lion and the fox, both unworthy of a human being. [[thinkers/machiavelli|Machiavelli]] later took that image and turned it upside down.

## Criticism and influence

Cicero was not an original philosopher and did not claim to be. His republic was run by a propertied elite, and he distrusted popular reformers. His own execution of citizens without trial sits badly with his praise of law.

His importance lies in what he passed on. For over a thousand years, educated Europeans learned their political vocabulary from him: republic, liberty, constitution, duty. *On Duties* was among the first classical texts printed after the invention of the press. Most of *On the Republic* was lost until a large part was rediscovered in the Vatican Library in 1819, so earlier readers knew it mainly through quotations in [[thinkers/augustine|Augustine]] and others.

## Where to start

*On Duties* is the most readable. From *On the Republic*, read Book I (forms of government) and the surviving parts of Book III (natural law), then the "Dream of Scipio" that ends Book VI.
