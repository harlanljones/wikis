# Martha Nussbaum

Martha Nussbaum is the American philosopher who turned the capabilities approach into a theory of basic justice with a definite list of what every person is owed. She has also argued, across many books, that emotions are forms of judgement and that a just society has to pay attention to them.

## Life

Nussbaum was born in New York in 1947 and grew up in a wealthy suburb of Philadelphia, in a world she has described as sheltered and full of prejudice. She studied theatre and classics, briefly worked as an actor, and took her doctorate in classical philosophy at Harvard, where she has said she met discrimination as a woman and a mother. She converted to Judaism when she married.

After teaching at Harvard and Brown she moved in 1995 to the University of Chicago, where she holds appointments in law and philosophy. In the late 1980s she worked with [[thinkers/sen|Amartya Sen]] at a United Nations research institute in Helsinki, which turned her interest in Aristotle towards questions of global development. She has written more than twenty books and has received the Kyoto Prize and the Berggruen Prize, among other honours.

## The main ideas

### Luck and the good life

Her first major book, *The Fragility of Goodness*, studies Greek tragedy and philosophy. The tragic poets saw that a good life is vulnerable. A person can be ruined by things outside their control, and can face conflicts in which every choice does wrong. [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] tried to make the good life safe from luck by locating it in reason alone. [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]], whom Nussbaum prefers, accepted that a full human life needs friends, family, a city and good fortune, and is therefore open to loss.

This picture of human beings as needy, attached and vulnerable, and no less dignified for it, runs through everything she has written since.

### The central capabilities

[[thinkers/sen|Sen]] proposed capabilities, what people are really able to do and be, as the right measure of how well a life is going. He refused to fix a list. Nussbaum argues that a theory of justice must say what citizens are entitled to, and she offers a list of ten **central capabilities**.

1. **Life.** Living a life of normal length.
2. **Bodily health,** including nourishment and shelter.
3. **Bodily integrity.** Moving freely, being safe from assault, having choice in sex and reproduction.
4. **Senses, imagination and thought,** developed by education and protected by freedom of expression and religion.
5. **Emotions.** Being able to love, grieve and feel justified anger, without being crippled by fear.
6. **Practical reason.** Forming an idea of the good and planning one's own life.
7. **Affiliation.** Living with and for others, and having the social bases of self-respect.
8. **Other species.** Living with concern for animals, plants and nature.
9. **Play.**
10. **Control over one's environment,** both political (taking part in choices that govern one's life) and material (holding property and seeking work on equal terms).

Each is needed for a life worthy of **human dignity**. They cannot be traded against each other: a shortfall in one is not made good by plenty of another. A just society brings every citizen up to a threshold in each. The list is meant as a basis for constitutional guarantees, and it is deliberately general so that each nation can specify it in its own way.

The goal is capability, not functioning. The state should make sure people are able to do these things and leave them free to choose. A person may fast for religious reasons, but must have the food.

Nussbaum presents the list in the spirit of [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls's]] political liberalism, as something people of different religions and philosophies can endorse for political purposes without agreeing on the meaning of life.

### Adaptive preferences

*Women and Human Development* draws on her work with poor women in India. A woman who has always been told that she eats last and needs no schooling may sincerely say she is content. This is the problem of **adaptive preferences**, and it is why Nussbaum rejects measures of justice based on satisfaction. It is also her answer to the charge that universal standards are a form of Western arrogance. To defer to "culture" is often to defer to the most powerful voices within it.

### The frontiers of the social contract

*Frontiers of Justice* argues that the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] tradition, up to and including Rawls, has a built-in limit. It imagines justice as an agreement for mutual advantage among parties who are roughly equal in power and ability. That leaves three groups outside.

- **People with disabilities,** including severe cognitive disabilities, who may not be able to "contribute" in the usual sense.
- **People in other nations,** since the contract is made within a state.
- **Non-human animals.**

The capabilities approach starts from dignity and need instead of from a bargain, and so, she argues, can do justice to all three. Her later book *Justice for Animals* carries the argument through: each sentient creature is entitled to live the kind of life characteristic of its species.

### Emotions in public life

In *Upheavals of Thought* Nussbaum defends a view she takes from the Stoics: emotions are not blind surges but judgements about what matters to us. Fear says that something I value is under threat. Grief says that it is lost.

If so, emotions can be examined, and some are better guides to law and politics than others. She argues that **disgust** and **shame** are unreliable grounds for law, since they express a wish to deny our own animal bodies and have been used against Jews, women, lower castes and gay people. She has criticised retributive **anger**. And she argues that liberal societies need to cultivate **compassion** and a critical kind of patriotism, through art, education and public ritual, if their commitment to justice is to last. She has also written a much-read defence of the humanities as necessary for democratic citizenship.

## Criticism and influence

The first objection is to the list itself. Who is one philosopher to say what all humans need? Nussbaum replies that the list is open to revision, comes out of long cross-cultural discussion, and protects choices instead of dictating them. [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] remains unpersuaded that a fixed list is wise.

Others doubt that her theory is as neutral between ways of life as she claims, since it rests on a substantial idea of human flourishing. Some ask how the thresholds are to be set and what happens when capabilities conflict.

Her version of the capabilities approach is widely used in development ethics, disability studies and animal ethics, and she is one of the most widely read philosophers now writing.

## Where to start

*Creating Capabilities* is a short introduction written for general readers. Then *Frontiers of Justice*, chapter 1.
