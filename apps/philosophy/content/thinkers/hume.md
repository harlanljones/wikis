# David Hume

David Hume was the Scottish philosopher and historian who applied a cool scepticism to morals and politics. He denied that government rests on a social contract, argued that justice is a human convention that exists because it is useful, and held that all authority depends in the end on opinion.

## Life

Hume was born in Edinburgh in 1711 and wrote his masterpiece, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, in his twenties. It "fell dead-born from the press," as he put it, and he rewrote its ideas in shorter, more polished books. His reputation as a religious sceptic twice cost him a university chair. He made his living instead as a librarian, a diplomat in Paris, where he was celebrated in the salons, and above all as the author of a six-volume *History of England* that made him rich.

He was a close friend of [[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]], and for a few months the host of [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]], who ended by accusing him of a plot. He died in Edinburgh in 1776, calm and cheerful to the last, to the amazement of those who thought an unbeliever must fear death.

## The main ideas

### Reason and passion

Hume's moral philosophy starts from a claim about motivation. Reason alone never moves anyone to act. It tells us about facts and about means to ends, but the ends are set by feeling. "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions." Moral judgements are therefore not conclusions of reason. They express the approval or disapproval we feel, through sympathy with others, when we look at a character from a general point of view.

He also noticed that writers on morals slide from statements about what **is** to claims about what **ought** to be without explaining the step. This "is and ought" gap has troubled [[ideas/natural-law|natural law]] theories ever since. See [[ideas/ethical-theories|ethical theories]].

### Justice as a convention

Some virtues, such as kindness to one's children, are natural. Justice, by which Hume chiefly means respect for property and promises, is **artificial**. It is a set of rules that humans invented.

The rules arise because of our circumstances. Goods are scarce but not hopelessly so, and people are selfish but not entirely. If there were abundance of everything, or if everyone loved everyone else as themselves, rules of property would be pointless. In our middle condition they are essential. They grow up gradually, without any promise, in the way two men rowing a boat fall into rhythm, or the way languages and money develop. We approve of them because we see that they are useful to everyone. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] later borrowed these "circumstances of justice" directly.

### Against the original contract

In the essay "Of the Original Contract" (1748), Hume takes apart [[thinkers/locke|Locke's]] theory of consent. Almost every government in history began in conquest or usurpation, not agreement. And ordinary people do not consent by staying where they are:

> Can we seriously say, that a poor peasant or artisan has a free choice to leave his country, when he knows no foreign language or manners, and lives from day to day, by the small wages which he acquires?

It is like saying a man carried aboard a ship while asleep consents to the captain's rule because he does not jump into the sea. Besides, the theory explains nothing. If we must obey government because we promised, why must we keep promises? Because society could not survive otherwise. But that is exactly the reason to obey government, so the promise is an idle step. See [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] and [[ideas/authority-and-obligation|authority and obligation]].

### Government rests on opinion

Nothing is more surprising, Hume says, than how easily the many are governed by the few. Since force is always on the side of the governed, rulers have nothing to support them but opinion: the general belief that the government is useful and that it has a right to rule. This holds for the most despotic regimes as well as the freest.

### Faction and constitutional design

Hume distrusted parties founded on abstract principle and religious "enthusiasm". He thought good institutions matter more than good men, and that in designing a constitution "every man ought to be supposed a knave." His essay "Idea of a Perfect Commonwealth" argued that a large republic, with elections filtered through several levels, could be more stable than a small one. [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] made that argument famous in *Federalist* No. 10.

## Criticism and influence

Hume's respect for custom and his suspicion of schemes drawn from pure reason make him a source for [[traditions/conservatism|conservatism]], though his religious scepticism separates him from [[thinkers/burke|Burke]]. His appeal to usefulness prepared the way for [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]]. [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] said that Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber," and then set out to refute him.

Hume added a racist footnote to his essay "Of National Characters", claiming the natural inferiority of non-white peoples. It was attacked in his own day and is now a standard example of Enlightenment prejudice.

## Where to start

The essays are the easiest way in: "Of the First Principles of Government", "Of the Original Contract" and "Of Parties in General". Then *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, section 3, on justice.
