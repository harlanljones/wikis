# Jeremy Bentham

Jeremy Bentham was the English legal reformer who founded utilitarianism. He held that the only test of a law or an action is whether it adds to the sum of human happiness, and he used that test to attack almost every institution of his day.

## Life

Bentham was born in London in 1748, a child prodigy who went to Oxford at twelve and trained as a lawyer. He never practised. The English law disgusted him as a heap of fictions, technicalities and judge-made rules serving the lawyers who administered it, and he gave his life to replacing it with rational codes. He wrote some pages every day for sixty years and published little of it himself. His papers are still being edited.

He spent years and much of his fortune on the Panopticon, a design for a circular prison, which the government finally rejected. That failure convinced him that rulers serve their own "sinister interests," and in later life he became a radical democrat. He gathered followers, including James Mill, father of [[thinkers/mill|John Stuart Mill]]. By his own instructions his body was dissected and preserved as an "auto-icon," which is still on display at University College London.

## The main ideas

### The principle of utility

Bentham's main work opens with a statement of psychology and ethics together:

> Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do.

The **principle of utility** approves or disapproves of every action according to whether it tends to increase or reduce the happiness of those affected. The interest of the community is nothing but the sum of the interests of its members. Any other standard, such as moral sense, natural law or the will of God, is on his view either utility in disguise or the speaker's own likes and dislikes dressed up as principle.

Pleasures and pains can in principle be measured, by their intensity, duration, certainty, nearness and so on. This "felicific calculus" was meant to make legislation a science. All pleasures count alike. In a much-quoted line, he said that the game of push-pin is of equal value with poetry if it gives equal pleasure. See [[ideas/ethical-theories|ethical theories]].

### Law as command

In *A Fragment on Government*, an attack on the jurist William Blackstone, Bentham separated two tasks: describing what the law **is** and judging what it **ought** to be. A law is a command of a sovereign backed by a sanction. Whether it is a good law is a further question, to be settled by utility. This separation is the root of legal positivism.

It also disposes of the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]]. People obey government when and because the probable mischief of obedience is less than the probable mischief of resistance. No fictional promise is needed.

### Nonsense upon stilts

For the same reason Bentham rejected natural rights. Rights are creatures of law. Where there is no law there are no rights, only reasons for wishing there were. His commentary on the French Declaration of 1789 is blunt: "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts." Such talk, he thought, is not only confused but dangerous, since it invites everyone to resist any law they dislike. See [[ideas/rights|rights]].

### Reform

Applied with consistency, utility was a radical tool. Punishment is itself an evil and is justified only when it prevents a greater one, so penalties should be no harsher than needed to deter. Bentham argued for abolishing most uses of the death penalty, for the reform of prisons and the poor law, for free trade, for the secret ballot and near-universal suffrage, and for the separation of church and state. In writings unpublished in his lifetime he argued that sexual acts between men harm no one and should not be crimes.

He also widened the circle of those who count. Of animals he wrote: "The question is not, Can they *reason*? nor, Can they *talk*? but, Can they *suffer*?"

### The Panopticon

The Panopticon was a ring of cells around a central inspection tower. Prisoners could be watched at any time without knowing when, and so would behave as if always watched. Bentham thought the plan humane and cheap, and suitable for schools, factories and hospitals too. [[thinkers/foucault|Foucault]] later made it the emblem of modern disciplinary power.

## Criticism and influence

The classic objections to utilitarianism were first aimed at Bentham. If only the total counts, the happiness of the many may justify sacrificing the few. A calculus that treats all pleasures alike was mocked as a doctrine "worthy only of swine," and [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] revised it by ranking pleasures by quality. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] argued that utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons."

In practice Bentham's influence was vast. The legal and administrative reforms of nineteenth-century Britain owe much to his followers, and cost-benefit analysis and welfare economics are his descendants. See [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarianism]].

## Where to start

*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, chapters 1 to 4, and the short preface to *A Fragment on Government*.
