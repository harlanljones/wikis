# Friedrich Hayek

Friedrich Hayek was the Austrian-born economist and philosopher who argued that no planner can ever have the knowledge needed to run an economy, and that a free society depends on general rules that leave people to use what they know. He was the leading intellectual opponent of socialism in the twentieth century.

## Life

Hayek was born in Vienna in 1899 into a family of scholars. He served in the Austro-Hungarian army in the First World War and came home, like many of his generation, a mild socialist. The economist Ludwig von Mises talked him out of it. In 1931 he moved to the London School of Economics, where he became the main rival of Keynes in the debates over the Great Depression, and by general opinion lost.

*The Road to Serfdom*, written during the war, made him famous, especially in America, where a magazine condensation reached millions. In 1947 he gathered liberal economists and philosophers at Mont Pèlerin in Switzerland to found a society that still bears the name. He taught at Chicago from 1950 and later at Freiburg. In 1974 he shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics with the Swedish social democrat Gunnar Myrdal. Margaret Thatcher and the reformers of post-communist Europe cited him as a guide. He died in Freiburg in 1992.

## The main ideas

### The knowledge problem

Hayek's central argument is in a short 1945 article. The economic problem of society is not how to allocate known resources. It is how to use knowledge that no single mind possesses. The facts that matter, such as who needs what, what is available where and what a machine could be used for instead, are scattered among millions of people. Much of it is knowledge of "the particular circumstances of time and place," and some of it cannot even be put into words.

Prices solve the problem. When tin becomes scarce its price rises, and users everywhere economise without needing to know why. The price system is a kind of telecommunications network. A central planner, however clever and well meaning, cannot gather the knowledge that prices carry, and by suppressing the market destroys it. See [[debates/markets-vs-planning|markets vs planning]].

### The road to serfdom

*The Road to Serfdom* turns this into a political warning. Comprehensive economic planning requires agreement on a complete ranking of ends, which a free people never has. Parliament cannot run a plan, so power passes to experts and then to a strong leader. And since control of economic life is control of the means to all our ends, the planner ends by controlling everything. Hayek argued that Nazism was not a reaction against the socialist trends of the previous decades but their outcome, and dedicated the book "to the socialists of all parties."

He was not arguing against all government action. The book accepts a guaranteed minimum income, social insurance and regulation of working conditions. His target was central direction of the economy.

### Spontaneous order

Hayek separates two kinds of order. An **organisation**, such as an army or a firm, is designed for a purpose. A **spontaneous order**, such as language, money, common law or the market, is "the result of human action but not of human design." It has no purpose of its own. It grows from people following rules, and it can handle far more complexity than any design.

The error he calls **constructivist rationalism** is to assume that every useful institution must have been invented, and so can be reinvented at will. This sounds like [[thinkers/burke|Burke]], and Hayek admired him. But in an essay titled "Why I Am Not a Conservative" he explained that he welcomed change and distrusted conservatism's fondness for authority.

### Liberty and the rule of law

*The Constitution of Liberty* defines freedom as the absence of **coercion**, meaning subjection to the arbitrary will of another. Law does not reduce freedom when it consists of general, abstract rules, known in advance and applying equally to all, including those who make them. Under such rules a person can plan their own life. What threatens liberty is discretion: commands aimed at particular people for particular ends. See [[ideas/liberty|liberty]].

### The mirage of social justice

In his last major work Hayek argued that "social justice" is a meaningless phrase. Justice is a quality of human conduct. The distribution of incomes in a market is not anyone's act. It is the unintended result of millions of choices, and so it can be neither just nor unjust, any more than the weather. To make incomes match some pattern of desert, government would have to treat people unequally and direct their lives. See [[ideas/justice|justice]].

## Criticism and influence

Critics reply that the democracies built large welfare states and mixed economies after 1945 without sliding into tyranny, which tells against the slippery slope of *The Road to Serfdom*. They add that a market's results are shaped by rules of property and contract that people do choose, so we are responsible for them. Rawls's theory is aimed at exactly those background rules. Hayek's defence of the market also depends on results, which sits uneasily with his claim that no one can judge them.

His public remarks defending General Pinochet's dictatorship in Chile, where he said he preferred a liberal dictator to a democracy without liberalism, damaged his reputation.

His knowledge argument, though, has been absorbed across the political spectrum. Even market socialists accept that prices carry information no planner has.

## Where to start

"The Use of Knowledge in Society" is fifteen pages and is the key. Then *The Road to Serfdom*, and "Why I Am Not a Conservative."
