# Immanuel Kant

Immanuel Kant was the German philosopher who grounded morality in reason and in the freedom of each person to give law to themselves. From that starting point he argued that human beings must always be treated as ends and never merely as means, and that states should be republics joined in a peaceful federation.

## Life

Kant was born in 1724 in Königsberg, a Baltic port then in East Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia), the son of a harness-maker. He never travelled far from the city. He studied and then taught at its university for decades, lecturing on everything from logic to geography, and his daily walk was said to be so regular that neighbours set their clocks by it.

He published his major works late. The *Critique of Pure Reason* appeared when he was fifty-seven, and the moral and political writings followed in his sixties and seventies. He welcomed the French Revolution in principle while rejecting any right of rebellion. In 1794 the Prussian government ordered him to stop writing on religion. He died in 1804.

## The main ideas

### Enlightenment

Kant's 1784 essay "What Is Enlightenment?" defines it as "man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity," the inability to use one's own understanding without another's guidance. Its motto is *Sapere aude*: dare to know. What enlightenment needs is freedom to make "public use of one's reason," which means the freedom to argue in print before the reading public.

### The moral law

The *Groundwork* begins from the thought that the only thing good without qualification is a **good will**. An act has moral worth when it is done from duty, not from inclination or for the sake of results. And duty is given by a single principle that reason imposes on itself, the **categorical imperative**. Kant states it in several ways that he considers equivalent.

- **Universal law.** "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." If I make a false promise to get a loan, I act on a rule that could not be everyone's rule, since promising would collapse.
- **Humanity.** "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means, but always at the same time as an end."
- **Autonomy.** Act as a member of a "kingdom of ends" whose laws you make together with all other rational beings.

A being who can act on self-given law has **dignity**, a worth beyond any price. This is the philosophical source of the modern idea of human dignity, and of the objection that [[traditions/utilitarianism|utilitarianism]] treats people as mere containers of happiness. See [[ideas/ethical-theories|ethical theories]].

### Right and the state

Kant's political theory is in the "Doctrine of Right," the first half of *The Metaphysics of Morals*. Right concerns only outward actions. Its universal principle is that an action is right if it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law. Each person has one innate right: **freedom**, meaning independence from being constrained by another's choice.

In a [[ideas/state-of-nature|state of nature]] rights would be insecure and disputed, so there is a duty to enter a civil condition. Kant treats the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] not as an event but as an "idea of reason," a test for laws: a law is just only if a whole people could have agreed to it. A rightful state is a **republic**, with representative government, separated powers and citizens who are free, equal before the law and independent.

Kant denies any right of revolution. To resist the supreme authority is to destroy the legal order that makes right possible. People may criticise and complain, and rulers have a duty to reform, but not more. His citizens are also limited to those who are economically independent, which excluded servants, labourers and all women.

### Perpetual peace

*Toward Perpetual Peace* is written in the form of a treaty. Its three "definitive articles" are:

1. The constitution of every state shall be republican. Where those who pay for and fight wars must consent to them, wars will be rarer.
2. The right of nations shall be based on a federation of free states, a league for peace and not a world government.
3. Cosmopolitan right shall be limited to "universal hospitality": a stranger has the right not to be treated as an enemy on arrival. Kant used this article to condemn European conquest abroad.

He adds that trade binds nations together, and that any policy which could not be made public without defeating itself is unjust. The modern "democratic peace" thesis, the League of Nations and the United Nations all look back to this essay.

## Criticism and influence

[[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]] charged that the categorical imperative is an empty form that can yield no specific duties without assuming existing institutions. Critics also point to the rigour of Kant's rules, as in his claim that one may not lie even to a murderer asking where a friend is hiding. And his lectures on anthropology and geography contain a racial hierarchy at odds with his own universalism.

His influence on political philosophy is nonetheless hard to match. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] called his theory of justice Kantian, [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]] rebuilt Kant's ethics as a theory of dialogue, and human rights law speaks in Kant's language of dignity.

## Where to start

"What Is Enlightenment?" and *Perpetual Peace* are short and readable. The *Groundwork*, sections 1 and 2, is hard but brief. Leave the "Doctrine of Right" for later.
