# Jürgen Habermas

Jürgen Habermas was the German philosopher who rebuilt the case for reason and democracy after the catastrophes of the twentieth century. He argued that the standards we need for criticising society are already present in everyday speech, and that a law is legitimate only if all those affected could agree to it in free and open discussion.

## Life

Habermas was born in Düsseldorf in 1929 and grew up in a small town near Cologne. He was born with a cleft palate and underwent repeated surgery as a child, which he later said made him aware of how deeply people depend on communication with others. Like other boys of his age he was in the Hitler Youth, and at fifteen he was sent to the western defences in the last months of the war. The Nuremberg trials and the films of the concentration camps were the shock that formed him. His generation, he said, saw that they had been living under a criminal regime.

In 1956 he became assistant to Theodor Adorno at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt. He taught at Heidelberg and Frankfurt and directed a Max Planck Institute at Starnberg, near Munich. For sixty years he was also Germany's best-known public intellectual, intervening on the student movement, the Nazi past, reunification, genetic engineering, religion and the European Union. At ninety he published a vast history of philosophy. He died at Starnberg on 14 March 2026, aged ninety-six.

## The main ideas

### The public sphere

His first book describes the rise of a new space in eighteenth-century Europe, in coffee houses, salons, clubs and newspapers, where private people came together to discuss public matters and hold the authorities to account. In this **public sphere**, at least in principle, status was set aside and arguments counted. It gave birth to the idea that government should answer to public opinion.

It also declined. With mass media, advertising and public relations, a public that debated became a public that consumes. Critics noted that the original public sphere excluded women and workers. The concept nonetheless became a basic tool for thinking about media and democracy.

### Communicative action

The older Frankfurt School, following [[thinkers/weber|Weber]], had come to see reason itself as a tool of domination. Habermas thought this a dead end, since it left the critic no ground to stand on. His reply was to distinguish two kinds of rationality.

- **Instrumental** or strategic action aims at success. It treats the world, and other people, as things to be managed.
- **Communicative action** aims at mutual understanding. When I sincerely say something to you, I implicitly claim that it is true, that it is right, and that I mean it, and I undertake to give reasons if you ask.

These claims are built into ordinary speech. They point towards an ideal of discussion in which everyone may speak, no one is coerced, and agreement rests on reasons alone. No actual conversation reaches the ideal, but we assume it whenever we argue seriously, and we can measure real conditions against it. That is where Habermas finds his standard of criticism.

### System and lifeworld

Modern societies have two sides. The **lifeworld** is the shared background of meanings, relationships and identities, reproduced through communication. The **system** consists of the market and the state bureaucracy, which coordinate action through money and power without needing agreement. Systems are necessary. No complex society could run on discussion alone.

The trouble comes when they overreach, and the logic of money and administration invades family life, education, culture and public debate. Habermas calls this the **colonisation of the lifeworld**. It is his version of the worries of [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] and Weber, and it suggests the task: to defend and extend the areas of life governed by talk.

### Discourse ethics

Habermas restates [[thinkers/kant|Kant's]] moral theory in terms of dialogue. Kant asked each person to test, alone, whether their maxim could be a universal law. Habermas's principle is that only those norms are valid that could meet with the agreement of all those affected, as participants in a practical discourse. The test must actually be carried out, among real people.

### Deliberative democracy

*Between Facts and Norms* applies this to law and the state. Law has two faces. It is a fact, backed by force, and it claims to be right. In a modern society with no shared religion, that claim can be made good only by the democratic process that produces the law.

Human rights and popular sovereignty, which liberals and republicans have set against each other, each presuppose the other. Private freedoms are needed for citizens to take part as equals, and only citizens deliberating together can spell out what those freedoms are. Democracy is not just the counting of votes. Opinion forms in the informal public sphere of civil society and flows, through elections and parliaments, into law. See [[ideas/democracy|democracy]].

He applied this to his own country as **constitutional patriotism**. After Nazism, Germans could not base their loyalty on ethnic nationhood. They could base it on the principles of their democratic constitution. He hoped the European Union could go the same way.

### The exchange with Rawls

In 1995 Habermas and [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] debated in print. Habermas described it as a dispute within the family. Both wanted principles that free and equal citizens could accept. Habermas argued that Rawls's original position has the philosopher work out in advance what citizens should be left to settle in real debate.

## Criticism and influence

The common objection is that the theory is too rationalist and too hopeful. Real politics involves interests, emotion and unequal power, and disagreements that no amount of talk dissolves. Followers of [[thinkers/schmitt|Schmitt]] such as Chantal Mouffe say that consensus is a way of silencing conflict. [[thinkers/foucault|Foucault's]] admirers ask whether any discussion is free of power. Feminists such as Nancy Fraser and Seyla Benhabib have revised his account of the public sphere from within.

Habermas shaped the theory of deliberative democracy, which has moved from books into practice in citizens' assemblies.

## Where to start

Habermas is a difficult writer. Start with the essay "Three Normative Models of Democracy" and the short book *The Inclusion of the Other*, then *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*, which is the most readable of the major works.
