# Isaiah Berlin

Isaiah Berlin was the Russian-born British historian of ideas who separated two concepts of liberty and argued that the things people rightly value cannot all be had together. His liberalism rests on that claim: since there is no single right way to live, people must be left room to choose.

## Life

Berlin was born in 1909 in Riga, then in the Russian Empire, to a prosperous Jewish family of timber merchants. The family moved to Petrograd, where at the age of seven he watched both revolutions of 1917. He remembered seeing a policeman dragged away by a mob, and said it gave him a lifelong horror of political violence. They emigrated to England in 1921.

He spent almost his whole career at Oxford, apart from wartime service for the British government in New York, Washington and Moscow. In Leningrad in 1945 he spent a night in conversation with the poet Anna Akhmatova, a meeting that marked them both and for which she was punished. He gave up technical philosophy for the history of ideas, became a celebrated lecturer and broadcaster, and was the founding president of Wolfson College. He died in 1997.

Berlin wrote essays, not treatises, and his ideas come through portraits of other thinkers.

## The main ideas

### Two concepts of liberty

Berlin's 1958 inaugural lecture is probably the most discussed essay in twentieth-century political theory. It separates two questions.

| | Negative liberty | Positive liberty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The question | "What is the area within which I am left to do what I can, without interference by others?" | "Who or what is the source of control over what I do?" |
| In short | Freedom **from** | Freedom **to**, or self-mastery |
| Champions | [[thinkers/hobbes\|Hobbes]], [[thinkers/locke\|Locke]], Constant, [[thinkers/mill\|Mill]] | [[thinkers/rousseau\|Rousseau]], [[thinkers/kant\|Kant]], [[thinkers/hegel\|Hegel]], [[thinkers/marx\|Marx]] |

Both are real ideals. The wish to be one's own master is as deep as the wish not to be interfered with. But Berlin argued that positive liberty has a history of turning into its opposite.

The route runs like this. To be self-governed is to be ruled by my "true" or "higher" self, my reason, and not by my passions. That higher self is then identified with something larger than me: the nation, the class, the party, the march of history. Those who know what this true self requires may then coerce my actual, mistaken self, and call it liberation. I am "forced to be free." Berlin saw this pattern behind the Jacobins and behind twentieth-century communism.

He did not conclude that negative liberty is the only value. Freedom for the Oxford professor means little to the Egyptian peasant who needs medicine. Liberty is one good among several, and sometimes it should give way. What he insisted on was that a loss of liberty be called a loss, even when it is worth it, and not be redescribed as a higher kind of freedom. See [[ideas/liberty|liberty]].

### Value pluralism

Behind the lecture lies Berlin's deepest conviction. Western thought, from [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] to the Enlightenment and [[thinkers/marx|Marx]], assumed that all real questions have one true answer, that the answers can be found, and that they fit together in a single harmonious whole. Berlin thought this false.

Liberty and equality, justice and mercy, loyalty and impartiality, spontaneous feeling and efficient organisation are all real goods. They conflict, and there is no common scale on which to weigh them. A society cannot have all of them in full, and neither can a single life. "We are doomed to choose, and every choice may entail an irreparable loss."

This is **not relativism**. The values are objective and limited in number, part of what it is to be human, and we can understand cultures that rank them differently. But it rules out utopia. If a perfect society is not even coherent in idea, then anyone who believes they can build one will sacrifice living people to a mirage. Berlin liked to quote [[thinkers/kant|Kant]]: "Out of the crooked timber of humanity no straight thing was ever made."

Pluralism supports liberalism, on his account, because a society that leaves people room to choose among real goods respects the human condition as it is. See [[traditions/liberalism|liberalism]].

### The Counter-Enlightenment

Berlin spent much of his career studying the enemies of the Enlightenment: Vico, Herder, Hamann, the reactionary Joseph de Maistre. He did not share their politics. He thought they had seen something the Enlightenment missed: the variety of cultures, the need to belong, the force of nationalism.

### Hedgehogs and foxes

"The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing." Berlin used this line from a Greek poet to sort writers into those who relate everything to a single system and those who pursue many ends. His essay argues that Tolstoy was a fox who believed he ought to be a hedgehog.

## Criticism and influence

Critics have questioned the two concepts from several sides. Gerald MacCallum argued that all freedom has one structure: someone is free **from** something **to** do something. Charles Taylor replied that we cannot understand freedom without judging which desires matter, so some "positive" content is unavoidable. Republican theorists such as Philip Pettit add a third concept, freedom as not being under another's arbitrary power, which Berlin's scheme overlooks.

Others ask whether pluralism really supports liberalism. If values cannot be ranked, why should liberty have any special place?

Berlin's essay still organises the debate, and his warning against sacrificing the present to a perfect future is the heart of post-war liberalism.

## Where to start

"Two Concepts of Liberty," then "The Pursuit of the Ideal," a short late lecture that sums up his whole outlook.
