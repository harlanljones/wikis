# Mary Wollstonecraft

Mary Wollstonecraft was the English writer who applied the Enlightenment's own principles of reason and rights to women. *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* argued that women appear weaker and less rational than men only because they are educated to be, and it is the founding text of feminist political thought.

## Life

Wollstonecraft was born in London in 1759. Her father drank, wasted an inheritance and beat her mother, and she left home early to earn her own living in the few ways open to a woman: as a lady's companion, a schoolteacher and a governess. In 1787 she moved to London to live by writing, working for the radical publisher Joseph Johnson, whose circle included Thomas Paine, William Blake and William Godwin.

When [[thinkers/burke|Burke's]] *Reflections* appeared in November 1790, she published a reply within weeks, among the first of many. *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* followed in 1792. She went to Paris to see the Revolution, lived through the Terror, had a daughter with an American who abandoned her, and twice attempted suicide. In 1797 she married Godwin. She died that September, days after giving birth to a second daughter, who grew up to write *Frankenstein* as Mary Shelley.

## The main ideas

### The reply to Burke

*A Vindication of the Rights of Men* attacks Burke for confusing sentiment with justice. His reverence for inheritance, she argues, is reverence for property and rank, and his tears for Marie Antoinette show no feeling for the poor. Rights belong to people as rational beings and cannot be lost because an injustice is old.

### Reason, virtue and education

The second *Vindication* starts from a simple premise. What raises humans above animals is reason, and virtue comes from using it. If women have souls and reason, as no one openly denied, their virtue must be of the same kind as men's and must rest on the same foundation.

Why, then, did women of her class seem frivolous, vain and governed by feeling? Because they were trained to be. From childhood they were taught that beauty and pleasing men were their whole business. They were kept "in a state of perpetual childhood," and then mocked for the results. Wollstonecraft compares them to soldiers and to idle aristocrats, other groups given show and obedience in place of independent thought.

The remedy is education. She proposes national day schools where boys and girls of all classes learn together. Women educated in this way would be better companions, better mothers and, above all, people in their own right.

### Against Rousseau

Her main opponent is [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]], whose *Emile* she otherwise admired. Rousseau taught that a woman's education should be relative to men, to please them and be useful to them. Wollstonecraft answers that he has made women into "gentle, domestic brutes," and that the obedience he asks of them is exactly the dependence he thought degraded men. "I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves."

### Independence

Wollstonecraft's argument reaches beyond the schoolroom. Marriage should be a friendship between equals, not a trade of beauty for support, which she calls "legal prostitution." Women should be able to earn their living as physicians, farmers or shopkeepers. She hints, cautiously, that they ought to have representatives in government "instead of being arbitrarily governed."

She also links the subjection of women to other kinds of arbitrary power. Hereditary rank, monarchy and the "divine right of husbands" stand or fall together, since all rest on birth instead of reason and all corrupt both the ruler and the ruled.

## Criticism and influence

After her death Godwin published a frank memoir that revealed her love affairs and her child born outside marriage. Her reputation was ruined for most of a century, and even later campaigners for women's rights kept their distance.

Modern feminists have raised two questions. Her ideal seems to ask women to become rational in a way that was modelled on men, and to distrust feeling and pleasure. And her argument addresses mainly middle-class women, often presenting education as a means to better motherhood. Others reply that she was arguing with the materials her age gave her, and that her linking of private relations to political power was far ahead of its time.

[[thinkers/mill|John Stuart Mill's]] *The Subjection of Women*, written with Harriet Taylor's influence, makes much the same case eighty years later. [[thinkers/beauvoir|Simone de Beauvoir's]] claim that one "becomes" a woman develops Wollstonecraft's insight that femininity is made, not given.

## Where to start

*A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*: the dedication to Talleyrand, the introduction and chapters 2 to 4 give the whole argument.
