# Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas was the medieval Dominican friar who joined Aristotle's philosophy to Christian theology. His account of natural law, a moral law that human reason can discover and that human laws must respect, is the classic statement of the idea.

| At a glance | |
| --- | --- |
| **Lived** | c. 1225–1274, Italy and Paris |
| **Wrote** | *Summa Theologiae*, *Summa contra Gentiles*, *On Kingship* |
| **Big idea** | Human law gets its authority from natural law, so an unjust law is a corruption of law |
| **Belongs with** | The [[ideas/natural-law\|natural law]] tradition |
| **Built on** | [[thinkers/aristotle\|Aristotle]] and [[thinkers/augustine\|Augustine]] |

## Life

Aquinas was born around 1225 at Roccasecca, between Rome and Naples, into a noble family that expected him to become abbot of the great monastery of Monte Cassino. Instead he joined the Dominicans, a new order of begging friars devoted to preaching and study. His family held him captive for about a year to change his mind and failed.

He studied under Albert the Great in Paris and Cologne and taught at the University of Paris, at a time when Aristotle's works were arriving in Latin translation and alarming the Church. Aquinas set out to show that Aristotle's philosophy and Christian faith fit together. He wrote at enormous length, dictating to several secretaries at once. In December 1273 he stopped writing, leaving the *Summa Theologiae* unfinished, and he died in March 1274 on his way to a church council. He was declared a saint in 1323.

## The main ideas

### Four kinds of law

The "Treatise on Law" in the *Summa Theologiae* defines law as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated." Aquinas then separates four kinds.

| Law | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Eternal law | God's rational plan for the whole of creation |
| Natural law | The part of the eternal law that human beings can grasp by reason |
| Human law | The particular rules a community makes for itself |
| Divine law | What God has revealed in scripture, guiding people to an end beyond this life |

The first precept of natural law is that good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided. From it, and from the basic inclinations humans share (to preserve life, to raise children, to live in society and to know the truth), reason works out more specific principles.

### Unjust laws

Human law is needed because natural law is general. It does not say which side of the road to drive on or what the penalty for theft should be. But human law draws its force from natural law. If a statute contradicts it, Aquinas says, it is "no longer a law but a perversion of law."

He adds a caution. An unjust law does not bind in conscience, yet it may still be right to obey it to avoid scandal or disorder, unless it commands something directly against God. Centuries later, Martin Luther King Jr. cited Aquinas by name in his "Letter from Birmingham Jail" to explain why segregation laws could be broken. See [[ideas/authority-and-obligation|authority and obligation]].

### Politics is natural

Here Aquinas sides with [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] against [[thinkers/augustine|Augustine]]. Humans are social and political by nature, so some form of rule directing people to the common good would exist even if there had been no sin. Government is not only a remedy for wickedness. It is part of a good human life.

In *On Kingship* he calls monarchy the best form in principle, because unity is the aim of rule. In the *Summa* he prefers a mixed government in which one person presides, the best share in ruling, and all have some part in choosing rulers. A tyrant, who rules for private gain, may be resisted, though by public authority and not by private assassins.

### Just war and property

Aquinas turned Augustine's remarks on war into three conditions: the authority of a sovereign, a just cause, and a right intention. This is the core of the just war tradition.

On property he holds that owning things privately is a useful human arrangement but that the goods of the earth are meant for everyone's use. A person in urgent need who takes what is necessary to survive does not commit theft. See [[ideas/property|property]].

## Criticism and influence

The standing objection to natural law is that people disagree about what nature and reason require, and that "natural" often turns out to mean "customary". [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] later argued that no statement about what ought to be can be derived from statements about what is. Legal positivists, starting with [[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]], deny that a law's validity depends on its morality at all.

Aquinas's framework survived all the same. It shaped the Spanish scholastics who debated the rights of the peoples of the Americas, the early modern theorists of natural rights, and Catholic social teaching. It has a modern revival in the work of John Finnis.

## Where to start

The *Summa Theologiae*, First Part of the Second Part, questions 90 to 97 (the Treatise on Law) is short and self-contained. Then Second Part of the Second Part, question 40 (war) and question 66 (property).
