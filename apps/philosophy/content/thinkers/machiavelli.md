# Niccolò Machiavelli

Niccolò Machiavelli was the Florentine official whose short book *The Prince* described how power is actually won and kept, not how moralists say it should be. He is often called the first modern political thinker, and he was also, in his longer *Discourses*, a committed defender of republics.

## Life

Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469. In 1498, after the fall of the preacher Savonarola, he became secretary to the Second Chancery of the Florentine Republic. For fourteen years he was a working diplomat. He met the French king, the German emperor and Pope Julius II, and he watched Cesare Borgia carve out a state in central Italy by speed, deceit and cruelty. He also organised a citizen militia for Florence, because he distrusted hired soldiers.

In 1512 the Medici family returned to power with Spanish troops, and the republic fell. Machiavelli was dismissed, and in 1513 he was arrested on suspicion of conspiracy and tortured. Released, he retired to his farm and wrote. *The Prince* was dedicated to a Medici in the hope of a job that never really came. He died in 1527. *The Prince* and the *Discourses* were printed only after his death, in 1532 and 1531.

## The main ideas

### The effectual truth

Chapter 15 of *The Prince* announces the break. Many writers, Machiavelli says, have imagined republics and principalities that have never been seen. He will go to "the effectual truth of the thing" instead. Because how people live is so far from how they ought to live, a prince who wants to survive "must learn to be able not to be good," and to use that ability or not as necessity requires.

The advice that follows made his name a synonym for cunning:

- It is best to be both loved and feared, but if one must choose, it is safer to be feared, as long as the prince avoids being hated.
- A prince should keep his word only when it serves him, while appearing faithful, merciful and religious.
- He must be both a **lion** to frighten wolves and a **fox** to recognise traps. [[thinkers/cicero|Cicero]] had called both beneath human dignity.
- Cruelty should be used all at once and then stopped. Benefits should be given little by little.

### Virtù and fortune

Machiavelli's key word is *virtù*. It does not mean moral virtue. It means the skill, energy and nerve needed to found and hold a state. Against it stands *fortuna*, the unpredictable turn of events. Fortune is like a river in flood, and virtù is the building of dikes in calm weather. He guesses that fortune rules about half of what we do and leaves the other half to us.

### The republican Machiavelli

The *Discourses on Livy* is a commentary on the history of the Roman Republic, and it shows a different side. Machiavelli argues that peoples are wiser and more constant than princes, and that republics last longer and expand further.

His most original claim is that conflict is healthy. Every city has two "humours", the great who want to dominate and the people who want not to be dominated. Rome's good laws came from the quarrels between them, which produced institutions such as the tribunes. A free state should give those quarrels a legal outlet, not suppress them. It also needs its own armed citizens and a civic spirit that puts the city first. See [[traditions/republicanism|republicanism]] and [[ideas/liberty|liberty]].

## How to read him

Readers have never agreed on how *The Prince* and the *Discourses* fit together.

- **A teacher of evil.** The oldest reading, and Leo Strauss's in the twentieth century. The Catholic Church put his works on its Index of banned books in 1559.
- **A scientist of politics.** He describes what works without endorsing it.
- **A republican in disguise.** [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] thought *The Prince* was "the book of republicans," a warning that exposed the methods of tyrants.
- **A patriot.** The last chapter calls for a leader to free Italy from foreign armies. Harsh means serve that end.

[[thinkers/berlin|Isaiah Berlin]] suggested the deepest shock was something else. Machiavelli did not deny Christian morality. He showed that it is incompatible with another, pagan morality of civic greatness, and that one cannot have both. That is an early glimpse of the idea that genuine values can conflict.

## Influence

Machiavelli separated the study of politics from theology and from moral philosophy. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]] shares his cool view of human motives, and the English republicans, [[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] and the American founders drew on the *Discourses*. The problem of "dirty hands", whether a good leader must sometimes do wrong, is still discussed in his terms, and returns in [[thinkers/weber|Weber's]] "Politics as a Vocation".

## Where to start

*The Prince* is under a hundred pages. Chapters 15 to 18 and 25 are the core. From the *Discourses*, read Book I, chapters 1 to 6 and 58.
