# Communitarianism

Communitarianism is the view that people are formed by the communities they belong to, and that political theory goes wrong when it starts from individuals imagined apart from any community. It arose in the 1980s as a criticism of the liberalism of John Rawls.

## The setting

[[thinkers/rawls|Rawls's]] *A Theory of Justice* (1971) asked what principles people would choose behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing their own values, attachments or place in society. It held that the right is prior to the good: a just state provides a fair framework of rights and stays neutral among its citizens' different ideas of a good life. See [[traditions/liberalism|liberalism]].

Within a decade, four philosophers had published objections with a family resemblance. None of them much liked the label "communitarian."

## The four critics

**Alasdair MacIntyre** (1929–2025). *After Virtue* (1981) argues that modern moral argument is a set of fragments left over from older traditions. We go on using words such as "rights" and "duty" after losing the shared idea of a human purpose that once gave them sense, and so our disputes cannot be settled. The choice, he says, is between [[thinkers/nietzsche|Nietzsche]], who saw that modern morality has no foundation, and [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]], for whom the virtues make sense within practices, traditions and the story of a whole life. MacIntyre chose Aristotle and later [[thinkers/aquinas|Aquinas]].

**Michael Sandel.** *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) is a direct critique of Rawls. The parties in the original position are **unencumbered selves**, stripped of the loyalties and convictions that make people who they are. But some of our ends are not chosen. I do not decide to be my parents' child or a member of my people. I find that I am. A self without such attachments is not free. It is empty. Sandel's later work argues that a state neutral about the good life cannot sustain the civic virtues that self-government needs, and that markets have spread into areas of life where they do not belong.

**Charles Taylor.** A Canadian philosopher and a student of [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]], Taylor attacked what he called **atomism**: the picture of society as a collection of self-sufficient individuals. The capacities liberals prize, such as autonomy and rational choice, develop only within a certain kind of society and culture, so those who value them have an obligation to sustain it. His essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) argued that identity is formed in dialogue with others, that being misrecognised is a real harm, and that this can justify measures to help a culture survive, such as language laws in Quebec.

**Michael Walzer.** *Spheres of Justice* (1983) argues that there is no single principle of distribution. Each kind of good, whether money, office, education, medical care or honour, has a social meaning that fixes how it should be shared, and meanings differ between societies. Justice is local. Its enemy is **dominance**, in which success in one sphere, usually wealth, buys success in all the others.

## The shared claims

| Liberal position (as the critics read it) | Communitarian reply |
| --- | --- |
| The self chooses its ends | The self discovers ends it is already bound by |
| The right is prior to the good | Principles of justice depend on a shared understanding of the good |
| The state should be neutral among ways of life | A community may, and must, promote its shared values |
| Principles of justice are universal | Moral reasoning starts from the traditions of a particular society |
| Politics protects rights | Politics is also about belonging, duty and the common good |

Behind the critics stand [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]], for whom humans are political animals, [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]], who set ethical life against abstract right, and [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] on associations. [[thinkers/burke|Burke's]] "little platoons" make a similar point from the right.

## The liberal response

- **Rawls** restated his theory in *Political Liberalism* (1993). The original position, he said, is a device for thinking about fairness, not a theory of what a person is. People can be deeply attached to their ends in private life and still, as citizens, set them aside when deciding the terms of cooperation.
- **Will Kymlicka** argued that liberals can and should value cultural membership, because a secure culture gives people the options from which they choose. This led to liberal theories of minority rights.
- **Whose community?** Communities have often been hierarchical and stifling, especially for women and dissenters. Susan Moller Okin pointed out that the "shared understandings" Walzer relies on usually reflect the view of those in charge. If justice is whatever a society believes, there is no ground for criticising a caste system from within.
- **Which community?** Family, church, nation and neighbourhood make rival claims.

## What became of it

By the mid-1990s the debate had cooled. Both sides had moved. Liberals said more about community, culture and civic virtue, and the communitarians made clear that they were not rejecting rights. A more political movement of the same name, led by the sociologist Amitai Etzioni, called for balancing rights with responsibilities. The themes carried over into arguments about multiculturalism, nationalism, civic [[traditions/republicanism|republicanism]] and the decline of civic life.
