# Republicanism

Republicanism is the tradition that sees freedom as self-government by citizens under their own laws, in a state that is no one's private possession. It has nothing in particular to do with the modern American party of that name. Its concerns are civic virtue, the common good and the danger of domination.

## Core ideas

- **The republic.** A *res publica* is a "public thing," a state that belongs to its citizens. Its opposite is rule by a master, whether a king, a clique or a foreign power.
- **Liberty as independence.** To be free is to have no master. A subject who depends on a ruler's goodwill is unfree even if the ruler is kind.
- **The rule of law.** Citizens are governed by laws, not by persons. The English republican James Harrington called this "an empire of laws and not of men."
- **Civic virtue.** Free government lasts only if citizens care for the common good, take part and keep watch on those in power.
- **Corruption.** The great danger is that private interest, luxury or faction eats away public spirit, and the republic falls to a strongman.
- **Mixed and balanced government.** Power shared among different bodies and classes so that none can dominate.

## A short history

**Rome.** The tradition looks back to the Roman Republic, as remembered by the historians Livy and Sallust and by [[thinkers/cicero|Cicero]], and to [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle's]] account of the citizen as one who rules and is ruled in turn. For Romans the basic distinction in law was between the free person and the slave, who lives "in the power of another."

**The Renaissance.** The city-republics of Italy revived these ideas. [[thinkers/machiavelli|Machiavelli's]] *Discourses on Livy* is the central text. A republic stays free through the vigilance of its people, a citizen army, and institutions that let the conflict between nobles and people serve liberty.

**England.** In the seventeenth century, Harrington, John Milton and Algernon Sidney argued against monarchy in republican terms. Their successors, the eighteenth-century "commonwealthmen," warned of corruption by ministers, standing armies and public debt.

**The age of revolutions.** These writers were read closely in the American colonies, and the revolution of 1776 was fought in their language of liberty against slavery, virtue against corruption. [[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] held that republics run on virtue and must be small. [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] answered with the extended federal republic, which relies on institutions more than on virtue. [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] gave the tradition its most demanding form, with a sovereign people making law directly. [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Wollstonecraft]] used the republican idea of independence against the dependence of women on men.

**Eclipse and revival.** In the nineteenth century republicanism was largely absorbed into [[traditions/liberalism|liberalism]]. Historians recovered it from the 1960s: J. G. A. Pocock in *The Machiavellian Moment* (1975) and Quentin Skinner in studies of Renaissance and English thought. Philip Pettit's *Republicanism* (1997) turned it into a contemporary political philosophy.

## Freedom as non-domination

Pettit's version starts from a contrast with the liberal idea of freedom as non-interference. Consider a slave with a kindly master who never interferes, and a citizen bound by fair laws they have a say in. On the liberal test the slave is freer. Republicans hold the reverse. What matters is whether anyone has **arbitrary** power over you: power they may use at will, without having to track your interests.

This has practical force. It condemns relations of dependence that involve no actual interference: the worker who can be fired at will, the wife without income of her own, the debtor at a creditor's mercy, the citizen under a government that could but does not abuse its powers. And it does not count law as a loss of freedom, when the law is made and controlled by those it binds. See [[ideas/liberty|liberty]].

## Republicanism and liberalism

The two overlap heavily. Both want limited, lawful government and both descend in part from the same sources. The differences are of emphasis.

- Liberals stress rights held against the state. Republicans stress citizenship and the duties that sustain a free state.
- Liberals tend to treat participation as optional. Republicans treat a passive citizenry as a danger sign. [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] is the great bridge figure.
- Republican freedom is more demanding about private power, in the workplace and the family.

## Objections

- **Virtue is demanding.** A state that needs virtuous citizens may be tempted to manufacture them, by civic religion, censorship or compulsory service.
- **Exclusion.** Historical republics were clubs of propertied, armed men, and relied on the labour of women, slaves and the poor.
- **Is it distinct?** Some liberals argue that non-domination adds little to a sufficiently careful account of non-interference.
- **Size.** The model was the city-state. Whether it scales to a nation of millions is the question [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] and [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] answered in opposite ways.
