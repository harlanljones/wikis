# Liberalism

Liberalism is the political tradition built on individual freedom, equal rights, government by consent and limits on power. It is the dominant outlook of modern democracies, and its family quarrels, especially over the role of the state in the economy, make up much of their politics.

A note on the word. In the United States "liberal" usually means left of centre. In much of Europe and Australia it means pro-market. In political philosophy it covers both, and names the broad tradition they share.

## Core commitments

- **The individual comes first.** Society exists for the sake of its members, not the reverse.
- **Liberty.** People should be free to live by their own beliefs, so far as this is compatible with the same freedom for others.
- **Equality of status.** No one is born to rule or to obey.
- **Consent.** Legitimate government answers to the governed.
- **Limited government.** Power is bound by law, divided and accountable. See [[ideas/separation-of-powers|separation of powers]].
- **Toleration.** The state does not impose a religion or a single way of life. See [[ideas/toleration|toleration]].

## A short history

**Origins.** Liberalism grew from the wars of religion, the struggle against absolute monarchy and the rise of commerce. [[thinkers/locke|Locke]] gave it a first full statement in 1689: natural rights, government as a trust, toleration and a right to resist. [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes]], no liberal in his conclusions, supplied the method of reasoning from free and equal individuals.

**The Enlightenment.** [[thinkers/montesquieu|Montesquieu]] added the division of powers, [[thinkers/smith|Adam Smith]] the case for free markets, and [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] the idea of persons as ends in themselves who must be free to use their own reason. [[thinkers/madison|Madison]] turned these into a working constitution. [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Wollstonecraft]] demanded that the principles be applied to women.

**The nineteenth century.** Benjamin Constant and [[thinkers/tocqueville|Tocqueville]] thought through liberty after the French Revolution and under democracy. [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] gave the classic defence of individuality and free speech.

**The new liberalism.** By the late nineteenth century, industrial poverty led liberals such as T. H. Green and L. T. Hobhouse in Britain and John Dewey in America to argue that freedom requires real opportunity, and so schools, public health and protection against unemployment and old age. This is the root of the welfare state. John Maynard Keynes and William Beveridge, its architects in Britain, were both Liberals.

**After 1945.** [[thinkers/berlin|Berlin]], Karl Popper and [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] defended the open society against totalitarianism. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] gave egalitarian liberalism its philosophical foundation in 1971, and [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick]] answered for the libertarian wing.

## The two wings

| | Classical liberalism | Social or egalitarian liberalism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freedom means | Not being coerced | Having real opportunity to shape one's life |
| Main threat | The state | Private power and poverty, as well as the state |
| Markets | The system of natural liberty | Useful, and in need of correction |
| The state should | Protect rights and keep the peace | Also secure education, health and a social minimum |
| Thinkers | [[thinkers/locke\|Locke]], [[thinkers/smith\|Smith]], [[thinkers/hayek\|Hayek]] | [[thinkers/mill\|Mill]] in part, [[thinkers/rawls\|Rawls]], [[thinkers/sen\|Sen]], [[thinkers/nussbaum\|Nussbaum]] |

[[traditions/libertarianism|Libertarianism]] is the classical wing taken to its conclusion. "Neoliberalism" is the name, mostly used by critics, for the revival of free-market policy since the 1970s.

A second division is philosophical. **Perfectionist** liberals such as Mill and Joseph Raz defend freedom because an autonomous life is a good life. **Political** liberals such as the later Rawls hold that the state should not rest on any one view of the good life, including that one.

## The critics

- [[traditions/conservatism|Conservatives]] say liberalism dissolves the traditions and attachments that make freedom workable. See [[thinkers/burke|Burke]].
- [[traditions/socialism|Socialists]] say liberal rights are formal and leave economic power untouched. See [[thinkers/marx|Marx]].
- [[traditions/communitarianism|Communitarians]] say it rests on a false picture of the self as prior to its community.
- [[traditions/feminism|Feminists]] say its line between public and private hid the subordination of women.
- [[traditions/republicanism|Republicans]] say it neglects citizenship and defines freedom too thinly.
- [[thinkers/schmitt|Schmitt]] said it cannot face the reality of political conflict.
- **Postcolonial critics** point out that Locke, Mill and Tocqueville all defended empire, and ask whether that was an accident. See [[thinkers/fanon|Fanon]].

Liberals have absorbed a good deal of this. Much of the tradition's history consists of extending its own principles to people it first left out.
