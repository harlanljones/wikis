# Socialism

Socialism is the political tradition that seeks to bring economic life under collective or democratic control, in order to end the inequality and dependence that it sees as built into capitalism. It ranges from revolutionary communism to the moderate social democracy that built the welfare states of Europe.

## Core commitments

- **Equality** of condition, not only of rights. Formal freedom means little to those without means.
- **Community and cooperation** over competition. People are social beings, and an economy should reflect that.
- **Common ownership** or democratic control of the major means of production, in some form.
- **A critique of capitalism** as exploitative, wasteful, unstable or alienating.
- **Class.** Society is divided by economic position, and politics reflects that division.

## A short history

**Before Marx.** Ideas of common property are ancient. [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] gave his guardians none, and Thomas More's *Utopia* (1516) abolished money. [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] traced inequality to private property. Modern socialism began in the early nineteenth century as a response to the factory system. Robert Owen in Britain and Henri de Saint-Simon and Charles Fourier in France proposed cooperative communities and planned industry. [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] and Engels called them "utopian socialists," because they appealed to goodwill and had no account of how change would come.

**Marx.** [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] claimed to put socialism on a scientific footing. Capitalism rests on the extraction of surplus value from workers, generates crises and a growing working class, and will be overthrown by that class. His theory of history, economics and revolution dominated the movement for a century.

**The great split.** By 1900 the German Social Democratic Party was the largest socialist party in the world, and it was arguing with itself. Eduard Bernstein, the first "revisionist," observed that capitalism was not collapsing and that workers were gaining through the ballot. Socialism should be pursued by democratic reform. Rosa Luxemburg replied that reform without revolution would leave capitalism in place.

In Russia, Lenin argued that a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries must lead the working class. The Bolsheviks took power in 1917 and built a one-party state. From then on the movement was divided between **communists**, loyal to the Soviet model, and **social democrats**, committed to parliamentary democracy.

**The twentieth century.** Communist regimes under Stalin and Mao combined state ownership and central planning with dictatorship, terror and famine, and most had collapsed or turned to markets by the early 1990s. Social democratic parties governed much of Western Europe after 1945. They kept markets and private ownership and added public services, social insurance, strong trade unions and progressive taxation.

## The family

| Branch | Aim | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communism (Marxism-Leninism) | A classless, stateless society, by way of state ownership | Revolution, rule by a vanguard party |
| Democratic socialism | Social ownership of the main industries | Elections and democratic institutions |
| Social democracy | A regulated market with a strong welfare state | Reform within capitalism |
| Market socialism | Worker-owned or publicly owned firms competing in markets | Varies |
| Libertarian socialism | Common ownership without the state | Direct action. See [[traditions/anarchism\|anarchism]] |

**Western Marxism** developed apart from the parties. Antonio Gramsci, writing in a Fascist prison, asked why workers consent to capitalism and answered with the theory of **hegemony**. The Frankfurt School (Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno, Herbert Marcuse) joined [[thinkers/marx|Marx]] to Freud and [[thinkers/weber|Weber]] to analyse mass culture and bureaucratic society. Its heir is [[thinkers/habermas|Habermas]]. [[thinkers/beauvoir|Beauvoir]] and [[thinkers/fanon|Fanon]] extended the critique to sex and to colonialism.

**Analytical Marxism** arose in the 1970s. G. A. Cohen defended Marx's theory of history with the tools of analytic philosophy, and later argued for socialism on moral grounds, as a matter of equality and community.

## Objections

- **Knowledge and incentives.** Ludwig von Mises and [[thinkers/hayek|Hayek]] argued that without market prices a planner cannot know what to produce, and that planning concentrates power. See [[debates/markets-vs-planning|markets vs planning]].
- **Liberty.** [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick]] argued that equality can be kept up only by constant interference with free exchange. [[thinkers/berlin|Berlin]] and Popper saw danger in the claim to know where history is going.
- **The record.** Every state that abolished private ownership of industry became a dictatorship. Socialists dispute whether this followed from the idea or from the circumstances.
- **Human nature.** Critics say socialism asks more public spirit than people have. Socialists reply that character reflects institutions.

## Where the argument stands

The debate between socialism and capitalism has largely become one about the mix: how much to leave to markets, how to tax and regulate, what to provide in common. [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] held that justice could be met either by a property-owning democracy or by liberal socialism, but not by welfare-state capitalism as we know it. Rising inequality, automation and climate change have renewed interest in the questions socialists asked.

## Start reading

*The Communist Manifesto*, Bernstein's *The Preconditions of Socialism*, George Orwell's *Homage to Catalonia* for a democratic socialist's view of communism in practice, and G. A. Cohen's short *Why Not Socialism?*
