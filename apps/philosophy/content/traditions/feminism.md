# Feminism

Feminist political thought examines how societies have subordinated women to men and argues for ending that subordination. It has also changed the subject of political philosophy, by showing that the family, sexuality and care are political matters and not a private realm outside justice.

## Core claims

- **Women's subordination is social, not natural.** Differences presented as nature are largely the results of upbringing, law and custom.
- **It is unjust**, and can be changed.
- **The personal is political.** Power operates in the household and in intimate life, as well as in parliaments and markets.
- **The canon is partial.** Theories about "man" and "the individual" were written with men in mind.

## The canon and women

Most of the thinkers on this wiki either ignored women or argued for their subordination. [[thinkers/aristotle|Aristotle]] held that the male is by nature fitter to rule. [[thinkers/rousseau|Rousseau]] gave women an education designed to please men. [[thinkers/kant|Kant]] and [[thinkers/hegel|Hegel]] kept them out of citizenship. The exceptions are notable: [[thinkers/plato|Plato]] admitted women to the guardian class, and [[thinkers/mill|Mill]] wrote *The Subjection of Women*.

Feminist scholars such as Susan Moller Okin and Carole Pateman showed that this was not a detail to be corrected with a footnote. Theories built on independent heads of households depend on someone else raising children and keeping house. Take that assumption away and the theory has to change.

## A short history

It is usual to speak of waves, though the picture is a simplification.

**Beginnings.** Christine de Pizan defended women against their detractors in 1405. Olympe de Gouges answered the French Revolution's Declaration of the Rights of Man with a Declaration of the Rights of Woman in 1791 and was guillotined two years later. [[thinkers/wollstonecraft|Mary Wollstonecraft's]] *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) is the founding text: women appear less rational because they are denied education, and reason has no sex.

**The first wave** (mid-nineteenth century to about 1920) sought legal equality: the vote, property rights in marriage, access to education and the professions. The Seneca Falls Convention of 1848 rewrote the American Declaration: "all men and women are created equal." Harriet Taylor and [[thinkers/mill|John Stuart Mill]] gave the philosophical case. Sojourner Truth, a former slave, asked whose womanhood the movement had in mind.

**The second wave** (1960s to 1980s) turned to work, sexuality, reproduction, violence and the family. Its philosophical source is [[thinkers/beauvoir|Simone de Beauvoir's]] *The Second Sex* (1949): "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) described the discontent of educated housewives. Radical feminists such as Kate Millett and Shulamith Firestone named the system **patriarchy** and located it in sexuality and reproduction.

**Since the 1980s.** Black feminists, including bell hooks and Audre Lorde, criticised a movement that took white middle-class women as the norm. The legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw coined **intersectionality** in 1989 for the way race, sex and class combine, so that a Black woman's situation is not simply that of women plus that of Black people. Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) argued that gender is not an inner essence but something done, through repeated acts.

## Schools of thought

| School | Diagnosis | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liberal | Unequal rights and opportunities | Equal treatment in law, education and work |
| Socialist and Marxist | Capitalism depends on women's unpaid work at home | Change the economy and socialise care |
| Radical | Patriarchy, rooted in control of women's bodies and sexuality | Transform sexuality, the family and culture |
| Care ethics | Moral theory ignores dependence and relationships | Put care at the centre |
| Black and intersectional | Sexism is bound up with racism and class | Begin from the most marginalised |
| Postmodern | The category "woman" is itself a product of power | Unsettle fixed identities |

## Contributions to political theory

**The public and the private.** Liberal theory limited the state's reach into private life. Feminists showed that the "private" sphere was where much of women's subordination took place, protected from scrutiny. Carole Pateman's *The Sexual Contract* (1988) argued that the [[ideas/social-contract|social contract]] of the classic theorists rested on an unspoken prior agreement giving men authority over women.

**Justice in the family.** Okin's *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (1989) argued that [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls's]] principles should apply inside the household. A family in which unpaid work falls on women makes them economically vulnerable and is a poor school of justice for children.

**Care.** Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982) described a way of moral reasoning centred on responsibility and relationships. Later theorists such as Joan Tronto and Eva Kittay turned this into a political claim. Everyone is dependent for part of their life, someone must do the work of care, and a theory that assumes independent adults leaves both the carers and the cared-for out. [[thinkers/nussbaum|Nussbaum]] builds this into the capabilities approach.

**Domination.** Catharine MacKinnon argued that inequality between the sexes is a matter of power more than of difference, expressed in sexual harassment, a legal category she helped create, and in pornography.

## Disagreements within

Feminists disagree about a great deal: whether to stress women's sameness to men or their difference; whether pornography and sex work should be opposed as exploitation or defended as free choice; how to weigh respect for cultures against the rights of women within them; and what follows from the distinction between sex and gender, including for transgender people.
