# Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is the view that the right action, law or institution is the one that produces the greatest total happiness or well-being, with everyone's interests counted equally. It is the leading form of consequentialism, one of the three main [[ideas/ethical-theories|ethical theories]], and it has shaped law, economics and public policy more than any other philosophical doctrine.

## The principle

Three ideas make it up.

1. **Consequentialism.** Only outcomes matter in judging what is right.
2. **Welfarism.** The outcome that matters is how well people's lives go.
3. **Impartial summing.** Everyone's welfare counts the same, and what should be maximised is the total.

[[thinkers/bentham|Bentham]] gave the slogan: "the greatest happiness of the greatest number." He is also credited with the formula "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one." In an age of rank and privilege this was radical. The happiness of a servant counts exactly as much as that of a duke.

## The classical utilitarians

**Forerunners.** The ancient Epicureans held that pleasure is the good. [[thinkers/hume|Hume]] argued that we approve of justice because it is useful. The Italian reformer Cesare Beccaria used the test of the greatest happiness in *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) to argue against torture and the death penalty.

[[thinkers/bentham|**Jeremy Bentham**]] made it a system. Pleasure and pain are the only things good and bad in themselves. They can in principle be measured, and legislation should be designed to maximise the balance of pleasure. He applied the test to prisons, courts, poor relief and the constitution, and found almost every existing institution wanting.

[[thinkers/mill|**John Stuart Mill**]] humanised the doctrine. Pleasures differ in quality as well as amount, and those of the intellect and feelings rank above those of the body. He argued that rights and justice are themselves grounded in utility, and that liberty is the condition of human progress.

**Henry Sidgwick** gave the most careful statement in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874). He admitted that he could not show why a person should sacrifice their own happiness for the general good, which he called the deepest problem in ethics.

## Varieties

| Question | Options |
| --- | --- |
| What is welfare? | **Hedonism**: pleasure and the absence of pain. **Preference satisfaction**: getting what one wants. **Objective goods**: things such as knowledge and friendship, whether or not one wants them |
| What is judged? | **Act** utilitarianism: each action, by its results. **Rule** utilitarianism: actions by rules, and rules by the results of everyone following them |
| What is maximised? | **Total** welfare, or **average** welfare per person |

Rule utilitarianism is an attempt to meet the standard objections. A society that followed the rule "never punish the innocent" would be happier than one whose officials judged each case by its results, so the rule should be kept even when breaking it would do good on one occasion. Critics ask whether this is still utilitarianism, or whether it collapses back into the act version whenever the stakes are high enough.

## Why it appeals

- It is **impartial**. No one's interests are privileged by birth, nation or species.
- It gives **a method**. Moral and political questions become, at least in principle, questions of fact about what improves lives.
- It is **reforming**. It asks of every law and custom what good it does. Utilitarians were early advocates of prison reform, the abolition of slavery, votes for women, the decriminalisation of homosexuality and the welfare of animals.

Welfare economics and cost-benefit analysis are utilitarianism in practical form.

## Objections

**Justice and rights.** If only the total counts, it may be right to sacrifice one person to benefit many: to convict an innocent man to stop a riot, or to take one patient's organs to save five. Utilitarians reply that such acts would have terrible wider effects, or retreat to rules.

**The separateness of persons.** [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]] argued that utilitarianism extends to a whole society the reasoning a single person uses about their own life, trading a loss here for a gain there. But a society is not one person. The loser and the gainer are different people, and the gain to one does not make up for the loss to the other.

**Distribution.** A given total can be shared equally or very unequally. Utilitarianism is indifferent, except that money usually does more good in poor hands.

**The experience machine.** [[thinkers/nozick|Nozick]] asked whether you would plug into a machine that gave you a lifetime of pleasant experiences. If not, there is more to a good life than how it feels.

**Adaptive preferences.** People adjust their wants to their circumstances. [[thinkers/sen|Sen]] pointed out that a measure based on satisfaction will rate the contented poor as well off.

**Integrity and demandingness.** Bernard Williams argued that utilitarianism treats a person as a channel for producing good outcomes and leaves no room for the commitments that make a life one's own. It also seems to demand that we give until giving more would hurt us as much as it helps others.

**Measurement.** There is no agreed way to compare one person's happiness with another's.

## Today

Peter Singer is the best-known utilitarian now writing. His *Animal Liberation* (1975) argued that the suffering of animals counts equally with similar human suffering, and "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) that people in rich countries are obliged to give far more than they do to relieve poverty abroad. The effective altruism movement, which tries to work out where money and effort do the most good, grew from this.

Derek Parfit explored the puzzles utilitarianism faces over future generations, including the "repugnant conclusion": that an enormous population with lives barely worth living could be better than a smaller, flourishing one.
