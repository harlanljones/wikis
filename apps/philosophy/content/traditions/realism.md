# Political realism

Political realism is the view that politics is first of all about power, conflict and order, and that it cannot be understood or conducted by applying the moral rules of private life. Realists distrust blueprints for a just society and ask what is possible given how people actually behave.

The word is used in two connected fields. In **international relations** it names a school that explains the behaviour of states by their pursuit of security and power. In **political theory** it names a reaction against approaches, above all that of [[thinkers/rawls|Rawls]], that treat political philosophy as applied ethics.

## Core themes

- **Conflict is permanent.** People disagree about interests and values, and no argument will end that.
- **Order comes first.** Before justice or liberty can be pursued, there must be peace and a working authority.
- **Power is the medium of politics** and cannot be wished away.
- **Human nature is flawed,** or at least not to be relied on.
- **Consequences matter.** Leaders must answer for results, not just for good intentions.
- **Suspicion of moralism.** Appeals to justice often cloak interest, and crusades do more harm than limited aims.

## The ancestors

**Thucydides** (fifth century BC) recorded the war between Athens and Sparta. In his account of the Athenians' ultimatum to the small island of Melos, their envoys set aside talk of right: "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians refused, and were destroyed. Readers still argue over whether Thucydides endorsed the Athenians or was showing the arrogance that ruined them.

[[thinkers/augustine|**Augustine**]] saw government as a remedy for sin, capable of keeping a rough peace and nothing higher.

[[thinkers/machiavelli|**Machiavelli**]] separated the question of how rulers should live from how they must act to keep their states, and advised the prince "to learn to be able not to be good."

[[thinkers/hobbes|**Hobbes**]] derived the need for a sovereign from a state of nature in which fear and distrust make war the default. International relations theorists took over his picture for a world of states with no common power.

[[thinkers/weber|**Weber**]] defined the state by its monopoly of legitimate violence and distinguished an ethic of conviction from an ethic of responsibility. Anyone who enters politics, he said, makes a pact with violent means.

[[thinkers/schmitt|**Schmitt**]] pushed realism to an extreme with his claim that politics rests on the distinction between friend and enemy.

## Realism between states

After the failure of the League of Nations, writers such as E. H. Carr in Britain and Hans Morgenthau and the theologian Reinhold Niebuhr in America attacked what they called idealism or utopianism: the belief that law, institutions and goodwill could abolish war.

| School | Why states compete | Key writer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical realism | Human nature. The drive for power is built into us | Hans Morgenthau, *Politics Among Nations* (1948) |
| Structural or neorealism | The system. With no world government, each state must look to its own security | Kenneth Waltz, *Theory of International Politics* (1979) |

The **security dilemma** is the central idea. One state arms to feel safe, its neighbour feels threatened and arms too, and both end less secure. No one need be wicked for this to happen.

Realists tend to recommend prudence: a balance of power, limited aims, and caution about wars fought for ideals. Morgenthau opposed the Vietnam War, and many realists opposed the invasion of Iraq in 2003. Realism is not the same as militarism.

Its main rival is the liberal tradition descending from [[thinkers/kant|Kant's]] *Perpetual Peace*, which holds that democracy, trade and international institutions can make relations between states less dangerous.

## Realism in political theory

From the 1990s, Bernard Williams and Raymond Geuss criticised the dominant style of political philosophy as **political moralism**: working out an ideal theory of justice first and then applying it to politics as if politics were a branch of ethics.

Williams argued that the "first political question" is [[thinkers/hobbes|Hobbes's]]: how to secure order, protection and the conditions of cooperation. A state must also satisfy what he called the **basic legitimation demand**. It must offer each person it rules some justification of its power that is more than force. A state that simply terrorises a group is not solving the first political question for them. It is the problem.

On this view, legitimacy is the central political value, it varies with historical circumstances, and liberalism is the answer that makes sense "now and around here," not a truth for all times.

Geuss, more radical, asks of any political theory Lenin's question: who does what to whom, for whose benefit?

## The problem of dirty hands

A question realists have kept alive is whether a good leader must sometimes do wrong. [[thinkers/machiavelli|Machiavelli]] said yes. [[thinkers/weber|Weber]] thought the politician must accept guilt as the price of responsibility. Michael Walzer, in a well-known 1973 essay, argued that a leader who orders something terrible to prevent a catastrophe may have done the right thing and still be guilty of a moral wrong. See [[ideas/ethical-theories|ethical theories]].

## Objections

- **Realism has its own morality.** To prefer order, stability or the national interest is to make a value judgement. Realists rarely defend it.
- **It can excuse anything.** "That is how the world is" is the standing alibi of the powerful.
- **It may be false.** States cooperate a great deal, democracies seldom fight each other, and norms such as the abolition of slavery have changed what is thought possible.
- **Self-fulfilment.** Treating others as threats tends to make them so.
- **What is possible changes.** Much that was once dismissed as utopian, including democracy itself, came to pass.

Realists answer that their aim is not to end moral argument about politics but to keep it honest about power.
