# White balance

Light sources have different colours. Candlelight is orange, midday sun is neutral and open shade is blue. Your eyes adapt without you noticing. A camera has to be told, or has to guess, and white balance is that setting.

## Colour temperature

The colour of a light source is measured in **kelvin** (K). The scale runs opposite to intuition: lower numbers are warmer (more orange), higher numbers are cooler (more blue).

| Light source | Approximate colour temperature |
| ------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Candle | 1900 K |
| Household tungsten bulb, "warm white" LED | 2700 K to 3000 K |
| Sunrise and sunset | 3000 K to 4000 K |
| Flash, midday sun | 5200 K to 5600 K |
| Overcast sky | 6000 K to 6500 K |
| Open shade under a blue sky | 7000 K to 8000 K |

A second axis, **tint**, runs from green to magenta. It matters under fluorescent and some LED lighting, which often has a green cast.

## The settings

- **Auto (AWB).** Right most of the time, especially outdoors. Many cameras offer two versions: one that neutralises warm indoor light and one that keeps some warmth ("ambience priority" or "keep warm colour").
- **Presets.** Daylight, cloudy, shade, tungsten, fluorescent, flash. Useful for keeping a series of photos consistent, since auto can drift from frame to frame.
- **Kelvin.** Dial in a number directly.
- **Custom.** Photograph a grey card or white surface in the same light and the camera measures it. This is the most accurate, and it is worth doing for product, food and indoor event work.

Setting the camera to a preset *warmer* than the light makes the picture warmer. Daylight white balance at sunset keeps the orange. Auto white balance may try to remove it.

## Raw makes it reversible

In a [[basics/raw-vs-jpeg|raw file]], white balance is only a note attached to the data. You can change it afterward with no loss of quality. In a JPEG it is baked in, and large corrections damage the colours.

This is one of the strongest practical reasons to shoot raw, especially indoors under mixed lighting.

## Mixed lighting

When two light sources of different colours light the same scene, such as window light and a tungsten lamp, no single white balance is right for both. The options are to turn one source off, to balance for the subject's face and accept a colour cast elsewhere, or to fix it locally when editing.

## Correct versus pleasing

Neutral is not always the goal. A sunrise should look warm, and blue hour should look blue. White balance is a creative choice as much as a correction. The real aim is that skin tones look healthy and that the picture feels like the scene did.
