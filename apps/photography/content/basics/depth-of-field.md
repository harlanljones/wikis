# Depth of field

Depth of field is the range of distances in a photo that look acceptably sharp. A portrait with a soft, blurred background has **shallow** depth of field. A landscape that is sharp from the foreground rocks to the horizon has **deep** depth of field.

A lens focuses at exactly one distance. Sharpness falls off gradually in front of and behind that distance, and depth of field describes how gradual the falloff is.

## What controls it

| Change | Effect on depth of field |
| ------ | ------------------------ |
| Wider [[basics/aperture\|aperture]] (f/1.8 instead of f/8) | Shallower |
| Longer [[basics/focal-length\|focal length]], same position | Shallower |
| Closer to the subject | Shallower |
| Larger sensor, same framing and f-number | Shallower |

Distance is the one people forget. At macro distances even f/11 gives a few millimetres of sharpness. At 30 metres, f/2.8 keeps a whole building sharp.

## Background blur

Depth of field and background blur are related but not the same. How blurred the background looks also depends on how far it is behind the subject. For a softer background:

1. Use a wider aperture.
2. Move closer to the subject.
3. Move the subject farther from the background. This is free and often the most effective.
4. Use a longer lens and step back.

**Bokeh** is the *quality* of the blur, not the amount: whether out-of-focus highlights are smooth discs or have hard edges, onion rings or a cat's-eye shape toward the corners. It is a property of the lens design.

## Deep focus for landscapes

To keep a whole scene sharp:

- Use a wide lens. Wide angles have far more depth of field than telephotos.
- Stop down to around f/8 to f/11 on full frame, f/5.6 to f/8 on APS-C. Narrower than that and diffraction softens everything.
- Focus about a third of the way into the scene, not on the horizon. The **hyperfocal distance** is the focus distance that keeps everything from half that distance to infinity sharp. For a 24mm lens at f/11 on full frame it is roughly 1.8 metres.
- If the foreground is very close, take several frames focused at different distances and merge them. This is **focus stacking**, and many cameras can shoot the sequence automatically (often called focus bracketing).

## Sensor size

For the same framing and the same f-number, a larger sensor gives shallower depth of field. Multiply the f-number by the crop factor to compare:

| Lens and sensor | Looks like, on full frame |
| --------------- | ------------------------- |
| 25mm f/1.8 on Micro Four Thirds | 50mm f/3.6 |
| 33mm f/1.4 on APS-C | 50mm f/2.1 |
| 50mm f/1.8 on full frame | 50mm f/1.8 |
| 63mm f/2.8 on medium format | 50mm f/2.2 |

This cuts both ways. Larger sensors make background blur easier. Smaller sensors make deep focus easier, which helps for macro, landscapes and video. See [[cameras/sensor-sizes#Equivalence|equivalence]].
