# Shutter speed

Shutter speed is how long the sensor collects light for one photo. It controls two things at once: how bright the image is, and whether motion is frozen or blurred.

It is written in seconds or fractions of a second. On a camera display, `250` usually means 1/250 s and `2"` means two seconds.

## Two kinds of blur

**Subject motion** is blur from something in the scene moving. Only a faster shutter speed fixes it.

**Camera shake** is blur from the camera moving in your hands. A faster shutter speed fixes it, and so does [[cameras/image-stabilization|image stabilization]] or a tripod.

Stabilization does nothing for a moving subject. A stabilized camera can hold a building sharp at 1/4 s, but a person walking past will still smear.

## Starting points

| Subject | Shutter speed |
| ------- | ------------- |
| Birds in flight, motorsport | 1/2000 s or faster |
| Field sports, running dogs, kids playing | 1/1000 s |
| People walking, casual events | 1/250 s |
| Posed portraits | 1/125 s |
| Static scenes, handheld with stabilization | 1/30 s and slower |
| Silky waterfalls and streams | 1/4 s to 2 s, on a tripod |
| Smooth lakes and surf, light trails | 10 s to 30 s, on a tripod |
| Stars as points (wide lens) | 10 s to 20 s, on a tripod |

## The handheld rule

Without stabilization, a safe handheld shutter speed is about **1 / focal length**: 1/50 s for a 50mm lens, 1/200 s for a 200mm lens. Use the full-frame equivalent focal length on smaller sensors, and go faster on cameras with 40 megapixels or more, because fine detail shows small movements.

Stabilization moves that limit by several stops. A system rated at 5 stops can, in good conditions, hold a 50mm lens at around 1/2 s. Treat ratings as a best case and expect two or three stops less when you are tired, cold or breathing hard.

## Long exposures

For exposures longer than you can hold:

- Use a tripod and a 2-second self-timer or a remote release.
- Most cameras time exposures up to 30 seconds. **Bulb** mode holds the shutter open as long as you like.
- In daylight, slow shutter speeds overexpose. A neutral density [[lenses/filters|filter]] cuts the light so you can use them.

## Shutter speed and flash

A camera with a mechanical shutter has a **flash sync speed**, usually 1/200 s or 1/250 s. Faster than that, part of the frame is covered while the flash fires. High-speed sync on the flash works around the limit at reduced power. See [[cameras/shutters|shutters]] for why.

## Shutter speed in video

Video uses a different convention. For natural-looking motion, the shutter speed is set to about double the frame rate: 1/50 s for 24 or 25 fps, 1/60 s for 30 fps. This is the **180-degree rule**. Outdoors that speed is far too slow for the available light, which is why video shooters depend on ND filters. See [[cameras/video|video features]].
