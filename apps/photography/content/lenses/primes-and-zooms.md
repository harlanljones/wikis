# Primes and zooms

A **prime** lens has one focal length. A **zoom** covers a range. Neither is better. They trade convenience against light, size and price.

## The trade

| | Prime | Zoom |
| - | ----- | ---- |
| Framing | You move | You turn a ring |
| Maximum aperture | f/1.2 to f/2.8 | f/2.8 at best for most, f/4 to f/6.3 for compact ones |
| Light gathering | 1.3 to 4 stops more than a typical zoom | Less |
| Background blur | Strong | Moderate, except with f/2.8 zooms at longer focal lengths |
| Size and weight | Small for moderate apertures | Larger, especially constant f/2.8 |
| Price for good quality | From about $200 | From about $500, and $2,000 or more for f/2.8 |
| Lens changes | Frequent | Rare |

## Why use a prime

- **Low light.** An f/1.8 prime gathers more than eight times the light of an f/5.6 kit zoom at the same focal length. That is the difference between ISO 6400 and ISO 800.
- **Background blur** that zooms cannot match.
- **Size.** A camera with a small prime goes places a camera with a big zoom stays home from.
- **Value.** The 50mm f/1.8 is the cheapest sharp lens in every system.
- **It teaches composition.** With a fixed view you start seeing in that focal length before you raise the camera.

## Why use a zoom

- **Speed and flexibility.** Events, travel, hiking, sport and children do not wait for a lens change.
- **Fewer lens changes** means less dust on the sensor and fewer missed moments.
- **Precise framing** when you cannot move: on a cliff edge, in a crowd, behind a rope.
- **One lens instead of three.**

Modern zooms are optically excellent. The old rule that primes are sharper is no longer a good reason on its own.

## The classic zooms

Professional zooms come in two families.

**The f/2.8 "trinity"** covers 16mm to 200mm in three lenses at a constant f/2.8:

- 16-35mm f/2.8 (or 14-24mm or 15-35mm)
- 24-70mm f/2.8
- 70-200mm f/2.8

Each costs roughly $2,000 to $3,000 from the camera maker, or $900 to $1,500 from Sigma or Tamron in their versions (such as 28-75mm f/2.8 and 70-180mm f/2.8).

**The f/4 set** covers the same ground one stop slower at about half the weight and price:

- 14-30mm f/4 or 16-35mm f/4
- 24-105mm f/4 or 24-120mm f/4
- 70-200mm f/4

For landscape and travel photography, where you are stopping down to f/8 anyway, the f/4 lenses are the better choice.

## The classic primes

| Focal length (full frame) | Character |
| ------------------------- | --------- |
| 20mm, 24mm | Wide. Landscapes, astrophotography, vlogging |
| 28mm | The phone view. Street and documentary |
| 35mm | Wide-normal. The storyteller's lens: a subject and their surroundings |
| 40mm, 50mm | Normal. Natural perspective, cheap and fast |
| 85mm | Portrait. Flattering distance and soft backgrounds |
| 105mm, 135mm | Tight portraits, stage, compressed scenes |

On APS-C, divide by 1.5: a 23mm acts like a 35mm, a 33mm or 35mm like a 50mm, a 56mm like an 85mm.

Most systems offer two tiers: compact f/1.8 or f/2 primes at $200 to $800, and large f/1.2 or f/1.4 primes at $1,300 to $3,000.

## A sensible combination

The most common advice, and good advice, is one zoom for coverage and one prime for light:

- **A standard zoom** (the kit lens, or a 24-105mm f/4 or similar)
- **A fast normal prime** (35mm or 50mm f/1.8 on full frame, 23mm or 33mm on APS-C)

Add other lenses only when you find a specific shot you cannot get. See [[lenses/building-a-kit|building a lens kit]].

## Teleconverters

A teleconverter fits between a telephoto lens and the camera and multiplies the focal length, at the cost of light:

| Converter | Focal length | Light lost | A 100-400mm f/4.5-5.6 becomes |
| --------- | ------------ | ---------- | ----------------------------- |
| 1.4x | x1.4 | 1 stop | 140-560mm f/6.3-8 |
| 2x | x2 | 2 stops | 200-800mm f/9-11 |

They only fit specific lenses, mostly telephotos. A 1.4x costs little sharpness on a good lens. A 2x costs more.
