# Filters

Filters screw onto the front of a lens or slide into a holder in front of it. Digital editing has replaced most of the filters film photographers carried. A few do things software cannot.

## The ones that still matter

### Circular polarizer (CPL)

A polarizer blocks reflected light of a particular orientation. You rotate it to control the effect. It:

- Cuts glare from water, wet rocks, leaves and glass
- Lets you see through the surface of a stream or lake
- Deepens blue skies and makes clouds stand out
- Makes foliage look richer by removing its sheen

None of this can be done afterward, because the detail hidden by glare was never recorded.

Things to know:

- The sky effect is strongest at 90 degrees to the sun and absent when the sun is directly ahead or behind.
- On lenses wider than about 24mm, the sky darkens unevenly in a visible band.
- It costs 1 to 2 stops of light.
- Take it off for panoramas and indoors.

### Neutral density (ND)

An ND filter is dark glass that reduces light evenly without changing colour. It lets you use slow shutter speeds or wide apertures in bright light.

| Filter | Stops | Light passed | Typical use |
| ------ | ----- | ------------ | ----------- |
| ND8 (0.9) | 3 | 1/8 | Waterfalls in shade, wide apertures in daylight, video |
| ND64 (1.8) | 6 | 1/64 | Blurred water and clouds around sunrise and sunset |
| ND1000 (3.0) | 10 | 1/1000 | Exposures of 30 seconds or more in full daylight |

ND filters are named three different ways, by factor (ND64), by optical density (1.8) or by stops (6-stop), which is why the table lists all three.

**Variable ND** filters adjust from about 2 to 5 or 6 to 9 stops by rotating. They are the standard tool for video, where the shutter speed is fixed by the [[basics/shutter-speed#Shutter speed in video|180-degree rule]]. Cheap ones show a dark cross pattern at their strongest settings and add a colour cast.

### Graduated ND (GND)

Dark at the top and clear at the bottom, to hold back a bright sky above a darker foreground. Exposure bracketing and the dynamic range of modern sensors have made these far less necessary. Landscape photographers who prefer to get it right in one frame still use them, usually as rectangular filters in a holder.

## Protection filters

A clear or UV filter left on the lens protects the front element from scratches, salt spray and fingerprints.

Opinions differ. In favour: it is cheaper to replace a filter than a front element, and some weather-sealed lenses need one to complete the seal. Against: any extra glass adds a risk of flare and ghosting, especially at night with bright lights in frame, and a lens hood protects against knocks better.

If you use one, buy a good multi-coated one. A cheap filter on an expensive lens undoes what you paid for. Digital sensors already block UV, so "UV" filters are now only protective.

## Specialty filters

- **Diffusion, mist, Pro-Mist.** Soften highlights and lower contrast for a cinematic look. Popular for video.
- **Light pollution filters.** Block the wavelengths of some street lighting for astrophotography. Less effective now that streetlights are broad-spectrum LEDs.
- **Infrared.** Block visible light for infrared photography. Works best with a converted camera.
- **Close-up filters.** Screw-on magnifiers that let a normal lens focus closer. A cheap introduction to macro.

## Sizes

Screw-in filters are sized by thread diameter in millimetres, marked on the lens with the Ø symbol: 49, 52, 55, 58, 62, 67, 72, 77, 82 and 95 are common.

To avoid buying every filter in several sizes:

- Buy filters for your **largest** thread size
- Use inexpensive **step-up rings** to mount them on smaller lenses

Many professional zooms use 77mm or 82mm.

Lenses with bulging front elements, such as most 14-24mm f/2.8 designs, have no filter thread. They need a large clamp-on holder, or filters that fit at the rear of the lens or in a drop-in slot.

**Magnetic filters** attach with a ring that stays on the lens. They are much faster to swap than threaded filters and have become popular for hiking and video.

## What you no longer need

Software or the camera now does the job of:

- Colour correction and warming filters: use [[basics/white-balance|white balance]]
- Coloured filters for black and white: use channel mixing when editing
- Soft focus and star filters, mostly: done when editing

## Quality

A filter becomes part of the optical path. Poor filters reduce sharpness, especially on telephoto lenses, and add colour casts and flare. Well-regarded makers include B+W, Hoya, NiSi, Breakthrough Photography, PolarPro, Kase, Haida and Urth. For a polarizer or ND you will use often, expect to pay $60 to $200.
