# Viewfinders and screens

You compose every photo through the viewfinder or on the rear screen. Their quality affects how pleasant a camera is to use more than most specifications do, and it is hard to judge without trying one.

## Optical and electronic viewfinders

**Optical viewfinder (OVF).** Found on DSLRs. You look through the lens by way of a mirror and prism. The view is immediate and uses no power, but it shows the scene, not the photo: you do not see exposure, white balance or the effect of your settings.

**Electronic viewfinder (EVF).** Found on mirrorless cameras. A tiny high-resolution display shows the live feed from the sensor. You see the exposure before shooting, along with a histogram, focus peaking, a level, and the photo you just took, all without lowering the camera.

Some entry-level and video-first cameras have **no viewfinder** and rely on the rear screen. This saves size and cost. It is a real drawback in bright sun, with long lenses, and for anyone who wears reading glasses.

## EVF specifications

| Spec | What it means | Typical values |
| ---- | ------------- | -------------- |
| Resolution | Counted in dots. Three dots (red, green, blue) make one pixel | 2.36M (1024 x 768), 3.69M (1280 x 960), 5.76M (1600 x 1200), 9.44M (2048 x 1536) |
| Magnification | How large the view appears. Bigger is more immersive | 0.6x (small) to 0.9x (very large), full-frame equivalent |
| Refresh rate | How often the image updates | 60 Hz standard, 120 Hz smooth, 240 Hz on some flagships |
| Eyepoint | How far your eye can be from the eyepiece and still see the whole frame. Matters for glasses | 20 mm or more is comfortable |
| Brightness | Matters for use in sunlight and for showing HDR | Up to 3,000 to 4,000 nits on bright panels |
| Blackout | Whether the view goes dark between frames in a burst | Blackout-free on stacked-sensor bodies |

2.36M dots is the entry level and looks slightly coarse. 3.69M is good. 5.76M and above look close to optical. Magnification matters as much as resolution: a large 3.69M-dot finder is nicer than a small one with more dots.

Check the fine print on high refresh rates. Some cameras lower the resolution to reach their fastest refresh mode.

## Rear screens

Three hinge designs are common, and people have strong preferences.

| Type | Movement | Best for | Weak for |
| ---- | -------- | -------- | -------- |
| Tilting | Up and down, staying behind the lens | Waist-level and low-angle stills. Quick to use | Vertical shots, filming yourself |
| Fully articulating (vari-angle) | Flips out to the side and rotates | Video, selfies, vertical shots at odd angles | Slower to deploy. Sits off the lens axis. Can clash with cables and L-brackets |
| Multi-axis | Tilts and also flips | Everything | Thicker and more complex. Found mainly on high-end bodies |

Screen resolution is also given in dots: 1.04M is basic, 1.62M to 2.1M is good. Size is usually 3.0 or 3.2 inches.

**Touch control** varies more than you would expect. The best implementations let you operate the whole menu, drag the focus point with your thumb while your eye is at the viewfinder, and swipe through images like a phone. Some cameras limit touch to focus point selection.

## Top screens and dials

A small status display on the top plate, found on higher-end bodies, shows settings at a glance without waking the main screen. It is handy on a tripod.

More important is the number of **control dials**. Two dials (one for aperture, one for shutter speed) plus an easy way to set exposure compensation is the minimum for comfortable manual control. Entry-level cameras with a single dial force more button presses. Fujifilm, the Nikon Zf and Zfc, and the OM System OM-3 use dedicated marked dials for shutter speed and ISO in the style of film cameras.

## Things to check in a shop

- Can you see the whole viewfinder image with your glasses on?
- Is the dioptre adjustment enough for your eyesight?
- Does the screen hinge suit how you shoot?
- Can you reach the main dials and the autofocus joystick without shifting your grip?
- How does it feel with the lens you would use most? A small body with a large lens is tiring.
