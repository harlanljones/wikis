# Memory cards and batteries

Cards and batteries are the unglamorous parts that stop a shoot when they fail. The labelling on memory cards is confusing, and the wrong card can limit an expensive camera.

## Card formats

| Format | Used by | Typical real-world speed | Cost |
| ------ | ------- | ------------------------ | ---- |
| SD, UHS-I | Entry-level cameras, older bodies | Up to about 90 MB/s | Low |
| SD, UHS-II | Most mid-range and many high-end bodies | Up to about 280 MB/s | Moderate |
| CFexpress Type A | Sony high-end bodies. The slot also accepts SD | 700 to 1,800 MB/s | High |
| CFexpress Type B | Canon, Nikon, Panasonic, Fujifilm high-end bodies | 1,500 to 3,500 MB/s | Moderate to high |
| XQD | Older Nikon and Sony bodies. Same shape as CFexpress Type B | About 400 MB/s | Being phased out |
| microSD | A few compact bodies, drones, action cameras | As SD | Low |

UHS-II cards have a second row of contacts on the back. They work in UHS-I cameras at the slower speed, and UHS-I cards work in UHS-II slots, so the two are interchangeable when speed is not critical.

CFexpress Type B cameras can often use XQD cards after a firmware update. Type A and Type B are different sizes and do not fit each other's slots.

## Reading an SD card label

An SD card carries several speed marks. Only one of them is important for cameras.

| Mark | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| The large number, such as "300 MB/s" | Maximum **read** speed. Affects how fast you copy to a computer. Says little about writing |
| **V30, V60, V90** | Guaranteed minimum sustained **write** speed in MB/s. This is the one that matters |
| U1, U3 | Older marks: 10 and 30 MB/s minimum |
| Class 10 (a C with a 10) | Older still: 10 MB/s minimum |
| I or II | UHS-I or UHS-II bus |
| SDHC, SDXC | Capacity standard: up to 32 GB, or 64 GB to 2 TB |

**What to buy:**

- **V30 UHS-I.** Enough for stills on entry-level cameras and for 4K at standard bitrates.
- **V60 UHS-II.** The sensible default for most cameras. Clears the buffer quickly after a burst and handles 10-bit 4K.
- **V90 UHS-II.** For high-bitrate All-Intra 4K, some 6K and 8K modes, and the fastest bursts. Roughly twice the price of V60.

For CFexpress, the equivalent guarantee is **VPG200** or **VPG400** (200 or 400 MB/s sustained). Cameras that record raw video internally often publish a list of approved cards. Follow it.

## Why write speed matters

When you shoot a burst, images pile up in the camera's **buffer** memory faster than they can be written to the card. When the buffer fills, the camera slows or stops. A faster card empties the buffer sooner. For single shots, card speed is irrelevant.

For video, a card that cannot sustain the bitrate causes the recording to stop.

## Two slots or one

Dual card slots let you:

- **Write every file to both cards** for instant backup. This is why professionals who cannot repeat an event insist on two slots.
- **Overflow** to the second card when the first fills.
- **Separate** raw files from JPEGs, or stills from video.

If the two slots are different types, such as CFexpress plus SD, backup mode runs at the speed of the slower card.

Single-slot cameras are fine for personal work. Card failures are rare with good cards, though not unknown.

## Looking after cards

- Buy known brands (SanDisk, Lexar, ProGrade, Sony, Kingston, Delkin, Angelbird, OWC) from reputable sellers. Counterfeit cards are common on marketplaces.
- **Format cards in the camera,** not on a computer, and do it after you have backed up.
- Do not delete individual images in the camera to free space when the card is nearly full. Swap cards.
- Several medium-sized cards are safer than one huge one.
- Replace cards that have ever produced an error.

## How much capacity

| Camera | Raw file | Shots on 64 GB | Shots on 128 GB |
| ------ | -------- | -------------- | --------------- |
| 24 megapixels | About 30 MB | About 2,000 | About 4,000 |
| 45 megapixels | About 55 MB | About 1,100 | About 2,200 |
| 61 megapixels | About 75 MB | About 800 | About 1,600 |

For video, 4K at 100 Mbps uses about 45 GB per hour. High-quality 10-bit modes at 400 to 600 Mbps use 180 to 270 GB per hour, and raw video several times that.

## Batteries

Battery life is quoted in shots per charge under the **CIPA** standard, which uses flash, reviews every image and powers the camera off and on between shots. Real use usually gives two to three times the rating for stills.

| Camera type | CIPA rating | Realistic day |
| ----------- | ----------- | ------------- |
| DSLR, optical viewfinder | 800 to 2,000+ | Rarely need a spare |
| Mirrorless with a large battery | 500 to 750 | One battery for a normal day, a spare for events |
| Compact mirrorless with a small battery | 250 to 400 | Carry a spare |

Things that help:

- **USB-C charging and power delivery.** Nearly all current cameras charge over USB-C, and many can run from a power bank while shooting. This has mostly replaced carrying a charger when travelling.
- **Buy one spare battery,** preferably the manufacturer's. Third-party batteries are cheaper and mostly fine, but some report charge inaccurately or are rejected after firmware updates.
- **Cold drains batteries.** Keep a spare in an inside pocket in winter.
- **Viewfinders and screens use the power.** Lower refresh rates, shorter auto-off times and airplane mode extend life.
- **Battery grips** hold a second battery and add a vertical shutter button, at the cost of size.

Manufacturers keep the same battery across many bodies, which is convenient when you upgrade: Canon LP-E6 series, Nikon EN-EL15 series, Sony NP-FZ100, Fujifilm NP-W235, Panasonic DMW-BLK22. Sony introduced a new, larger NP-SA100 with the a7R VI in 2026.
