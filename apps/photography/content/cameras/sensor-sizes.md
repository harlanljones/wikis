# Sensor sizes

Sensor size is the most consequential choice in a camera system. It sets how much light the camera can gather, how easily it blurs a background, and how big, heavy and expensive the lenses are.

Manufacturer names vary. Nikon calls full frame **FX** and APS-C **DX**. Canon and Sony lens names mark APS-C with **RF-S** and **E** (as opposed to **RF** and **FE**). "Full frame" itself just means the size of a 35mm film negative.

The inch-based names for small sensors, such as "1-inch" and "1/1.3-inch", are a holdover from video camera tubes. They are not the sensor's real diagonal, which is about two thirds of the number.

## What a bigger sensor gives you

**More total light.** At the same f-number and shutter speed, every sensor gets the same light per square millimetre. A bigger sensor has more square millimetres, so it collects more light in total. More light means less noise. Full frame has roughly a one-stop advantage over APS-C and two stops over Micro Four Thirds. In other words, full frame at ISO 6400 looks about like APS-C at ISO 3200 or Micro Four Thirds at ISO 1600.

**Shallower depth of field.** For the same framing and f-number, a bigger sensor blurs the background more. See [[basics/depth-of-field|depth of field]].

**More dynamic range at base ISO,** because larger sensors can hold more light before they clip. See [[cameras/dynamic-range-and-noise|dynamic range and noise]].

## What a smaller sensor gives you

**Smaller, lighter, cheaper lenses.** This is the big one, and it grows with focal length. A 600mm-equivalent view needs a 600mm lens on full frame, a 400mm on APS-C and a 300mm on Micro Four Thirds.

**More reach per dollar.** Wildlife and bird photographers often choose APS-C or Micro Four Thirds on purpose.

**More depth of field,** which helps for macro, landscapes and video where you want everything sharp.

**Lower cost** for the body and for every lens that follows.

## Equivalence

To compare lenses across formats, multiply both the focal length **and the f-number** by the crop factor. The result is the full-frame lens that gives the same framing, the same depth of field and the same total light.

| On this sensor | This lens | Behaves like, on full frame |
| -------------- | --------- | --------------------------- |
| Micro Four Thirds | 12-40mm f/2.8 | 24-80mm f/5.6 |
| APS-C | 16-55mm f/2.8 | 24-83mm f/4.2 |
| APS-C | 56mm f/1.4 | 84mm f/2.1 |
| Medium format | 80mm f/1.7 | 63mm f/1.3 |

Two things equivalence does **not** mean:

- It does not change exposure. An f/2.8 lens gives f/2.8 shutter speeds on any format.
- It does not make smaller formats pointless. A Micro Four Thirds 12-40mm f/2.8 is much smaller than a full-frame 24-70mm f/2.8. It is closer in size, and in results, to a full-frame 24-70mm f/4 or slower.

The honest summary: a smaller format gives you a smaller kit, and a larger format gives you the option of more light when you are willing to carry it.

## Which to choose

| If you mostly... | Consider |
| ---------------- | -------- |
| Hike, travel, or want to carry the camera everywhere | APS-C or Micro Four Thirds |
| Shoot indoors, at events, or at night without flash | Full frame |
| Photograph birds and wildlife on a budget | APS-C or Micro Four Thirds |
| Want strong background blur for portraits | Full frame |
| Make very large landscape or studio prints | High-resolution full frame, or medium format |
| Are starting out and unsure | APS-C, or an entry-level full-frame body |

Full-frame bodies now start at around $1,000 to $1,400 new, so the entry price is no longer the barrier it was. The lenses are still where the cost and weight add up.
