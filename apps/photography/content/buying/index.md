# Buying guide

Choosing a camera is easier than the number of models suggests. Almost every current camera is good. The work is in matching one to what you photograph, how much you want to carry and what you want to spend, then stopping.

Recommendations here were last checked in **November 2026**.

## The short version

1. **Set a total budget** that includes a lens, a memory card and a spare battery. The body should be half of it or less.
2. **Decide how much you are willing to carry.** A smaller camera that comes with you beats a better one left at home.
3. **Name your main subject.** Moving subjects and low light are what cost money. Landscapes and travel do not.
4. **Pick a [[systems|system]]** by looking at the lenses you will want next.
5. **Handle the shortlist** in a shop if you can.
6. **Buy, and stop reading reviews.** Then go and use it.

## What matters and what does not

| Worth paying for | Why |
| ---------------- | --- |
| A good lens | The biggest effect on image quality |
| Recent autofocus | The biggest effect on your hit rate with people, pets and wildlife |
| A viewfinder | Essential in bright light and with long lenses |
| In-body stabilization | Sharper handheld shots in low light with any lens |
| A grip and controls that suit you | You will use them thousands of times |
| Weather sealing, if you shoot outdoors a lot | Peace of mind in rain and dust |

| Usually not worth paying for | Why |
| ---------------------------- | --- |
| More than about 24 megapixels | Unless you crop heavily or print very large |
| 8K video | Unless you know why you need it |
| The highest burst rate | 10 to 15 fps is plenty for most action |
| The latest model when the previous one is discounted | Generational gains are often small |
| A flagship body with a kit lens | The lens is holding it back |

## When to upgrade

Replace a camera when it is preventing a specific photograph: it cannot focus on what you shoot, it is too noisy in the light you work in, or it is too heavy to take along. If you cannot name the photograph, a new lens, a trip or a class will improve your pictures more than a new body.

A good rule is to skip at least one generation. The step from a Mark II to a Mark III is rarely dramatic. The step across two generations usually is.
