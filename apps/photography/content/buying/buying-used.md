# Buying used

Used equipment is the best way to stretch a budget. Lenses in particular have few parts that wear and can be bought used with little risk. Bodies need a little more care.

## What to buy used and what to buy new

| Item | Used? | Why |
| ---- | ----- | --- |
| Lenses | Yes | Durable, slow to lose value, easy to inspect. Often 30 to 50 percent less than new |
| Camera bodies one generation old | Yes | Big discounts once a successor is out |
| Older camera bodies | With care | Check the autofocus generation and whether repairs are still offered |
| Tripods, bags, filters | Yes | Little to go wrong |
| Batteries | No | Capacity fades with age |
| Memory cards | No | Wear and counterfeits make it a poor saving |

## Where to buy

| Source | Advantages | Drawbacks |
| ------ | ---------- | --------- |
| Specialist used dealers (MPB, KEH, Map Camera, the used departments of B&H, Adorama, Roberts and local camera shops) | Graded condition, a warranty of 6 to 12 months, returns | Prices a little higher |
| Manufacturer refurbished (Canon, Nikon, Sony, Fujifilm and OM System stores) | Inspected by the maker, with a warranty | Limited stock |
| Marketplaces (eBay, Facebook Marketplace, forum classifieds) | Lowest prices | No warranty, some scams, condition as described by the seller |
| Friends and local camera clubs | You know the history | Limited choice |

For a first used purchase, a dealer with a return period is worth the small premium.

## Checking a camera body

**Shutter count.** The mechanical shutter is the main wearing part. Entry-level bodies are rated for about 100,000 to 150,000 actuations, mid-range for 200,000 to 300,000, and professional bodies for 400,000 or more. The count is stored in the camera and can be read from a file with free tools, or from the menu on some models. A count that is high for the model's age points to professional use. Cameras used mostly with the electronic shutter can have very low mechanical counts and still be heavily used.

**Sensor.** Set f/16, photograph a plain bright surface out of focus, and look at the image. Soft dark spots are dust and can be cleaned. Lines, bright dots that appear at every setting, or marks with hard edges are scratches or defects.

**Controls.** Press every button, turn every dial, move the joystick, open every door.

**Screen and viewfinder.** Check for dead pixels, scratches and delamination. Test the hinge. Confirm the eye sensor switches between screen and viewfinder.

**Mount and contacts.** The mount should be clean and unbent, and the lens should lock without play.

**Ports and card slots.** Look for bent pins in CFexpress and old CompactFlash slots. Test USB charging.

**Signs of trouble.** Corrosion in the battery compartment, fogging inside the viewfinder, rubber grips peeling, or a salt crust in the seams suggest water exposure.

**Firmware and accounts.** Reset the camera and check it is not locked to a previous owner's account or region.

## Checking a lens

Shine a phone torch through the lens from behind and look through the front.

| Finding | Verdict |
| ------- | ------- |
| A few specks of dust inside | Normal and harmless |
| Light cleaning marks on the front element | Usually harmless |
| Fungus: fine threads or web patterns | Avoid. It spreads and etches the glass |
| Haze: a milky film | Avoid. It lowers contrast |
| Scratches on the **rear** element | Avoid. These show in images more than front scratches |
| Oil on the aperture blades | Avoid. The aperture may stick |
| Balsam separation: rainbow patches at the edges of an element | Avoid |

Then check that:

- Autofocus is quick and quiet across the range, with no grinding
- Zoom and focus rings turn smoothly, without stiff spots or wobble
- The aperture closes and opens briskly when you shoot at f/16
- Stabilization engages and does not rattle or jump
- A test shot of a flat, detailed surface is evenly sharp on both sides. One soft side means a decentred element

## Which generations to buy

Mirrorless autofocus improved sharply in steps. For moving subjects, prefer bodies from the newer side of these lines.

| Brand | Good used value | Be aware |
| ----- | --------------- | -------- |
| Sony | a7 III, a7R III and newer. a6100, a6400, a6600 | The a7 and a7 II have short battery life and slow autofocus |
| Canon | EOS R6, R5, RP, R8, R10, R7 | The original EOS R and RP have older autofocus. Fine for still subjects |
| Nikon | Z6 II, Z7 II, Z5, Zf, Z50 | First-generation Z6 and Z7 have one card slot and weaker tracking, but are bargains for landscape |
| Fujifilm | X-T3, X-T4, X-S10, X-H2S | Earlier bodies track slowly |
| Panasonic | S5 II, G9 II, GH6 | Bodies before 2023 use contrast-based autofocus |
| OM System and Olympus | E-M1 Mark III, OM-1, E-M5 Mark III | Older menus are hard to navigate |
| DSLRs | Nikon D750, D810, D850, D7200, D500. Canon 6D, 5D Mark III and IV, 80D, 90D | See [[systems/dslr-systems\|DSLR systems today]] |

For landscapes, portraits and travel, older bodies are entirely adequate. The sensors of 2016 are still very good.

## Grey market

"Grey market" or "import" items are genuine products brought in from another country outside the official distributor. They are cheaper, and the manufacturer's local warranty does not apply. Some makers will refuse to repair them even at your expense. Know what you are buying.

## Selling and trading in

Keep the box, caps and charger, and your gear will sell for more. Trading in to a dealer is quick and pays less. Selling privately takes effort and pays more. Because good lenses hold value, the real cost of owning one for a few years is often small.
