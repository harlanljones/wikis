# Chaw'se, Indian Grinding Rock

> **On your hike list:** Indian Grinding Rock (Chaw'se), Indian Grinding Rock State Historic Park, Pine Grove (about 1.5 miles of trails)

In a meadow ringed by valley oaks is a flat slab of pale marble about 170 feet long. It is pocked with 1,185 holes, each worn into the rock by women pounding acorns. *Chaw'se* is the Northern Sierra Miwok word for a grinding rock. This is the largest collection of bedrock mortars known in North America.

## The acorn economy

Across most of California, acorns were the staple food. That fact explains a good deal about what the state was like before 1769. California Indians, with a few exceptions along the Colorado River, did not farm in the usual sense. They did not need to. The oaks produced a huge crop every fall, and acorns keep for years in a dry granary. One estimate is that acorns supplied half or more of the calories for three quarters of the people in the state.

The catch is that raw acorns are full of bitter tannin. Making them edible took skill and work:

1. **Gather** in autumn. A family might collect a ton or more. Black oak acorns were the favorite here.
2. **Dry and store** them in a *chakka*, a basket-like granary on stilts, lined with wormwood to keep insects out.
3. **Crack and pound** the kernels into flour in a mortar hole with a stone pestle.
4. **Leach** the flour by pouring water through it in a basin of sand until the bitterness is gone.
5. **Cook** it in a watertight basket by dropping in hot stones and stirring, to make soup, mush or bread.

Each hole in the rock started as a shallow cup. When one wore too deep to work well, it was abandoned and a new one begun. The 1,185 holes are the record of many generations of women working side by side, talking, with children playing nearby. It was the social center of the village.

## The carvings

Among the mortars are 363 petroglyphs: circles, spoked wheels, wavy lines, and human and animal tracks. Some are thought to be two or three thousand years old. Finding rock carvings and mortars together on one surface is rare. The marble is soft, and both are slowly weathering away, which is why you look from a platform instead of walking on the rock.

## The roundhouse

The large building with the conical roof is a ceremonial roundhouse, a *hun'ge*, sixty feet across. It is not an exhibit. Miwok people and their neighbors use it for dances and gatherings through the year, including the Big Time, held each September to celebrate the acorn harvest. Four great oak posts hold up the roof, and the door faces east.

## What the Gold Rush did here

This village sat in the middle of the Mother Lode. After 1848, miners poured into every creek in Miwok country. They muddied the salmon streams, shot the game, cut the oaks and let hogs loose to eat the acorns. They also attacked villages. The Miwok population fell by something like ninety percent in a few decades.

Some families held on by working on ranches and keeping their gatherings going quietly. The land around the rock was farmed by settlers who left it largely alone, and it became a state park in the 1960s at the urging of local Miwok people and their neighbors.

## Look for on the trail

- **Valley oaks** in the meadow, some several hundred years old. They are the largest oaks in North America.
- **The reconstructed village**: cedar-bark houses called *u'macha*, acorn granaries, and a field for a hard-fought ball game something like soccer.
- **Acorn woodpeckers**, clown-faced birds that store thousands of acorns in holes they drill in a single "granary tree."
- **Soaproot, redbud and deergrass** along the nature trail, all basket and tool plants. Many were kept in good condition by regular burning.
- **The museum**, built to echo a roundhouse, with baskets from a dozen Sierra peoples.
