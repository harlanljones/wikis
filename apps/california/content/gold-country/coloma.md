# Coloma

> **On your hike list:** Marshall Gold Discovery Monument Loop, Marshall Gold Discovery State Historic Park (1.5 mile loop)

On the morning of January 24, 1848, a moody carpenter from New Jersey walked along a ditch beside the American River and picked up a few bright flakes. Within a few years a quarter of a million people would come to California because of them. Almost none of the people standing at the mill that morning got rich.

## Sutter needs lumber

John Sutter was a Swiss shopkeeper who had left his debts and his family in Europe and talked the Mexican governor into granting him nearly 50,000 acres at the meeting of the Sacramento and American rivers. He called it New Helvetia. By 1847 he had a fort, thousands of cattle, and hundreds of Native workers, many of them held in conditions close to slavery. What he lacked was sawn lumber.

He hired James Marshall to build a sawmill 45 miles up the American River in a valley the Nisenan people called Cullumah. The crew was a mix of Nisenan laborers and veterans of the Mormon Battalion working their way back to Utah. The mill was nearly finished, but the tailrace, the ditch that carried water away from the wheel, was too shallow. Each night they opened the gate to let the river scour it deeper. Marshall inspected it each morning.

## "I have found it"

"My eye was caught by something shining in the bottom of the ditch," Marshall recalled later. He picked up the pieces and pounded one between rocks. It flattened without breaking. The camp cook boiled another in a kettle of lye soap, and it came out bright. Marshall rode to the fort through a rainstorm, and he and Sutter tested the metal behind a locked door with nitric acid and an encyclopedia. It was gold.

One of the Mormon workers, Henry Bigler, wrote in his diary: "This day some kind of mettle was found in the tail race that looks like goald." That entry is how we know the date.

Nine days later, and with nobody in Washington or Mexico City aware of the find, Mexico signed the treaty that handed California to the United States.

## The secret gets out

Sutter wanted it kept quiet until the mill was done. It lasted about six weeks. A storekeeper and newspaper owner named Sam Brannan first stocked his store at the fort with every pick, pan and shovel he could buy. Then in May he walked through San Francisco waving a bottle of gold dust and shouting, "Gold! Gold! Gold from the American River!" The town emptied in days. Brannan became California's first millionaire without ever swinging a pick.

## Nobody at the mill won

Sutter's workers quit. Gold seekers trampled his fields, butchered his cattle and squatted on his land. He went bankrupt and spent his last years in Pennsylvania, petitioning Congress for compensation. He died in 1880, two days after Congress adjourned without acting on his claim.

Marshall was pushed off his own land, failed at mining and at growing grapes, and ended up selling his autograph. He died poor in 1885 in a cabin nearby. Five years later the state built the monument you climb to, with a bronze Marshall on top pointing at the spot where he found the gold. He is buried underneath it.

The Nisenan fared worst of all. Miners overran their villages along the river within months. Disease, hunger and killing reduced a people of several thousand to a few hundred by the 1860s. Their descendants are still in the foothills, and still working to get federal recognition restored.

## Look for on the trail

- **The mill replica**, built from Marshall's own drawing. The original site, by the river, is marked with a stone cairn. The river has moved since 1848.
- **The Wah Hop and Man Lee stores**, stone buildings from the 1850s. After the first rush moved on, Coloma had a large Chinese community reworking the gravels.
- **Marshall's cabin**, on the hill below the monument.
- **Bedrock mortars** near the river, where Nisenan women ground acorns.
- **The statue's finger.** Follow where it points.
