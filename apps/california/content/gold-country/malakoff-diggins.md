# Malakoff Diggins

> **On your hike list:** Diggins Loop Trail, Malakoff Diggins State Historic Park, near Nevada City (2.8 mile loop)

The pit at Malakoff looks like a small Bryce Canyon, with fluted cliffs striped pink, white and rust. It is a little over a hundred and forty years old, and people made it with water. It is also where one of the first great environmental court cases in America began.

## Gold in dead rivers

By the mid-1850s the easy gold in the stream beds was gone. Miners noticed that some of the richest gravel lay high on the ridges, nowhere near a modern river. They had found the beds of rivers that flowed here about 50 million years ago, when the land was lower and the climate tropical. Later volcanic flows buried the old channels, and the rising Sierra lifted them into the hills. These ancient gravels were hundreds of feet thick and salted with gold from top to bottom, but thinly. Digging them by hand made no sense.

## The monitor

In 1853, near Nevada City, a miner named Edward Matteson attached a nozzle to a hose and aimed it at a gravel bank. That was the beginning of hydraulic mining.

Within twenty years it was heavy industry. Companies built reservoirs high in the mountains and hundreds of miles of ditches and flumes to bring water to the mines. The water dropped through iron pipes into cast-iron cannons called monitors. A big monitor threw a jet from an eight-inch nozzle at more than a hundred miles an hour, with enough force to kill a man two hundred feet away and to move boulders. Crews played the jets on the hillsides around the clock, by torchlight at night. The mud ran through long sluices lined with mercury, which grabbed the gold, and everything else went downstream.

The North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company, backed by San Francisco bankers, ran the biggest operation of all here. It drove a drainage tunnel a mile and a half through solid rock, used more water in a day than a large city, and strung what is often called the first long-distance telephone line in the world, in 1878, to coordinate its dams and ditches. The pit it carved is about 7,000 feet long, as much as 3,000 feet wide and, in its day, nearly 600 feet deep.

## Where the mountains went

Hydraulic mines sent well over a billion cubic yards of mud, sand and gravel down the Yuba, Bear, Feather and American rivers. That is several times the volume dug for the Panama Canal.

The debris filled the river channels in the Central Valley. The bed of the Yuba at Marysville rose until it stood higher than the streets of the town, held back by levees. In 1875 the levees broke and Marysville went under. Farms and orchards were buried under sterile sand and "slickens." Steamboats could no longer reach Sacramento in the dry season, and the mud moved on into San Francisco Bay, where it filled in shallows that are still there.

## The Sawyer Decision

Farmers organized as the Anti-Debris Association, and in 1882 a Marysville landowner named Edwards Woodruff sued North Bloomfield in federal court. Mining was the most powerful industry in the state, and it had built California. Few people expected the farmers to win.

On January 7, 1884, Judge Lorenzo Sawyer, a former miner, read a 225-page decision that took three and a half hours. He ruled that the companies could mine all they liked but could not dump their debris into the rivers. In practice that ended hydraulic mining in the Sierra. The ruling held that an industry has no right to destroy other people's property downstream, and it is often called the first major environmental decision in American law.

The town of North Bloomfield, once called Humbug, shrank from about 1,500 people to a handful.

## Look for on the trail

- **The colors.** White quartz gravel at the bottom of the old river, red and yellow clays stained by iron above.
- **The pit floor**, now a marsh of willows and cattails on debris that has filled much of the original hole.
- **The Hiller Tunnel**, a 556-foot drain you can walk through with a flashlight when the water is low.
- **A monitor** on display in North Bloomfield, sometimes fired for visitors.
- **Petrified wood** from the forests that lined the ancient river.
- **Mercury warnings.** Much of the quicksilver used here is still in the watershed, and still in the fish.
