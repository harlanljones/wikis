# First Peoples

Before 1769, California was home to more people, speaking more languages, than any other part of North America north of Mexico. Every trail on the hike list crosses the homeland of a living people. This page is the short version of who they were, what happened, and where things stand.

## A crowded, varied place

Something like 300,000 people lived in what is now California when the Spanish arrived. They spoke around a hundred distinct languages, belonging to more than twenty language families. For comparison, nearly all the languages of Europe, from Ireland to Russia, belong to just a handful. A day's walk could take you to a place where the language was as different from yours as English is from Chinese.

People lived in several hundred small independent nations, usually a main village and its outlying camps, with firmly understood boundaries. Trade, marriage and ceremony tied them together across long distances. Shell beads from the [[south-coast/channel-islands|Channel Islands]] and obsidian from the [[eastern-sierra/mono-lake|eastern Sierra]] crossed the whole state.

## Tending the wild

Early visitors described California as a natural park and its people as living off the land without working it. Both ideas were wrong. The oak savannas, flowery meadows and open forests were the result of constant, skilled management.

The main tool was fire. People burned grasslands to bring fresh growth for deer and to make seed crops easier to gather, burned under oaks to kill acorn pests, and burned patches of shrubs to get the long straight shoots needed for baskets. They also pruned, weeded, transplanted and sowed. The staple food across most of the state was the acorn, which you can read about at [[gold-country/chawse|Chaw'se]].

California baskets are among the finest ever made anywhere. Some are woven tightly enough to hold water and be used for cooking, and Pomo weavers made baskets the size of a pea.

## The missions, 1769 to 1834

Spain's twenty-one missions were meant to turn coastal peoples into Catholic farmers. About 80,000 people were baptized. Once inside they could not leave, and their labor built the whole colony. Crowding and unfamiliar diseases killed them in great numbers. In the mission zone the Native population fell by something like three quarters in sixty-five years.

People resisted, by running away, by raiding the herds, and by open revolt, as at [[central-coast/la-purisima|La Purísima]] in 1824. When Mexico closed the missions in the 1830s, the land promised to the mission Indians went mostly to ranchers, and many survivors became ranch hands on what had been their own country. [[bay-area/mission-peak|Mission Peak]] has the story of one mission.

## The catastrophe, 1846 to 1873

About 150,000 Native people were living in California when the United States took it in 1846. By 1873 about 30,000 remained.

Disease and starvation, as miners destroyed the streams and the game, caused many of the deaths. But many were deliberate. The state's first governor, Peter Burnett, told the legislature in 1851 "that a war of extermination will continue to be waged between the two races until the Indian race becomes extinct." The state paid militias to hunt Indians and billed the federal government for the cost. Towns offered bounties for scalps. An 1850 law, called an act "for the government and protection of Indians," allowed Native children and adults to be bound over to white settlers as unpaid servants, and thousands were kidnapped and sold.

In 1851 and 1852 federal agents signed eighteen treaties with California tribes, promising about seven and a half million acres. Under pressure from the new state, the Senate refused to ratify them and ordered them sealed. The tribes were not told. They had given up their claims and got nothing, which is why California has so many tiny reservations, called rancherias, and so many tribes with no land at all.

The [[volcano-country/lava-beds|Modoc War]] of 1872 to 1873 was the last armed resistance. Historians increasingly use the word genocide for this period, and in 2019 Governor Gavin Newsom did too, in a formal apology on behalf of the state.

## Still here

California today has about 110 federally recognized tribes, more than any other state, and dozens more that are seeking recognition, including the Muwekma Ohlone of the Bay Area and the Nisenan of [[gold-country/coloma|Coloma]]. More people of Native American descent live in California than in any other state.

Some recent events that touch places on the hike list:

- The Yurok Tribe brought condors back to the [[north-coast/redwood-national-park|redwood coast]] in 2022.
- The Esselen Tribe regained land at [[central-coast/big-sur|Big Sur]] in 2020.
- The Timbisha Shoshone won a homeland inside [[desert/death-valley|Death Valley]] National Park in 2000.
- The Wiyot Tribe got back most of Tuluwat Island in Humboldt Bay in 2019, the site of an 1860 massacre. It was the first time a U.S. city returned land to a tribe without being made to.
- Parks across the state are working with tribes to bring back [[big-picture/fire|cultural burning]].
- The Chumash paddle to [[south-coast/channel-islands|Limuw]] again.

## On the trail

- **Bedrock mortars**, round holes worn in flat rock near water and oaks, are everywhere once you start looking.
- **Shell middens**, dark soil full of broken shell on coastal bluffs, are village sites and often burial grounds. They are protected by law. Look and walk on.
- **Place names.** Yosemite, Tahoe, Tamalpais, Mono, Shasta, Napa, Sonoma, Malibu, Ojai, Yuba and Tehachapi all come from Native languages.
