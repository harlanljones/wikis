# How California Was Built

California looks the way it does because it sits on the edge of a continent where plates of the Earth's crust have been colliding, diving and sliding past each other for more than 200 million years. Nearly every landscape on the hike list is a result. Here is the whole story in six steps.

## 1. The edge of the continent was once in Nevada

Half a billion years ago the west coast of North America ran through what is now Nevada and eastern California. Most of the rock that makes up the state today did not exist yet. A few scraps of that older world survive, such as the banded gneiss of Ryan Mountain at [[desert/joshua-tree|Joshua Tree]] and the pale dolomite under the [[eastern-sierra/bristlecone-pines|bristlecone pines]].

California was added on, piece by piece, from the west.

## 2. Subduction builds the state (about 200 to 30 million years ago)

For most of the age of the dinosaurs, an ocean plate called the Farallon Plate pushed against the continent and dived under it, a process called subduction. That one process built three belts of rock side by side, and you can still drive across them in an afternoon.

- **The Coast Ranges** are what got scraped off. As the ocean plate went down, its top layer of sea-floor mud, chert and lava was peeled off against the edge of the continent and piled up in a jumbled mass geologists call the Franciscan Complex. The red chert at [[bay-area/lands-end|Lands End]], the serpentinite of [[bay-area/mount-tamalpais|Mount Tamalpais]] and the blueschist of [[bay-area/angel-island|Angel Island]] are all part of it.
- **The Central Valley** was a deep sea basin between the pile and the volcanoes. It filled with miles of sediment.
- **The Sierra Nevada** was the line of volcanoes. Sixty miles down, the sinking plate released water into the hot rock above it, which melted. Most of the magma never reached the surface. It cooled slowly underground into granite, in blob after blob, mainly between about 120 and 85 million years ago. The same belt of granite continues south through the [[desert/san-jacinto|Peninsular Ranges]].

Hot fluids moving through cracks around the cooling granite left veins of quartz laced with gold, the source of the [[big-picture/gold-rush|Mother Lode]].

Over tens of millions of years, erosion stripped off the volcanoes and several miles of rock under them and exposed the granite. That is what you are standing on in [[sierra-nevada/yosemite-valley|Yosemite]], at [[sierra-nevada/lake-tahoe|Tahoe]] and on [[eastern-sierra/mount-whitney|Mount Whitney]].

## 3. The San Andreas Fault takes over (about 30 million years ago to now)

Eventually the continent overran the spreading ridge on the far side of the Farallon Plate and came into contact with the next plate over, the Pacific Plate. The Pacific Plate is not moving toward North America. It is moving northwest, alongside it. Where the two met, subduction stopped and a sideways-slipping boundary began. That boundary is the San Andreas Fault system.

The fault runs about 750 miles, from the Salton Sea to Cape Mendocino. Everything west of it, including Los Angeles, Monterey and Point Reyes, is on the Pacific Plate and is moving northwest at about two inches a year relative to the rest of the continent.

Two inches a year for 20 million years is a long way.

- [[central-coast/pinnacles|Pinnacles]] is half of a volcano whose other half is 195 miles to the southeast.
- The granite of [[bay-area/point-reyes|Point Reyes]] and [[central-coast/point-lobos|Point Lobos]] started out near the southern end of the Sierra.
- The fault's bends squeeze up mountains, including the [[central-coast/santa-cruz-mountains|Santa Cruz Mountains]], [[bay-area/mission-peak|Mission Peak]] and [[desert/san-jacinto|San Jacinto]].
- Its grinding makes underground dams of clay that force water up as springs, like the palm oases of [[desert/anza-borrego|Anza-Borrego]].

A leftover piece of the Farallon Plate, the Gorda Plate, is still subducting north of Cape Mendocino. The point offshore where the three plates meet is the reason the [[north-coast/lost-coast|Lost Coast]] is rising so fast. The Gorda Plate is also why California still has active volcanoes, and why they are all in the north: [[volcano-country|Shasta and Lassen]] stand above it.

## 4. The crust stretches (about 17 million years ago to now)

East of the Sierra, the continent has been pulled apart, nearly doubling in width between Reno and Salt Lake City. Stretched crust breaks into long blocks. Some drop to make valleys and some tilt up to make mountains. This is the Basin and Range, and its western edge is the most dramatic scenery in the state.

- The Sierra Nevada is a single huge block, hinged on the west and lifted on the east, which is why its west slope is a long ramp and its east side is a cliff. The view from [[sierra-nevada/donner-pass|Donner Pass]] and the wall behind [[eastern-sierra/manzanar|Manzanar]] both show it.
- [[desert/death-valley|Death Valley]] and the Owens Valley are blocks that dropped.
- [[sierra-nevada/lake-tahoe|Lake Tahoe]] fills another.
- Thin, stretched crust lets magma rise. That accounts for the Long Valley supervolcano, [[eastern-sierra/devils-postpile|Devils Postpile]] and the craters at [[eastern-sierra/mono-lake|Mono Lake]].

Geologists still argue about exactly when the Sierra reached its present height. Much of the tilting seems to have happened in the last five to ten million years, and it is still going on. The 1872 Lone Pine earthquake raised the range a few more feet in one night.

The rising mountains made the deserts. Storms from the Pacific drop their water climbing the west slope, and almost none is left for the other side.

## 5. Ice (about 2.5 million to 12,000 years ago)

During the ice ages, glaciers formed on the Sierra crest again and again and flowed down the river canyons. Ice carved [[sierra-nevada/yosemite-valley|Yosemite Valley]] and [[sierra-nevada/kings-canyon|Kings Canyon]] into U shapes, left the hanging waterfalls, scooped out Emerald Bay and thousands of lake basins, and polished granite to a shine. The last major advance peaked about 20,000 years ago.

Away from the ice, the climate was cooler and wetter. Lakes filled the desert basins, including [[desert/death-valley|Death Valley]] and a [[eastern-sierra/mono-lake|Mono Lake]] several times its present size. Sea level was about 400 feet lower, because so much water was frozen on land. There was no San Francisco Bay, and the northern [[south-coast/channel-islands|Channel Islands]] were one island with mammoths on it. Forests of pine and cypress grew along the southern coast, and the [[south-coast/torrey-pines|Torrey pines]] and the cypresses of [[central-coast/point-lobos|Point Lobos]] are what remain of them.

People arrived during this period, at least 13,000 years ago.

## 6. It is not finished

California's landscape is young and still being made.

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| About 650 years ago | Panum Crater erupts at [[eastern-sierra/mono-lake\|Mono Lake]] |
| 1872 | The Lone Pine earthquake lifts the [[eastern-sierra/mount-whitney\|eastern Sierra]] |
| 1906 | The San Andreas slips up to 20 feet at [[bay-area/point-reyes\|Point Reyes]] |
| 1914 to 1917 | [[volcano-country/lassen\|Lassen Peak]] erupts |
| 1983 | A landslide gives [[central-coast/big-sur\|McWay Falls]] a beach |
| 1989 | The Loma Prieta earthquake raises the [[central-coast/santa-cruz-mountains\|Santa Cruz Mountains]] |
| 1992 | The [[north-coast/lost-coast\|Lost Coast]] jumps four feet out of the sea |
| 2017 | A slide at Mud Creek adds fifteen acres to [[central-coast/big-sur\|Big Sur]] |

## Rocks to know on sight

| Rock | Looks like | Means | See it at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Granite | Pale, speckled, sparkly | Magma that cooled slowly underground | Yosemite, Tahoe, Whitney, Joshua Tree |
| Basalt | Dark, heavy, sometimes in columns | Runny lava | Devils Postpile, Burney Falls, Lava Beds |
| Chert | Red or green, in thin folded ribbons | Deep-sea ooze | Lands End, Marin Headlands |
| Serpentinite | Green, waxy, slippery | A piece of the Earth's mantle | Mount Tamalpais |
| Tufa | White, knobby towers | Limestone formed at underwater springs | Mono Lake |
| Volcanic breccia | Lumpy, reddish, full of chunks | An exploded volcano | Pinnacles |
| Sandstone | Tan, layered, gritty | Old beaches and sea floors | Torrey Pines, Mission Peak |
