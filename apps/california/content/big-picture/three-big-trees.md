# Three Big Trees

California has the tallest trees in the world, the largest trees in the world and the oldest trees in the world. They are three different species, living in three very different places, and the hike list visits all of them.

Redwoods and sequoias are cousins, the last of a family that covered much of the northern hemisphere in the age of the dinosaurs. A third cousin, the dawn redwood, was known only from fossils until living trees were found in a valley in China in the 1940s.

## Coast redwood

**Why so tall.** Height takes water, a long life and a mild climate. The redwood coast has heavy winter rain, almost no hard frost, and fog in the dry summer. Fog cuts the amount of water the trees lose, and their needles can absorb it directly. Fog also condenses on the foliage and drips, and that drip can add up to a third or more of a forest's yearly water. The tallest trees grow on river flats, in deep soil brought by floods.

**Why so hard to kill.** Thick bark with no resin, heartwood full of tannin that insects and fungi avoid, and an ability to resprout from roots, stumps, burls and trunks. A redwood that falls over can send up a row of new trunks from its side.

**What happened to them.** There were about two million acres of old-growth redwood forest in 1850. About five percent is left. The wood is light, strong, straight and slow to rot, the perfect building material, and it was in the wrong place, next to a state that needed to build fast. The rest of the range is still forest, but it is young forest, cut two or three times.

**Where to see them.**

- Close to home: [[bay-area/muir-woods|Muir Woods]], [[bay-area/armstrong-redwoods|Armstrong Redwoods]], [[central-coast/santa-cruz-mountains|the Santa Cruz Mountains]].
- What was lost: [[bay-area/oakland-redwoods|the Oakland Redwoods]], [[north-coast/mendocino-coast|the Mendocino Coast]].
- After fire: [[central-coast/big-basin|Big Basin]].
- At the southern edge: [[central-coast/big-sur|Big Sur]].
- At full size: [[north-coast/humboldt-redwoods|Humboldt Redwoods]] and [[north-coast/redwood-national-park|Redwood National Park]].

## Giant sequoia

**Why so big.** A sequoia does not get as tall as a redwood, but it keeps its thickness much farther up, like a column instead of a spire, and it never stops adding wood. An old sequoia puts on more wood each year than almost any other tree on Earth. They grow in a narrow belt where the Sierra snowpack is deep and melts slowly into the granite soil.

**Why they need fire.** The cones stay closed on the tree for years. Heat from a fire on the ground dries them, and they open and rain seeds a week or two later onto bare ash, with the competition burned away and sunlight coming through the gaps. Without fire there are almost no seedlings.

**What happened to them.** About a third of the old sequoias were logged between the 1850s and the 1920s, which was mostly a waste. The wood is brittle, and the huge trunks often shattered when they hit the ground. Much of it ended up as fence posts, shingles and grape stakes. Nearly all the remaining groves are now protected. The new threat is severe fire. See [[big-picture/fire|Fire]].

**Where to see them.** [[sierra-nevada/mariposa-grove|Mariposa Grove]] and [[sierra-nevada/giant-forest|Giant Forest]].

## Bristlecone pine

**Why so old.** Hardship. Bristlecones grow on cold, dry, windy ridges in poor soil, where there are few insects, little rot and not enough fuel to carry fire. They grow so slowly that their wood is extremely dense. When part of a tree is damaged, that part dies and the rest carries on. The oldest ones are the ones on the worst ground.

**What they are good for.** Their rings are a year-by-year record of climate going back nearly 9,000 years, and they were used to correct radiocarbon dating.

**Where to see them.** [[eastern-sierra/bristlecone-pines|The Bristlecone Pines]] of the White Mountains.

## How the trees were saved

California's conservation movement began with these trees. The outcry over a sequoia stripped for a sideshow helped protect [[sierra-nevada/mariposa-grove|Mariposa Grove]] in 1864. A photographer's anger started the campaign for [[central-coast/big-basin|Big Basin]] in 1900. A congressman's donation made [[bay-area/muir-woods|Muir Woods]] in 1908. And a road trip in 1917 led to the Save the Redwoods League, which has bought more than 200,000 acres and is the reason for most of the redwood state parks, as told at [[north-coast/humboldt-redwoods|Humboldt Redwoods]].
