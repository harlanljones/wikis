# The Gold Rush

In January 1848 California was a remote Mexican province with perhaps 14,000 residents who were not Native Americans. Four years later it was an American state with a quarter of a million. Nothing like it had happened before. The Gold Rush was the largest voluntary migration in the history of the country up to that time, and it explains a great deal about why California is the way it is.

## Why the gold was there

Gold is spread very thinly through the Earth's crust. To get a deposit worth mining, something has to concentrate it. In California that happened twice.

First, when the [[big-picture/how-california-was-built|granite of the Sierra]] was forming, hot water circulating through the surrounding rock dissolved traces of gold and carried them into cracks. As the water cooled, it left veins of white quartz with gold in them. The richest belt of veins, about 120 miles long and a mile or two wide, became known as the Mother Lode.

Second, as the mountains wore down over millions of years, rivers broke up the veins and carried the pieces downstream. Gold is nineteen times heavier than water, so it drops out wherever the current slows, on the inside of bends, behind boulders and in cracks in the bedrock. These river deposits are called placers. They could be worked by one person with a pan.

## The discovery

James Marshall found flakes of placer gold in the tailrace of a sawmill at [[gold-country/coloma|Coloma]] on January 24, 1848. Nine days later, with neither government aware of it, Mexico signed the treaty giving California to the United States.

In 1848 the rush was local. Californians, Oregonians, Mexicans from Sonora, and Hawaiians got there first, and many did very well. In December President Polk confirmed the stories in a message to Congress and put 230 ounces of California gold on display in Washington. Then the world came.

## Getting there

About 90,000 people arrived in 1849, the forty-niners. There were three ways to come from the eastern states, all bad.

| Route | Time | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overland by wagon | 4 to 6 months | 2,000 miles on foot. Cholera on the plains, then deserts, then the Sierra before the snow. See [[sierra-nevada/donner-pass\|Donner Pass]] and [[desert/death-valley\|Death Valley]]. |
| By sea around Cape Horn | 5 to 8 months | 15,000 miles, storms, scurvy and boredom |
| By sea, across Panama, and by sea again | 1 to 3 months if lucky | Malaria and yellow fever on the crossing, and no guarantee of a ship on the Pacific side |

They came from everywhere: Chile, Peru, China, Australia, France, Ireland, Germany. San Francisco went from about 800 people to 25,000 in under two years. Crews deserted the moment they anchored, and hundreds of abandoned ships rotted in the cove. Some were hauled ashore to serve as hotels, warehouses and a jail. Their hulls are still under the streets of the Financial District.

## Who actually made money

A few miners struck it rich. Most did not, and by 1852 the easy gold was gone. The dependable fortunes went to people who sold things to miners.

- **Sam Brannan** cornered the market in shovels and pans.
- **Levi Strauss**, a Bavarian dry-goods dealer, sold cloth and later riveted work pants.
- **Domenico Ghirardelli** sold chocolate. **Henry Wells and William Fargo** moved gold and mail.
- **John Studebaker** built wheelbarrows in Placerville and took his savings home to start a wagon company.
- **Collis Huntington, Mark Hopkins, Charles Crocker and Leland Stanford** were Sacramento shopkeepers. They used their profits to build the [[sierra-nevada/donner-pass|railroad over the Sierra]].

Women were scarce in the camps, and those who came could earn more cooking, washing and running boardinghouses than most men made digging.

## A state in a hurry

California skipped the usual years as a territory. Its people wrote a constitution in 1849 and it was admitted as the thirty-first state on September 9, 1850. The constitution banned slavery, mostly because miners did not want to compete with slave labor. That upset the balance between free and slave states in the Senate and led to the Compromise of 1850, one of the steps on the road to the Civil War. California's gold later helped pay for the Union side.

## From pans to water cannons

Mining changed fast as the gold got harder to reach.

1. **The pan**, 1848. One person, one creek.
2. **The rocker and the long tom**, 1849. Small teams washing more gravel.
3. **River mining**, early 1850s. Companies dammed whole rivers and turned them out of their beds to get at the bottom.
4. **Hydraulic mining**, 1853 to 1884. Water under pressure washed away hillsides to reach ancient river gravels. See [[gold-country/malakoff-diggins|Malakoff Diggins]].
5. **Hard-rock mining**, 1850s to 1950s. Shafts and tunnels followed the quartz veins thousands of feet down. Stamp mills crushed the ore. This is what [[eastern-sierra/bodie|Bodie]] and the [[desert/joshua-tree|Lost Horse Mine]] were.

After the first few years, mining took money and machinery, and the independent prospector became a wage worker.

## What it cost

**Native Californians.** The gold country was the homeland of the Nisenan, Miwok, Maidu and others. Miners overran it in months. See [[big-picture/first-peoples|First Peoples]] and [[gold-country/chawse|Chaw'se]].

**Californios and Latin Americans.** The Spanish-speaking ranchers who had owned California were guaranteed their land by treaty and then spent decades and fortunes in court proving their titles, usually losing the land to lawyers and squatters. Mexican, Chilean and Peruvian miners, often the most skilled, were driven from the diggings by mobs and by the Foreign Miners' Tax of 1850, which charged twenty dollars a month.

**The Chinese.** About 25,000 Chinese had come to "Gold Mountain" by 1852. A second miners' tax was aimed at them, and for years it supplied a large share of the state's revenue. They were pushed onto worked-out claims, barred from testifying against white people in court, and later [[bay-area/angel-island|barred from entering the country]].

**African Americans.** Several thousand Black people came, some free and some brought as slaves despite the constitution. A number bought their freedom with gold and built lasting communities, but a state fugitive slave law in 1852 put even free people at risk.

**The land.** Hydraulic debris buried farms and filled San Francisco Bay. Miners used millions of pounds of mercury to capture gold, and it is still in the rivers. Forests were cut for [[bay-area/oakland-redwoods|lumber]] and [[sierra-nevada/lake-tahoe|mine timbers]].

## What it left

The Gold Rush fixed some lasting habits: a population from everywhere, a taste for risk and fast money, a rush-first approach to natural resources, and, in reaction to the wreckage, some of the country's first conservation law and its first [[sierra-nevada/yosemite-valley|parks]].
