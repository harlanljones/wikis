# Fire

Almost every natural landscape in California is built to burn. The state has wet winters that grow a great deal of vegetation and long, rainless summers that dry it out, and it gets dry lightning in the mountains and hot winds in the fall. Fire has been part of the place for as long as it has had this climate, and the plants show it.

## Built to burn

- **Giant sequoias** need fire to open their cones and clear a seedbed. See [[sierra-nevada/giant-forest|Giant Forest]].
- **Coast redwoods** wear fireproof bark and resprout from the trunk. See [[central-coast/big-basin|Big Basin]].
- **Ponderosa and Jeffrey pines** drop their lower branches and grow thick, plated bark so that ground fires pass underneath.
- **Chaparral** shrubs either resprout from a woody burl at the base or leave seeds that only sprout after being heated or smoked.
- **Knobcone and bishop pines** keep their cones glued shut with resin for decades until a fire melts them.
- **Fire-following wildflowers**, such as fire poppies and whispering bells, appear only in the first springs after a burn, from seeds that may have waited fifty years.
- **Fan palms and oaks** survive with their growing tissue protected. See [[desert/anza-borrego|Anza-Borrego]].

## How much used to burn

Before 1800, fire was common nearly everywhere. Tree rings in the Sierra show that a typical patch of pine forest burned every five to twenty years. Lightning started many of those fires. People started many more.

Native Californians burned on purpose, carefully and often, for dozens of reasons: to grow basket materials, improve acorn crops, bring in game, clear travel routes, and protect villages by using up fuel. See [[big-picture/first-peoples|First Peoples]]. Researchers estimate that roughly four and a half million acres or more burned in California in an average year before 1800. That is about what burned in 2020, the worst fire year in the state's modern records. Summer skies in old California were probably often smoky.

The difference was in how it burned. Frequent fires stayed mostly on the ground. They thinned out small trees and brush and left the big trees standing in open, park-like forests. Early travelers wrote that you could gallop a horse through the Sierra pine woods.

## A century of putting it out

The Spanish banned Indian burning as early as 1793. The state of California outlawed it in 1850. But the real change came after 1910, when a storm of wildfires in Idaho and Montana burned three million acres and killed 86 people in two days. The young Forest Service made putting out every fire its central mission. By 1935 the official goal was to have any new fire under control by ten o'clock the next morning.

It worked, for a while, and it changed the forests. Without fire, shade-loving firs and cedars filled in under the pines. Dead wood and needles piled up. Forests that once held fifty or sixty big trees to the acre came to hold several hundred, most of them small. There was now a ladder of fuel from the ground to the treetops.

Some people saw the problem early. Harold Biswell, a forestry professor at Berkeley, ran experimental burns from the 1940s and was nicknamed "Harry the Torch" by colleagues who thought he was a crank. In the 1960s scientists in the sequoia groves proved that the big trees had stopped reproducing because there was no fire. Sequoia and Kings Canyon began prescribed burning in 1968 and Yosemite followed in 1970.

## The megafire years

Three things came together after about 2000: overgrown forests, a warming climate that dries fuel earlier and keeps it dry longer, and millions of houses built in places that burn.

| Year | Fire | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2018 | Camp Fire | Destroyed the town of Paradise in a morning and killed 85 people, the deadliest fire in state history |
| 2020 | August Complex | Burned more than a million acres in the northern Coast Ranges, the first "gigafire" in modern California |
| 2020 | CZU Lightning Complex | Burned 97 percent of [[central-coast/big-basin\|Big Basin]] |
| 2020 | Castle Fire | Killed an estimated 10 to 14 percent of the world's large sequoias |
| 2020 | Dome Fire | Killed more than a million Joshua trees near [[desert/kelso-dunes\|Kelso Dunes]] |
| 2021 | Dixie Fire | Burned nearly a million acres and most of [[volcano-country/lassen\|Lassen Volcanic National Park]] |
| 2021 | KNP Complex and Windy Fire | Killed another 3 to 5 percent of large sequoias, and reached [[sierra-nevada/giant-forest\|Giant Forest]] |

More than four million acres burned in 2020 alone. The total is close to the old normal. The severity is not. Fires now kill whole stands of old trees that had come through dozens of earlier fires.

## Bringing good fire back

The answer most scientists, tribes and fire agencies now agree on is more fire of the right kind, along with thinning. The best evidence is in the places that have tried it. In 2021 and 2022, wildfires burning toward [[sierra-nevada/giant-forest|Giant Forest]] and [[sierra-nevada/mariposa-grove|Mariposa Grove]] dropped to the ground and slowed down when they reached areas that had been burned on purpose.

Tribes including the Yurok, Karuk, North Fork Mono and Amah Mutsun are leading a revival of cultural burning, sometimes on land where their grandparents were jailed for it. California changed its laws in 2021 and 2022 to make that easier.

## On the trail

- **Cat faces.** The triangular black scars at the base of big trees, on the uphill side, where fuel collected and burned hot. Many healthy trees have several, from fires centuries apart.
- **Green fuzz on black trunks.** Redwoods resprouting, at [[central-coast/big-basin|Big Basin]], [[bay-area/armstrong-redwoods|Armstrong Redwoods]] and [[central-coast/big-sur|Big Sur]].
- **Sequoia seedlings** by the thousand in burned patches, each about an inch tall.
- **Doghair thickets.** Dense stands of skinny trees, all the same age, are a forest that has missed several fires.
- **Snag forests.** Standing dead trees after a severe fire are busy places. Woodpeckers, especially the black-backed woodpecker, depend on them.
- **Burn piles and scorched ground** beside the trail in spring or fall usually mean a prescribed burn. That is the forest being looked after.
