# Water

California has plenty of water. It falls in the wrong place at the wrong time. Three quarters of the rain and snow comes down north of Sacramento, and most of the people and farms are south of it. Nearly all of it falls between November and April, and the crops need it from May to October. The history of the state since 1850 is largely the story of moving water from where it is to where someone wanted it.

## The frozen reservoir

The key to the system is the Sierra Nevada snowpack. Storms pile up snow all winter, and it melts slowly through spring and early summer, releasing water just as the dry season starts. In an average year the snowpack supplies about a third of the water California uses.

Before dams, the melt ran into the Central Valley and spread out. The valley floor was one of the largest wetlands in North America. In wet years it became an inland sea 300 miles long, and steamboats ran across farms. Tulare Lake, in the southern valley, was the largest freshwater lake west of the Mississippi, fed by the [[sierra-nevada/kings-canyon|Kings River]] and its neighbors. It was drained for cotton by the early 1900s. In the wet spring of 2023 it came back for a few months and covered more than a hundred thousand acres of farmland.

## Hetch Hetchy

North of Yosemite Valley, inside the national park, the Tuolumne River ran through a second valley of the same kind, with granite walls, waterfalls and a meadow floor. It was called Hetch Hetchy.

San Francisco had wanted it as a reservoir site since the 1880s. After the 1906 earthquake and fire, the city argued that a reliable public water supply was a matter of survival. John Muir and the Sierra Club fought it for seven years in the first national battle over wilderness. "Dam Hetch Hetchy!" Muir wrote. "As well dam for water-tanks the people's cathedrals and churches, for no holier temple has ever been consecrated by the heart of man."

Gifford Pinchot, the founder of the Forest Service, took the other side. To him, conservation meant using resources wisely for the greatest number of people. The split between preserving nature and managing it runs through American environmental politics to this day.

Congress approved the dam in December 1913. Muir died a year later. O'Shaughnessy Dam was finished in 1923, and the water still flows 167 miles by gravity to Bay Area taps. The fight had one lasting effect. Three years after the vote, Congress created the National Park Service, in part so that it could not happen again.

## The Owens Valley

In 1900 Los Angeles had about 100,000 people and a river that could support perhaps twice that. Its water chief, a self-taught Irish engineer named William Mulholland, looked 230 miles north to the Owens River, which carries the snowmelt of the eastern Sierra.

Agents for the city, some posing as ranchers or as federal employees working on an irrigation project for the valley, quietly bought land and the water rights that went with it. By the time valley people understood what was happening, the city controlled the river. Mulholland built an aqueduct across the Mojave, 233 miles of canal, pipe and tunnel, powered entirely by gravity. On November 5, 1913, in front of a crowd of 40,000, the first water came down the spillway into the San Fernando Valley. Mulholland's whole speech was, "There it is. Take it."

Los Angeles grew to more than a million people by 1930. Owens Lake, once 100 square miles and deep enough for steamboats, was dry by 1926, and its bed became the largest single source of dust pollution in the United States. Valley ranchers dynamited the aqueduct repeatedly in the 1920s. Los Angeles has since spent more than two billion dollars controlling the dust.

The city went on to buy most of the valley, including the orchard town at [[eastern-sierra/manzanar|Manzanar]], and then reached farther north to [[eastern-sierra/mono-lake|Mono Lake]], where it was finally stopped.

## The big projects

In the twentieth century the state and federal governments replumbed California.

- **The Central Valley Project**, begun in the 1930s, dams the Sacramento River at Shasta and moves the water south for farms.
- **The Colorado River** was dammed at Hoover Dam in 1935. Its water goes to Los Angeles and San Diego by aqueduct and to the farms of the Imperial Valley by canal. The [[desert/san-jacinto|Salton Sea]] is a relic of the first try at that canal.
- **The State Water Project**, approved by voters in 1960, dams the Feather River at Oroville and sends water 444 miles down the California Aqueduct. Pumps lift it nearly 2,000 feet over the Tehachapi Mountains to Southern California. It is the largest single user of electricity in the state.

The system has about 1,500 dams. It made California the country's biggest farm state and let more than 20 million people live in a near-desert. It also blocked salmon from most of their spawning streams and cut the flow of fresh water through San Francisco Bay roughly in half.

## Turning points

Some of the most important decisions were decisions to stop.

- [[gold-country/malakoff-diggins|The Sawyer Decision]] of 1884 put farms and rivers ahead of mining debris.
- [[volcano-country/burney-falls|Burney Falls]] was bought by a family around 1920 to keep it from a power company.
- [[sierra-nevada/kings-canyon|Kings Canyon]] was kept free of dams in 1940 and 1965.
- The [[eastern-sierra/mono-lake|Mono Lake]] rulings of 1983 and 1994 established that the public has a right to living lakes and streams.
- In 2024 the last of four dams on the Klamath River, in far northern California, was taken out. It was the largest dam removal in history, and salmon were swimming past the old dam sites within weeks.

## On the trail

- **Bathtub rings** on reservoirs and on Mono Lake show how far the water has dropped.
- **Hanging valleys and waterfalls** peak in May and June, when the snow melts. Go to [[sierra-nevada/yosemite-valley|Yosemite]] then.
- **Springs in dry country** often come from far away or long ago, as at [[volcano-country/burney-falls|Burney Falls]] and [[desert/anza-borrego|Borrego Palm Canyon]].
- **Aqueducts.** You cross the Los Angeles Aqueduct on the drive to [[eastern-sierra/mount-whitney|Whitney]] and the California Aqueduct on the way to almost anything south.
