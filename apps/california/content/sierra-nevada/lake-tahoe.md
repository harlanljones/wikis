# Lake Tahoe

> **On your hike list:** Eagle Lake Trail, from the Eagle Falls trailhead at Emerald Bay (1.9 miles round trip)

Lake Tahoe is 22 miles long, 12 wide and 1,645 feet deep, the second deepest lake in the United States after Crater Lake. It holds enough water to cover all of California more than a foot deep. Mark Twain, who nearly burned down its forest with a campfire in 1861, said it "must surely be the fairest picture the whole earth affords."

## A lake in a crack

Tahoe does not sit in a valley carved by a river or a glacier. It sits in a block of the Earth's crust that dropped. The Sierra Nevada rose on the west side and the Carson Range on the east, along faults, and the piece between them sank. That happened over the last two to three million years. Lava from a volcano on the north shore then dammed the outlet, and the basin filled.

The faults under the lake are still active. Scientists mapping the bottom have found the scars of enormous landslides, including one on the west shore where a whole section of the shelf collapsed and sent a wave around the lake.

Glaciers came later and only worked on the edges. Emerald Bay is their best piece. A glacier flowed down Eagle Creek canyon, gouged out a basin, and left ridges of rubble along both sides and across the mouth. When it melted, the lake flooded in through a narrow gap. Eagle Lake, where you hike, sits in a smaller bowl scooped by the same ice higher up, in the bare granite of the Desolation Wilderness.

## Da ow a ga

The Washoe people have spent their summers at the lake for thousands of years. They call it *Da ow a ga*, "the edge of the lake." Early mapmakers mangled the first two syllables into "Tahoe." Families came up from the valleys on the Nevada side each spring to fish for the giant Lahontan cutthroat trout, which could weigh more than 30 pounds, and to gather plants and hold ceremonies.

The lake's best known sacred site, Cave Rock, on the east shore, was dynamited for a highway tunnel in 1931. The Washoe Tribe never ceded the lake and has spent decades regaining pieces of it. In 2003 the Forest Service banned rock climbing at Cave Rock at the tribe's request.

## The forest that went down a mine

In 1859 silver was discovered at the Comstock Lode, just over the mountains at Virginia City, Nevada. The ore lay in soft, crumbling ground, and the mines needed massive timber frames to keep from caving in. Over the next thirty years the Tahoe basin was stripped to feed them. Logs were rafted across the lake by steamer, hauled over the ridge by railroad, and shot down to the valley in V-shaped flumes filled with running water. It was said that a forest was buried under Virginia City.

By the 1890s nearly two thirds of the basin had been cut. Most of the trees you see at Tahoe today are about 130 years old and all the same age, which is one reason the forest is crowded and prone to burn.

## Keeping it blue

Tahoe is blue because it is clear, and it is clear because very little grows in it. The basin is mostly granite, which gives up few nutrients, and the lake is so big that the water in it stays an average of about 650 years.

In 1968 scientists could lower a white dinner-plate-sized disk into the lake and see it at 102 feet. Then came casinos, ski resorts, highways and tens of thousands of houses. Fine dust and fertilizer washed in, and by the late 1990s the disk vanished at about 65 feet. California and Nevada created a joint agency to control building in 1969, the first of its kind. Clarity has roughly leveled off since, at 60 to 70 feet, but it has not come back.

## Vikingsholm

In 1928 Lora Knight, a wealthy widow who had helped pay for Charles Lindbergh's flight to Paris, bought the head of Emerald Bay because it reminded her of a fjord. She sent her architect to Scandinavia for ideas and had 200 workers build a 38-room Norse castle in one summer, with sod roofs and carved dragon beams. She also built a stone teahouse on Fannette Island, the only island in the lake. Its shell is still up there.

## Look for on the trail

- **Glacial polish and scratches** on the granite slabs.
- **Eagle Falls**, roaring in June and nearly dry by September.
- **Twisted junipers** growing out of cracks in bare rock, some more than a thousand years old.
- **The moraines** of Emerald Bay from the trailhead: the two long, forested ridges on either side.
- **Ospreys.** Emerald Bay has one of the densest groups of osprey nests in the Sierra.
