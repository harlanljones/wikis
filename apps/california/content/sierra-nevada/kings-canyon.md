# Kings Canyon

> **On your hike list:** Mist Falls Trail, from Roads End (8 miles round trip, 800 feet of climbing)

John Muir came to the canyon of the South Fork of the Kings River in 1873 and wrote that it was "a rival of the Yosemite." Almost nobody believed him, because almost nobody could get there. The road you drive to the trailhead was not finished until 1939, and it is still the only road into the canyon. It stops at a place called Roads End.

## Deeper than the Grand Canyon

Downstream from the park, where the Middle and South Forks of the Kings meet, the river runs at about 2,260 feet above sea level. Spanish Mountain, on the rim above, tops 10,000 feet. That is a drop of about 8,200 feet, which makes Kings Canyon one of the deepest canyons in North America, deeper than the Grand Canyon by half a mile.

The upper canyon, where you hike, is the Yosemite part. Rivers cut it into a V, and then glaciers flowed down it and reshaped it into a U with a flat floor and granite walls nearly 4,000 feet high. Grand Sentinel and North Dome stand in for El Capitan and Half Dome. Where the glacier ended, a few miles below Cedar Grove, the canyon pinches back into a V, and the road gets very exciting.

## The name

In the winter of 1805 to 1806 a Spanish expedition under Gabriel Moraga reached the river where it leaves the mountains. It was the feast of the Epiphany, so they named it *El Río de los Santos Reyes*, the River of the Holy Kings, for the three wise men.

The canyon was summer country for the Western Mono, or Monache, who crossed the crest every year over passes above 11,000 feet to trade with the Paiute of the Owens Valley. Acorns, baskets and shell beads went east. Obsidian, salt and pine nuts came west.

## The park that almost was a reservoir

Muir proposed a national park here in 1891. It took 49 years.

In between, the canyon was claimed by everyone. Sheepmen ran tens of thousands of animals through the meadows, which Muir called "hoofed locusts." The city of Los Angeles filed for dam sites to make electricity. Farmers in the San Joaquin Valley wanted the water stored for irrigation. The Forest Service wanted roads and resorts. Plans were drawn for dams that would have flooded both Cedar Grove and Tehipite Valley, on the Middle Fork.

The Sierra Club pushed for a park with no roads through its heart. In 1936 they sent a young photographer to Washington with a portfolio of prints to show Congress what was at stake. His name was Ansel Adams. Two years later he sent his new book of Sierra photographs to the Secretary of the Interior, who showed it to President Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt kept it. Kings Canyon National Park was signed into law in 1940.

Even then, the two valleys the dam builders wanted were left out. Cedar Grove and Tehipite were not added to the park until 1965.

The Kings is still mostly wild. It is dammed in the foothills at Pine Flat, and its water irrigates the fields around Fresno, but from its headwaters down to the reservoir it runs free.

## Mist Falls

The trail follows the river through open forest, the legacy of fires that still burn through the valley every decade or so. Mist Falls is not tall, but it carries the whole South Fork over a granite ledge, and in June, when the snow is melting, it soaks everybody within a hundred yards. Beyond it the trail climbs to Paradise Valley and the Rae Lakes Loop, one of the most popular backpacking trips in the Sierra.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Sphinx**, a spiky granite crest on the south wall, about three miles in. Look back.
- **Glacial erratics**, boulders left stranded in the forest when the ice melted.
- **Black bears** in the meadows near the trailhead, and bear boxes at every camp.
- **Rattlesnakes** sunning on the first two miles of sandy trail. They are the reason to watch your feet.
- **Muir Rock**, a flat boulder by the river near Roads End, where Muir gave talks to Sierra Club outings.
- **Zumwalt Meadow**, named for the railroad lawyer who helped save it.
