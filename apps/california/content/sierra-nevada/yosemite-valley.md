# Yosemite Valley

> **On your hike list:** Mist Trail to Vernal and Nevada Falls, from Happy Isles (5.4 miles round trip, 2,000 feet of climbing)

Yosemite Valley is seven miles long, about a mile wide and three thousand feet deep, with walls that go straight up. Nothing else on Earth looks quite like it, and for most of the 1860s and 1870s the best scientific minds in California could not agree on how it got that way.

## The fight over the ice

Josiah Whitney, the State Geologist and a Harvard man, declared that the floor of the valley had simply dropped, as if the bottom had fallen out. John Muir, a wandering Scottish-born sheepherder and sawmill hand with two years of college, said glaciers had carved it. Whitney called him "a mere sheepherder" and an "ignoramus."

Muir spent years climbing over the high country, reading scratches on the rock, and in 1871 he found a living glacier tucked under Merced Peak. He was mostly right. Geologists now think rivers cut a deep canyon first, as the range rose, and then glaciers moved in at least three times, widening it into a U and steepening the walls. The last glacier melted back roughly 15,000 years ago and left a lake behind a ridge of rubble near El Capitan. The lake filled with silt, which is why the valley floor is so flat.

## Why the Mist Trail is a staircase

The granite of Yosemite formed as molten rock that cooled miles underground about 100 million years ago. Some of it has very few cracks. Glaciers work by plucking loose blocks, so where the rock was cracked, the ice quarried deep, and where it was solid, the ice just polished it. The Merced River comes down what geologists call the Giant Staircase. Nevada Fall, 594 feet, and Vernal Fall, 317 feet, are the two steps, each one a slab of unbroken granite the ice could not remove.

The same reasoning explains the famous waterfalls on the valley walls. Small side glaciers could not keep up with the big one in the main valley, and when the ice melted their streams were left hanging in midair. Yosemite Falls drops 2,425 feet in three stages, one of the tallest waterfalls in the world.

## Ahwahnee

The people who lived here called the valley Ahwahnee, usually translated as "place like a gaping mouth," and themselves the Ahwahneechee. They had been here for thousands of years, and they managed the valley with fire. Regular burning kept the floor open, with black oaks spaced like an orchard and wide meadows. The park-like scenery that amazed the first tourists was a tended garden.

In March 1851, during the Gold Rush, a state-backed militia called the Mariposa Battalion rode in to remove the Ahwahneechee after fighting between miners and Native people in the foothills. They burned the villages and the acorn stores. They are the first non-Native people known for certain to have entered the valley. The battalion's doctor, Lafayette Bunnell, proposed naming it after the tribe, and "Yosemite" is thought to come from a Miwok word for the Ahwahneechee used by their neighbors, meaning something like "those who kill." Their leader, Tenaya, is remembered in the name of a lake.

Some Ahwahneechee returned and lived in the valley for another century, working for the hotels and the Park Service. The last village was closed in 1969. Their descendants are rebuilding a roundhouse and village site in the valley today.

## The first park

Tourists arrived in 1855, and within a few years there were hotels, fences and squatters' claims. On June 30, 1864, with the Civil War still on, Abraham Lincoln signed a bill granting Yosemite Valley and the [[sierra-nevada/mariposa-grove|Mariposa Grove]] to California, "for public use, resort, and recreation," forever. It was the first time any government had set aside wild land purely so that ordinary people could enjoy it. Yellowstone, eight years later, gets the title of first national park, but the idea was tried here.

The high country around the valley became a national park in 1890, after a campaign by Muir. In 1903 Muir took President Theodore Roosevelt camping for three nights, ditching his official escort, and talked to him about forests by the fire. California gave the valley back to the federal government in 1906.

## Look for on the trail

- **Rainbows** in the spray below Vernal Fall on spring mornings.
- **The Emerald Pool and the Silver Apron** above Vernal, where the river slides over polished granite. People drown here. Stay out.
- **Liberty Cap**, the dome beside Nevada Fall, smoothed on its upstream side by ice.
- **Glacial polish.** Patches of granite as shiny as a countertop, with scratches showing which way the ice moved.
- **Steller's jays and ground squirrels** who have learned about backpacks.
- **Stone steps.** More than 600 of them below Vernal Fall, first laid out in the 1850s.
