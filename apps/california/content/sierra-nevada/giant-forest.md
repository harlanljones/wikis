# Giant Forest

> **On your hike list:** Congress Trail and General Sherman Tree, Sequoia National Park (2.7 miles round trip)

Five of the ten largest trees on Earth grow in Giant Forest. The largest of all, the General Sherman, stands at the start of this walk. It is not the tallest tree, the widest or the oldest. It is simply the most wood in one piece that any living thing has ever been measured to contain.

## The numbers

- **Height:** 275 feet.
- **Distance around at the ground:** about 103 feet.
- **Volume of the trunk:** roughly 52,500 cubic feet, enough to fill more than half of an Olympic swimming pool.
- **Weight of the trunk:** estimated at around 1,400 tons, about the same as nine or ten blue whales.
- **Age:** more than 2,000 years. Sherman is big because it grows fast in a good spot, and many smaller sequoias are older.

It is still growing. Each year it adds about as much wood as there is in a good-sized ordinary tree. The top is dead, which is normal for an old sequoia, so it grows out instead of up.

## The Karl Marx Tree

The official story is that a cattleman named James Wolverton named the tree in 1879 for William Tecumseh Sherman, under whom he had served in the Civil War.

A few years later it had another name. In 1886 a group of San Francisco labor organizers and socialists founded the Kaweah Colony in the canyon below. They planned to pay for a cooperative utopia by logging, and they spent four years building a wagon road up toward the big trees by hand. They renamed the giant sequoias after their heroes. The biggest became the Karl Marx Tree.

In September 1890 Congress created Sequoia National Park, the country's second. A week later it tripled the park's size and took in the colony's timber claims. The colonists were arrested for cutting trees on what was now federal land, and the colony fell apart. The powerful Southern Pacific Railroad had quietly lobbied for the park, and historians still argue about why. The road the socialists built was the only way into the park for more than ten years.

## Captain Young

In its early years the park was guarded by the Army. In the summer of 1903 the troops were Buffalo Soldiers of the 9th Cavalry, and their commander, Captain Charles Young, was the acting superintendent. Young was the third Black graduate of West Point and the first Black superintendent of a national park. In one season his men built more road than had been built in the three years before, and finished the wagon road to Giant Forest. When local people offered to name a tree for him, he asked that it be named for Booker T. Washington instead. In 2004 a sequoia was named for Young as well.

## The fires

Sequoias need fire. Heat dries their cones and releases the seeds, and the seeds only sprout on bare, ash-covered soil in sunlight. Scientists here worked that out in the 1960s, and Sequoia and Kings Canyon began setting prescribed burns in 1968, the first national parks to do so. Giant Forest has been burned on purpose many times since.

It mattered. Fires in 2020 and 2021, burning in forests dried by drought and choked by a century of fire suppression, killed an estimated 13 to 19 percent of all the large sequoias in the world in just two summers. Trees that had lived through dozens of fires over two thousand years died in a day. In September 2021, as the KNP Complex fire approached Giant Forest, firefighters wrapped the base of the General Sherman in aluminum foil blankets. The fire reached the grove, met the areas that had been thinned by prescribed burns, and dropped to the ground. Sherman was unharmed.

## Look for on the trail

- **The President**, about 3,200 years old and one of the three largest trees, with an estimated two billion leaves.
- **The House and the Senate**, tight clusters of big trees. Sequoias often grow in groups where a past fire opened a gap.
- **Chief Sequoyah**, named for the inventor of the Cherokee writing system. The tree's scientific name, *Sequoiadendron*, is usually said to honor him too.
- **Fire scars** shaped like tall black triangles, some big enough to walk into, on trees that are otherwise healthy.
- **Tiny cones and tinier seeds.** A sequoia seed looks like a flake of oatmeal and weighs about 1/6,000 of an ounce.
- **Black bears** in the meadows in early summer.
