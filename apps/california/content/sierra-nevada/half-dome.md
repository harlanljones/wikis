# Half Dome

> **On your hike list:** Half Dome, via the Mist Trail from Happy Isles (14 to 16 miles round trip, 4,800 feet of climbing)

Half Dome stands 8,846 feet above sea level and nearly a mile above the floor of Yosemite Valley. In the 1860s Josiah Whitney, the State Geologist, wrote that it was "perfectly inaccessible, being probably the only one of all the prominent points about the Yosemite which never has been, and never will be, trodden by human foot." That prediction did not last long.

## It was never a whole dome

The name suggests that half of the mountain fell off. It did not. Half Dome is a single body of granite, a rock called Half Dome Granodiorite, that cooled underground about 87 million years ago. The sheer northwest face follows a set of vertical cracks. Glaciers undercut the base, slabs peeled away along the cracks, and the ice carried the rubble off. Geologists think only about a fifth of the original mass is missing. From the side, it is clear the mountain is really a narrow ridge.

The rounded back is a different process. Granite forms under enormous pressure. When erosion strips away the miles of rock above it, the granite expands and cracks into curved shells parallel to the surface, like the layers of an onion. The shells flake off, the corners go first, and what is left is a dome. You climb these shells on the cables, and you can see them stacked on the neighboring domes.

The ice never covered the top. At the height of the last glaciation, the summit stood about 700 feet above a sea of ice.

## Tis-sa-ack

The Ahwahneechee name for the dome is Tis-sa-ack. In one telling she was a woman who came to the valley with her husband from the dry country to the east, quarreled with him over water, and was turned to stone along with him. The dark streaks down the face are her tears. They are bands of lichen, growing where water runs.

## The first ascent

George Anderson was a Scottish sailor turned blacksmith and trail builder who lived in the valley. In October 1875 he started up the smooth eastern slope alone. He drilled a hole in the granite, hammered in an iron eyebolt, stood on it and drilled the next one, six feet higher. He is said to have tried going barefoot, then coating his feet in pine pitch for grip. It took days. On October 12 he reached the top.

Anderson left a rope strung through his bolts, and within a week other people were using it. Among the first was Sally Dutcher of San Francisco, the first woman known to climb the dome. John Muir went up in November. Anderson dreamed of building a staircase and a hotel on top, and died before he could.

The Sierra Club replaced the rope with steel cables in 1919. They have been rebuilt since, but the route is the same, and a few of Anderson's bolt holes are still in the rock.

## The other way up

In 1957 Royal Robbins, Jerry Gallwas and Mike Sherrick climbed the 2,000-foot northwest face over five days, the hardest rock climb yet done in North America. Yosemite became the center of world rock climbing for the next half century. In 2008 Alex Honnold climbed the same face alone with no rope.

## Why you need a permit

By the 2000s more than a thousand people were on the cables on a busy Saturday, shoulder to shoulder, and a storm or a slip could stall the whole line. Since 2010 a permit has been required, with about 300 people a day allowed.

## Look for on the trail

- **The onion layers** on Sub Dome and on the cable route.
- **The Diving Board** and **the Visor**, overhanging lips at the top of the face.
- **Clouds building** in the afternoon. The dome is a lightning rod. If you can see a storm, do not go up.
- **Clouds Rest** to the northeast, a thousand feet higher than Half Dome, and the high peaks of the park beyond.
- **Marmots** on the summit, well practiced at opening unattended packs.
