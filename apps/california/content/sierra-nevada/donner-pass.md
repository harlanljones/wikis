# Donner Pass

> **On your hike list:** Donner Memorial State Park Lakeside Trail, Truckee (1.4 miles to China Cove)

Donner Pass is the lowest good crossing of the northern Sierra, 7,056 feet, and almost everything that has ever needed to get over the mountains has gone through it: the first wagons, the first railroad, the first transcontinental highway and now Interstate 80. The view from the top explains why it was so hard. The west side is a long, gentle ramp. The east side falls a thousand feet to the lake in about two miles.

## The first wagons

The Stephens-Townsend-Murphy party brought the first wagons over the pass in November 1844, guided by a Paiute man the emigrants called Truckee, whose name went on the river. They made it by unloading the wagons and hauling them up the granite ledges with chains. A seventeen-year-old named Moses Schallenberger stayed behind to guard the goods and spent the winter alone in a cabin by the lake, living on foxes he trapped. He survived. Two years later that cabin was used again.

## The Donner Party

In the summer of 1846 a group of families from Illinois and neighboring states, led by George Donner and James Reed, left the main trail to try a shortcut recommended in a guidebook by a promoter named Lansford Hastings, who had never taken wagons over it. The Hastings Cutoff led across the Wasatch Mountains, where they had to chop a road through brush, and then over 80 miles of waterless salt desert. It cost them a month, most of their oxen and their trust in one another.

They reached the lake at the end of October, 87 people in all, about half of them children. Snow was already falling. They tried the pass and were turned back within sight of the top. A storm on November 4 closed it for good.

Most of the party built cabins at the lake, and the Donner families camped six miles back at Alder Creek. It was one of the heaviest winters ever recorded in the Sierra. They ate the oxen, then the hides, then bark and bones. In December fifteen of the strongest set out on homemade snowshoes. They were out for 33 days, and seven lived to reach a ranch on the other side. Along the way, and later at the camps, the starving ate the dead.

Four relief parties fought their way up from the Sacramento Valley between February and April of 1847. Of the 87, 48 survived. The death rate among the men was about twice that among the women. Nearly all the survivors went on to ordinary lives in California.

The stone base of the Pioneer Monument is 22 feet high, the depth of the snow that winter.

## The Summit Tunnel

Twenty years later the Central Pacific Railroad arrived at the same wall of granite. Its chief engineer, Theodore Judah, had picked this route because it needed only one summit crossing, but that crossing required fifteen tunnels. The longest, Tunnel 6, ran 1,659 feet through the hardest rock on the line.

The railroad could not find enough workers, and in 1865 it began hiring Chinese men, first from the gold camps and then directly from Guangdong province. By 1867 they were about nine tenths of the workforce, more than 10,000 men. They drilled the summit granite by hand, one man holding a steel bit and two swinging sledgehammers, then packed the holes with black powder. Progress was sometimes less than a foot a day. To speed it up, they sank a shaft from above and worked four faces at once, and the company began mixing nitroglycerin on the spot.

They worked through two savage winters, living in tunnels dug through the snow. Avalanches carried away whole camps, and some of the dead were not found until spring. Nobody recorded how many died. In June 1867 several thousand Chinese workers put down their tools and asked for pay equal to that of white workers and a shorter day in the tunnels. It was the largest strike the country had yet seen. The company cut off their food, and they went back after a week.

The tunnel was holed through in August 1867, and the rails met the Union Pacific in Utah in May 1869. The trip from New York to San Francisco dropped from months to about a week.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Pioneer Monument**, dedicated in 1918, which stands on the site of one of the cabins.
- **The large boulder** that formed one wall and the fireplace of the Murphy cabin, with a plaque listing who lived and who died.
- **China Cove**, at the end of the lakeside walk.
- **The railroad** high on the wall above the lake, with its long concrete snowsheds. The original summit tunnels were abandoned in 1993 and can be walked.
- **The China Wall**, a mortarless stone embankment built by the railroad workers just below the summit, still solid.
- **Petroglyphs** on the granite slabs below the wall, pecked by ancestors of the Washoe.
