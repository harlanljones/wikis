# Mariposa Grove

> **On your hike list:** Grizzly Giant Loop, Mariposa Grove (2 mile loop, 300 feet of climbing)

Mariposa Grove holds about 500 mature giant sequoias, the largest group in Yosemite. Along with Yosemite Valley, it was the first piece of wild land the United States ever set aside for the public. What tipped the decision was partly shame over what had been done to another sequoia.

## A crime up the road

Giant sequoias came to the world's attention in 1852, when a hunter chasing a wounded bear walked into the Calaveras grove, about sixty miles north of here. Within a year, promoters had cut down the biggest tree they could find. No saw was long enough, so five men spent three weeks drilling it through with pump augers. Its stump was planed into a dance floor. Another tree was stripped of its bark to a height of more than a hundred feet, and the bark was shipped to New York and London and reassembled as a show. The tree, called the Mother of the Forest, died standing.

Easterners assumed the exhibit was a fake. Once they were convinced, many were disgusted. The outcry was fresh in memory in 1864 when a bill came before Congress to protect Yosemite and the "Mariposa Big Tree Grove." Lincoln signed it on June 30.

## Galen Clark

Galen Clark came to the Gold Country with lung disease so bad that a doctor gave him six months. In 1856 he moved up to the meadows at Wawona to die in the open air. He explored the grove the next year, counted and measured the trees, and told everybody he met about them. He lived another 53 years, more than twenty of them as the first guardian of the Yosemite Grant, which made him, in effect, the country's first park ranger. He is buried in Yosemite Valley under sequoias he planted himself for the purpose.

## The Grizzly Giant

The Grizzly Giant is the oldest tree in the grove, around 3,000 years old by the usual estimate, though nobody can count its rings without cutting it. It is 209 feet tall and close to 30 feet through at the base. One of its limbs is nearly seven feet thick, which is bigger than the trunk of any tree of any other species in the grove. It leans about five degrees and has been struck by lightning more times than anyone could count.

## Tunnel trees

In 1881 the Yosemite Stage and Turnpike Company paid two brothers $75 to enlarge a fire scar in a sequoia into a tunnel wide enough for a stagecoach. The Wawona Tunnel Tree became the most photographed tree in the world. Weakened by the hole, it fell under a heavy load of snow in the winter of 1968 to 1969. It was about 2,100 years old.

The California Tunnel Tree, which you walk through on this loop, was cut in 1895 and is the last one standing in the park. Nobody would do this now, and that change in attitude is part of the story.

## Too much love, too little fire

For a century the grove was managed in ways that hurt it. Roads and parking lots were paved over shallow roots, and trams ran among the trees. Worse, every fire was put out, so firs and cedars grew thick in the shade and almost no young sequoias sprouted.

The grove closed from 2015 to 2018 for a $40 million overhaul. The parking lot at the trees was torn out, wetlands were restored, and trails were put on boardwalks. The Park Service has been lighting careful fires in the grove since the 1970s, and that work paid off in July 2022, when the Washburn Fire burned to the edge of the grove. It dropped to the ground where earlier burns had cleared the fuel, and firefighters set up sprinklers around the Grizzly Giant. No big sequoias were lost.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Fallen Monarch**, near the start. It has been on the ground for centuries. Tannin in the wood keeps it from rotting.
- **The Bachelor and Three Graces**, four trees whose roots are so intertwined that if one fell it might take the rest.
- **Cones the size of a hen's egg.** They can hang on the tree, green and closed, for twenty years, until heat or a squirrel opens them.
- **Bark like a catcher's mitt**, up to two feet thick, soft and fibrous. Push on it.
- **Buffalo Soldiers.** Black cavalry regiments patrolled this park in 1899, 1903 and 1904, before there were rangers.
