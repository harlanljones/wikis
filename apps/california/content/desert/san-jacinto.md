# San Jacinto

> **On your hike list:** San Jacinto Peak via the Palm Springs Aerial Tramway (11.6 miles round trip, about 2,400 feet of climbing, 10,834 foot summit)

The north face of San Jacinto Peak rises more than 10,000 feet from the desert floor in about seven horizontal miles. It is one of the steepest mountain fronts in North America, on a par with the east side of the Sierra. You can ride up most of it in ten minutes.

## Five climates in a tram ride

The tram starts in Chino Canyon at 2,643 feet, among creosote bushes and barrel cactus, where summer days pass 110 degrees. It ends at 8,516 feet in a forest of Jeffrey pine and white fir, usually 30 to 40 degrees cooler, with snow on the ground half the year.

In between you pass through belts of vegetation stacked up the mountainside: desert scrub, then chaparral, then oak and pinyon woodland, then pine forest. Above the upper station the trail climbs on through lodgepole and limber pine to bare granite. Going up 8,000 feet changes the climate about as much as driving from Mexico to Canada.

## Why it is so steep

San Jacinto is the northern end of the Peninsular Ranges, the long chain of granite mountains that runs down into Baja California. The granite is about the same age and origin as the Sierra's.

Two of the biggest faults in California bracket the mountain. The San Jacinto Fault, the most active in Southern California, runs along its southwest side. The San Andreas runs through San Gorgonio Pass, right at the foot of the north face. Squeezed between them, the block of the mountain has been pushed up very fast.

On the other side of the pass stands San Gorgonio Mountain, 11,503 feet, the highest peak in Southern California. The pass between them, at about 2,600 feet, is one of the deepest in the country. Wind pours through it almost all the time, as cool ocean air rushes to replace hot air rising off the desert. That is why the pass bristles with thousands of wind turbines, one of the first big wind farms in the world.

## The tram

Francis Crocker, an electrical engineer, looked up at the snow on the peak on a sweltering day in 1935 and decided there ought to be a way up. People called it "Crocker's Folly." The idea was vetoed twice by governors and interrupted by two wars. Construction finally began in 1961.

Four of the five towers stand on cliffs with no road. Helicopters flew some 23,000 trips to carry in men and every piece of steel and concrete. The tram opened in September 1963. In 2000 the cars were replaced with rotating ones, the largest of their kind in the world, whose floors turn twice on each trip.

## Tahquitz

The mountain is at the heart of the country of the Cahuilla, and the Agua Caliente Band still owns the canyons at its foot, along with a large part of the city of Palm Springs, in a checkerboard of square-mile sections.

In Cahuilla tradition, the high country is the home of Tahquitz, the first and most powerful shaman, who turned his power to selfish ends, stole people's souls, and was banished to a cave below the peak that bears his name. Rumbling in the mountain and strange lights are his doing. The Cahuilla name for the peak itself is usually given as *Aya Kaich*, "smooth cliffs."

Climbers at Tahquitz Rock in the 1950s worked out a system for rating the difficulty of climbs. It grew into the Yosemite Decimal System, the "5.9" and "5.10" grades now used all over North America.

## The summit

The state park was created in the 1930s, and the Civilian Conservation Corps built the trails and the stone hut just below the top in 1935. It is still kept stocked as an emergency shelter.

A sentence attributed to John Muir appears on signs and in guidebooks: "The view from San Jacinto is the most sublime spectacle to be found anywhere on this earth." Nobody has found it in his writing. Go up and judge for yourself. On a clear day the view takes in the Salton Sea, the Mojave, the peaks around Los Angeles and sometimes the ocean and the Channel Islands.

## Look for on the trail

- **Round Valley**, a wet meadow in a granite bowl about two miles in.
- **Limber pines** near the top, with twigs you can tie in a knot.
- **The Salton Sea** to the southeast, a lake made by accident in 1905 when the Colorado River broke through an irrigation canal and poured into a dry basin for two years.
- **The San Andreas Fault**, visible as a line of hills and green patches across the desert floor.
- **Ice.** The trail can hold hard snow into June.
