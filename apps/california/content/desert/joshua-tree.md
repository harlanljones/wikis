# Joshua Tree

> **On your hike list:** Ryan Mountain (3 miles round trip, 1,050 feet of climbing) · Lost Horse Mine Trail (4 miles round trip)

Joshua Tree National Park sits on the line between two deserts. The western half is high, cool Mojave, with Joshua trees and heaps of rounded boulders. Drive east and downhill and the trees stop. You are in the Colorado Desert, with creosote, ocotillo and cholla. Ryan Mountain, at 5,457 feet, is near the middle of the high part and looks out over all of it.

## A tree and its moth

The Joshua tree is a yucca, a relative of agave and asparagus, and it does not have growth rings, so nobody can say for sure how old a big one is. Several hundred years is a fair guess.

It cannot reproduce without a single kind of small gray moth. The female yucca moth has special mouthparts found on no other insect, which she uses to gather a ball of pollen. She flies to another flower, lays her eggs in its ovary, and then deliberately packs the pollen onto the stigma. The flower is fertilized and makes seeds. Her caterpillars eat some of them and leave the rest. Neither species can survive without the other. Charles Darwin called it "the most wonderful case of fertilisation ever published."

The seeds have their own puzzle. The fruits are large and do not split open, and they seem designed to be eaten by something big. The best candidate is the Shasta ground sloth, which went extinct about 13,000 years ago. Fossil sloth dung in desert caves is full of Joshua tree seeds and leaves. Today only rodents move the seeds, a few dozen yards at a time, which is a problem as the climate warms and the trees need to move uphill and north.

The name is credited to Mormon travelers of the 1850s, who are said to have seen in the upraised limbs the prophet Joshua pointing the way. There is no record of it from the time, but it is a good story.

## The rocks

The boulder piles are a pale granite that cooled underground about 100 million years ago. While it was still buried, groundwater seeped down its cracks and rotted the rock along them, rounding off the corners of the blocks. Later, erosion stripped away the soil and loose rot and left the solid cores stacked up. The rock's coarse crystals give it a surface like sandpaper, which is why climbers come from all over the world.

Ryan Mountain itself is something else. It is made of dark, banded gneiss about 1.7 billion years old, among the oldest rock in California.

## Lost Horse Mine

Gold was found in these hills in the 1870s. The Lost Horse was by far the best of about 300 mines in what is now the park. Between 1894 and 1931 it produced more than 10,000 ounces of gold and 16,000 of silver.

The story of its discovery comes from a cowboy named Johnny Lang. His horses went missing, and he tracked them to the camp of a gang of rustlers, who told him to forget the horses. On the way home he met a prospector who had found a rich vein and was afraid of the same gang, and Lang bought the claim for $1,000. Later the mine's owner, a rancher named J. D. Ryan, noticed that the night shift, which Lang supervised, produced much less gold than the day shift. Lang was stealing it. He was given the choice of selling his share or going to jail. He sold, lived on in a nearby canyon, and died of exposure in the winter of 1925. His grave is beside the road to the trailhead.

The ten-stamp mill at the mine is one of the best preserved in the national parks. Ryan had its steam engine fueled with wood, and you can still see how bare the surrounding hills are.

## Minerva Hoyt

In the 1920s, new roads brought Los Angeles to the desert, and a fashion for cactus gardens took hold. People dug up plants by the truckload. Motorists set Joshua trees on fire at night to light the way.

Minerva Hamilton Hoyt was a wealthy Pasadena widow who had found comfort in the desert after the deaths of her husband and a son. She set out to save it. She built prize-winning desert exhibits, with real plants and stuffed animals, for flower shows in New York, Boston and London. She founded a conservation league, hired biologists to survey the land, and badgered officials for years. In 1936 President Franklin Roosevelt set aside 825,000 acres as Joshua Tree National Monument. It became a national park in 1994. A mountain in the park is named for her.

## The first people

The Serrano, Cahuilla and Chemehuevi lived around the oases and the canyons. The Oasis of Mara, at Twentynine Palms, was a Serrano village. Its name is said to mean "the place of little springs and much grass."

## Look for on the trail

- **Wonderland of Rocks** to the north from Ryan Mountain, and the snowy peaks of San Jacinto and San Gorgonio to the west.
- **Cream-colored blossoms** at the branch tips in March and April, in years with a cold winter and enough rain.
- **Young Joshua trees.** They are unbranched poles. A tree only forks after it flowers or is damaged.
- **The stamp mill** and the fenced shaft at Lost Horse, 500 feet deep.
- **Stone foundations** below Ryan Mountain, the ruins of the Ryan ranch.
