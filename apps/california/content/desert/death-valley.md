# Death Valley

> **On your hike list:** Golden Canyon to Red Cathedral, Death Valley National Park (3 miles round trip, about 580 feet of climbing)

Death Valley holds three American records at once. Badwater Basin, 282 feet below sea level, is the lowest point in North America. Furnace Creek recorded 134 degrees Fahrenheit on July 10, 1913, which still stands as the highest air temperature officially measured on Earth, though some meteorologists doubt that old reading. And it is the driest place in the country, with about two inches of rain in an average year.

## A valley that is being pulled open

Death Valley was not carved by a river. The crust of the western United States is being stretched, and as it stretches it breaks into long blocks. Some blocks drop and some tilt up. Death Valley is a block that has dropped, and it is still dropping. The Panamint Range on the west side is the tilted-up edge of the same block. Telescope Peak, its summit, stands 11,049 feet above sea level and more than two miles directly above Badwater.

The valley floor would be far deeper if it were not full of debris. The bedrock under Badwater is buried beneath about 9,000 feet of gravel, sand, mud and salt washed in from the mountains.

Why is it so hot? The valley is long, narrow and deep, with high walls. Sun heats the bare ground, the hot air rises, cannot get out over the mountains, cools slightly and sinks back down, where it is compressed and heated again. It is a convection oven.

## The lake that comes and goes

During the ice ages, with a cooler and wetter climate, the valley held a lake about 100 miles long and 600 feet deep. Geologists call it Lake Manly. It dried up about 10,000 years ago and left behind the salt flats.

It still comes back. After the remains of Hurricane Hilary dumped a year's rain in a day in August 2023, a shallow lake spread across Badwater Basin and lasted for months. In early 2024, after more storms, people were kayaking on it.

## Golden Canyon

The yellow badlands you walk through are the bed of an older lake still, one that existed here several million years ago, before the valley dropped. Its mud and volcanic ash hardened into soft mudstone that holds almost no water and grows almost no plants. With nothing to protect it, each cloudburst cuts the slopes into sharp ridges and gullies. As the valley sank and the mountains rose, these lake beds were tilted on end, which you can see in the slanting layers.

Red Cathedral, at the head of the canyon, is made of tougher stuff: gravel and boulders washed off the ancient mountains and cemented together, then stained red by iron.

A paved road used to run up Golden Canyon. A four-day storm in February 1976 sent a flood down it that tore out most of the pavement. Chunks of asphalt still stick out of the wash.

## "Goodbye, Death Valley"

The Timbisha Shoshone have lived here for at least a thousand years, and they call the valley Tümpisa, "rock paint," after the red ochre found in the hills. They wintered on the valley floor and moved up to the cool mountains in summer to harvest pine nuts. They are still here. In 2000 Congress returned more than 7,000 acres to the tribe, including the village at Furnace Creek.

The English name came from people who should never have been here. In December 1849 a wagon train of gold seekers bound for the diggings left the Old Spanish Trail in Utah to follow a map showing a shortcut. About a hundred people wandered into the valley and could not find a way out over the Panamints. Two young men, William Lewis Manly and John Rogers, walked 250 miles to a ranch near Los Angeles, got food and three horses and a one-eyed mule, and walked back. They found the two families who had waited for them still alive after 26 days. As the party finally climbed out of the valley, one of them is said to have turned and said, "Goodbye, Death Valley." Only one of the forty-niners is known to have died in the valley itself.

## White gold

The valley's real wealth turned out to be borax, a white mineral used in soap, glass and pottery glazes. From 1883 to 1889 the Harmony Borax Works hauled it out with teams of eighteen mules and two horses, known forever after as twenty-mule teams. Each team pulled two wagons and a water tank, a load of more than thirty tons, on a ten-day trip of 165 miles to the railroad at Mojave.

## Look for on the trail

- **Ripple marks and mud cracks** in the canyon walls, left in the mud of the ancient lake.
- **Manly Beacon**, the sharp yellow peak above the badlands, named for the rescuer.
- **Old pavement** in the first quarter mile.
- **White crusts of borax and salt** seeping from the rock.
- **Tatooine.** Several scenes in the first *Star Wars* film were shot in and around Golden Canyon.
- **The Panamints** across the valley, often with snow on top while it is 80 degrees where you stand.
