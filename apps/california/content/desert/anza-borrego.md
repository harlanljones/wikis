# Anza-Borrego

> **On your hike list:** Borrego Palm Canyon Trail, Anza-Borrego Desert State Park (about 3 miles round trip)

Anza-Borrego is the largest state park in California, more than 600,000 acres of badlands, washes and mountains taking up a fifth of San Diego County. The name joins a Spanish soldier to a sheep. *Borrego* is Spanish for lamb or young sheep, and here it means the desert bighorn.

## An oasis on a fault

Borrego Palm Canyon holds one of the larger wild groves of California fan palms, the only palm native to the western United States. They are true desert plants in one sense only. They need their roots in water all year.

So a palm grove in the desert marks a spring, and a spring often marks a fault. Along a fault, rock is ground into fine clay that blocks groundwater and forces it to the surface. Strings of palm oases line the faults of this region, including the San Andreas.

A palm's dead fronds hang down around the trunk in a thick "skirt" that shelters owls, bats, orioles and snakes. The Cahuilla, whose country this is, ate the small sweet fruit, ground the seeds for flour, thatched their houses with the fronds, and burned the groves from time to time to clear the dead skirts and kill pests, which also made the trees fruit better. The grinding holes in the boulders along the trail are theirs.

The grove has had a hard century. In 2004 a flash flood after a summer storm came down the canyon as a wall of water and uprooted many of the palms, carrying trunks out into the campground. In 2020 a fire set by a person burned the heart of the oasis. Fan palms are well defended against fire, and most of the scorched trees put out new fronds.

## The sheep

Peninsular bighorn sheep live in the steep desert mountains from here to Baja California. They can go days without drinking, and in summer they come down to the creek in the canyon. By the late 1990s only about 280 remained in the United States, hit by disease from livestock, mountain lions, cars and the spread of houses into their range. They were listed as endangered in 1998 and have recovered to several hundred. The canyon is one of the best places to see them. Look high on the slopes for pale rumps.

## Anza's road

In 1774 Juan Bautista de Anza, the commander of a small Spanish fort in what is now southern Arizona, set out to find a land route to the coast of California. The missions there were starving, and supply by sea was slow and deadly. He was guided across the worst of the desert by Sebastián Tarabal, a Cochimí man who had run away from Mission San Gabriel and nearly died crossing in the other direction.

The next year Anza came back with 240 people, most of them poor families recruited from the frontier towns of Sinaloa and Sonora, and about a thousand head of livestock. More than half the party were children. They were a mix of Spanish, Native and African ancestry, as most colonists of California were. They crossed the Colorado River with the help of the Quechan, walked through this desert in a freezing December, and camped in Coyote Canyon, a few miles north of the palm grove, where a baby was born on Christmas Eve. Three children were born on the trip, and only one person died, a woman in childbirth on the first night.

The families went on to Monterey, and in 1776 some of them founded the presidio and mission of San Francisco. Their names are still on the map of the Bay Area: Berryessa, Peralta, Bernal, Castro, Alviso.

The route was shut five years later. The Quechan, pushed too far by settlers and soldiers at the river crossing, rose in 1781 and closed the road for good. California was cut off by land for the rest of the Spanish era, which is one reason it stayed so thinly settled.

## An ocean and a savanna

The park's badlands are one of the richest fossil beds in North America, a nearly continuous record covering the last seven million years. The oldest layers are sea floor, from a time when the Gulf of California reached up here. Above them are the deposits of the Colorado River's ancient delta, including sand that can be matched to the walls of the Grand Canyon. Higher up are the bones of animals from a wet savanna: mammoths, camels, zebras, giant ground sloths, sabertooth cats, and a bird with a seventeen-foot wingspan.

## Look for on the trail

- **Bighorn sheep** on the canyon walls, especially on summer mornings.
- **Ocotillo**, the bundle of spiny whips. It leafs out within days of rain and drops the leaves when the soil dries, several times a year.
- **Desert varnish**, the dark coating on boulders, made by bacteria over thousands of years.
- **Flood debris**: palm trunks and boulders left by the 2004 flood.
- **Wildflowers** in February and March after a wet winter. Borrego Springs is known for its "superblooms."
- **Metal monsters.** The 130 huge welded sculptures scattered around the town, of mammoths, sloths and a 350-foot sea serpent, were inspired by the fossils.
