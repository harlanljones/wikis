# Kelso Dunes

> **On your hike list:** Kelso Dunes, Mojave National Preserve (3 miles round trip, 600 feet of climbing)

The Kelso Dunes cover 45 square miles and rise as much as 650 feet, which puts them among the tallest in North America. They are also one of only a few dozen dune fields in the world that make noise.

## Where the sand came from

Look northwest from the top. Thirty-five miles away is the sink of the Mojave River, a river that runs backward, away from the sea, and dies in the desert. During wetter times it filled a lake where the dry beds of Soda Lake and Silver Lake are now. When the lake dried, wind picked up sand from the bare bed and from the river's delta and carried it southeast across a wide plain called the Devils Playground.

Here the wind runs into the Granite and Providence Mountains. It slows, swirls back on itself, and drops its load. Winds from other directions push the sand back and forth instead of moving it on, so the dunes pile up and stay put.

The sand did not arrive all at once. The dunes were built in at least five pulses over the last 25,000 years, each during a dry spell when lake beds were exposed. Today there is very little new sand coming in. Most of the field is held in place by grass and shrubs, and the dunes are living on their inheritance.

The grains are mostly pale quartz and feldspar. The dark streaks that collect on the ridges are magnetite, an iron mineral heavier than the rest. A magnet dragged through the sand will come up furry.

## Booming sand

When the sand on a steep face avalanches, it can produce a deep hum, something like a propeller plane or a didgeridoo, that you feel in your legs as well as hear. It can last for many seconds after the slide stops.

To make it happen, climb to the top of the highest dune and slide down the steep side on your heels or the seat of your pants, pushing a wave of sand ahead of you. It works best when the sand is very dry and hot, and not at all after rain.

Dunes boom only when their grains are well rounded, nearly all the same size, and dry. The moving grains fall into step with one another and vibrate together. Physicists still argue about the details, including whether the note is set by the size of the grains or by the dune itself acting like the body of a guitar. Marco Polo described the sound in the deserts of Asia and blamed it on spirits.

## Life on a dune

In spring after a wet winter, the lower dunes bloom with sand verbena and evening primrose. Early in the morning, before the wind erases them, the sand is covered in tracks: kangaroo rats, kit foxes, beetles, sidewinders. The Mojave fringe-toed lizard lives only in fine windblown sand like this. It has scales like snowshoes on its toes and can dive into the sand and swim under it. Several insects here, including a kind of sand treader cricket, are found nowhere else on Earth.

## The railroad stop

The dunes are named for the old railroad town of Kelso, eight miles north. In 1905 the railroad that became the Union Pacific built a line from Salt Lake City to Los Angeles. Just east of Kelso the tracks climb 2,000 feet in under twenty miles, and steam locomotives needed extra "helper" engines and a great deal of water to get up. Kelso had a spring, so it got the helper station.

In 1924 the railroad built the Kelso Depot, a handsome Spanish-style building with a restaurant and rooms for crews, shaded by lawns and trees in the middle of nowhere. During World War II, with an iron mine working nearby, about 2,000 people lived here. Diesel engines ended the need for helpers, and the depot closed in 1985. It was saved from demolition and is now a visitor center.

The Chemehuevi and Mojave peoples crossed this country on foot along a chain of springs, a route the Army later took over as the Mojave Road. The preserve was created in 1994 by the California Desert Protection Act, which also made national parks of Death Valley and Joshua Tree.

## Look for on the trail

- **Tracks at dawn.** Sidewinders leave a row of separate J-shaped marks.
- **Black magnetite** streaks on the ridges.
- **The boom**, or at least a squeak, on the steep slip face just below the crest.
- **Burned Joshua trees** to the north on Cima Dome, where the 2020 Dome Fire killed more than a million of them.
- **Dark sky.** This is one of the darkest places in Southern California.
