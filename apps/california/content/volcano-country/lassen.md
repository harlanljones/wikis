# Lassen Peak and Bumpass Hell

> **On your hike list:** Lassen Peak Trail (5 miles round trip, 1,957 feet of climbing) · Bumpass Hell Trail (3 miles round trip)

Until Mount St. Helens in 1980, Lassen Peak was the only volcano in the lower 48 states to erupt in the twentieth century. It did it in front of a local businessman with a camera, which is how it became a national park.

## The eruptions of 1914 to 1917

On May 30, 1914, after 27,000 quiet years, a steam explosion blew a small crater in the summit. More than 150 explosions followed over the next year, and people climbed up to look.

In May 1915 things turned serious. Glowing lava welled up into the crater and spilled over the rim. On the night of May 19 an avalanche of hot rock hit the deep snowpack on the northeast side and melted it. A wall of mud, boulders and logs raced down Lost Creek and Hat Creek for miles. Homesteaders were woken by a dog and scrambled uphill in the dark, and nobody died.

Three days later, on May 22, the mountain let go. A column of ash and gas rose more than 30,000 feet and was visible from Eureka, 150 miles away. Ash fell as far east as Nevada. A blast of hot gas and pumice swept down the same northeast slope and flattened three square miles of forest. The trees still lie pointing away from the peak in the place called the Devastated Area.

Benjamin Loomis, who ran a hotel and store nearby, photographed the whole sequence on glass plates with a heavy view camera. His pictures were printed all over the country. Congress made Lassen a national park in 1916. Smaller blasts went on into 1917.

## One of the world's biggest lava domes

Lassen is not a classic cone like Shasta. It is a plug dome, a pile of lava too thick and pasty to flow away, which squeezed up like toothpaste and hardened in place about 27,000 years ago. At 10,457 feet it is one of the largest plug domes on Earth.

The park has all four of the main kinds of volcano within a few miles of each other: plug domes like Lassen, a shield volcano at Prospect Peak, a cinder cone called Cinder Cone, and the eroded stump of a stratovolcano. That last one is the most surprising. The ridge of peaks southwest of Lassen, including Brokeoff Mountain, is the rim of a much bigger volcano, Mount Tehama, which stood about 11,000 feet high before it collapsed and was eaten away by glaciers and steam.

## Bumpass Hell

The plumbing of old Tehama is still hot. Rain and snowmelt sink down, meet rock heated by magma a few miles below, and come back up as steam. Bumpass Hell is the largest of the park's hydrothermal basins, sixteen acres of boiling pools, mudpots and roaring vents. One vent, Big Boiler, has been measured at 322 degrees Fahrenheit, among the hottest in the world.

The colors are chemistry. Yellow is sulfur. The grays and whites are rock rotted into clay by acid. The glittering crust on some pools is pyrite, fool's gold. The rotten-egg smell is hydrogen sulfide.

The name is a warning. In the 1860s a cowboy and guide named Kendall Vanhook Bumpass found the basin and broke through the thin crust, scalding his leg. He recovered, came back to show the place to a newspaper editor, and warned him to watch his step. Then he broke through again, into boiling mud. This time he lost the leg. The boardwalk is there for a reason.

## Four peoples and one man

The Atsugewi, Yana, Yahi and Mountain Maidu all spent summers on the mountain, hunting and gathering, and left before the snow. In the 1860s settlers hunted the Yahi nearly to extinction. A small band hid in the canyons southwest of the peak for forty years. In 1911 the last of them, a man in his fifties, walked out of the hills near Oroville, starving and alone. He lived his last five years at a museum in San Francisco, where he was known as Ishi, the Yahi word for "man." He never told anyone his own name.

## Look for on the trail

- **Vulcan's Eye**, a dark patch of rock high on the peak, on the way up.
- **The summit craters**, including the black tongue of 1915 lava.
- **Mount Shasta**, 75 miles north, from the top.
- **Snow in August.** The area around Lassen gets some of the deepest snowfall in California, often more than 30 feet in a winter.
- **Burned forest.** The Dixie Fire of 2021 burned more than two thirds of the park.
- **Whitebark pines** near treeline, twisted and now threatened across the West.
