# Volcano Country

The northeast corner of California is the least visited part of the state and the most volcanic. The Cascade Range, the chain of volcanoes that runs from British Columbia through Mount Rainier and Mount St. Helens, ends here with two giants: Mount Shasta and Lassen Peak. East of them stretches the Modoc Plateau, a high tableland of lava flows, cinder cones and sagebrush.

## Why there are volcanoes here

Offshore of far northern California, a small piece of ocean floor called the Gorda Plate is sliding under North America. About sixty miles down it gets hot enough to release water into the rock above, which lowers the melting point, and magma rises. South of Cape Mendocino the plates slide sideways past each other instead, so there is nothing to feed volcanoes. That is why the chain stops at Lassen.

## Mount Shasta

You will see Shasta on the way to anything else up here. At 14,179 feet it stands almost alone, two miles above the surrounding country, and holds seven named glaciers. It is a stack of at least four overlapping volcanoes built over several hundred thousand years, including the big side cone called Shastina. The U.S. Geological Survey says magma last reached the surface there about 3,200 years ago, and it rates Shasta as one of the most dangerous volcanoes in the country because of the towns at its feet. The mountain is sacred to the Wintu, Shasta, Karuk, Modoc and Pit River peoples. It has also attracted an unusual number of legends since the 1880s, including a hidden city of survivors from a lost continent.
