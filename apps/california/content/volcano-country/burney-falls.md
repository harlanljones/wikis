# Burney Falls

> **On your hike list:** Falls Loop Trail, McArthur-Burney Falls Memorial State Park (1 mile loop)

Most waterfalls shrink to a trickle by the end of a California summer. Burney Falls pours about a hundred million gallons a day in August, the same as in April, and a good part of it comes straight out of the middle of the cliff. The explanation is under your feet.

## A river that runs underground

The country around the falls is made of basalt lava flows, stacked one on another over the last few million years. Basalt cracks as it cools, and the tops and bottoms of flows are rubbly and full of holes. Rain and snowmelt from a huge area sink right in. There are few streams on the surface. The water travels underground through the porous layers instead.

Burney Creek itself often vanishes into its bed about half a mile above the falls in summer, then reappears from springs just upstream. At the cliff, the creek goes over the top in two main streams. Below them, water sprays out along a line in the rock face where a porous layer sits on top of a dense one that water cannot get through. The hidden river simply runs out of rock.

Spring water stays cold. It comes out at about 42 degrees Fahrenheit all year, and the pool at the bottom rarely gets much warmer. The mist cools the whole bowl, and you can feel the temperature fall as you walk down the trail.

## Whose falls

For the Ilmawi, one of the bands of the Pit River people, the falls are a sacred place, and they still are. The Pit River got its English name from the pits its people dug along game trails to trap deer.

The name Burney comes from Samuel Burney, a Scottish settler who lived in the valley in the 1850s. The McArthur family arrived in the 1860s and became the area's main ranchers and storekeepers. Around 1920, a power company was buying up the Pit River and its side streams for dams. Frank McArthur bought the land around the falls and gave it to the state, in memory of his pioneer parents, so that it could not be flooded or dried up. It became one of California's earliest state parks. The reservoir you can walk to, Lake Britton, is what the rest of the river got.

Theodore Roosevelt is supposed to have called the falls the eighth wonder of the world. The story is on every sign, and nobody has found where he said it.

## Birds behind the water

Black swifts are among the least known birds in North America. They nest only on wet cliffs, often behind waterfalls, and Burney Falls has one of the best known colonies in California. They arrive in late spring and spend the day hunting insects high in the air, out of sight. Watch at dusk for dark, sickle-winged birds that fly straight at the falls and disappear into the spray. In winter they go to the Amazon, a fact nobody knew until tiny tracking tags were fitted to a few of them around 2010.

## Look for on the trail

- **Two ages of rock** in the cliff. The falls are 129 feet high and slowly eating their way upstream as blocks of basalt break off.
- **Moss and ferns** fed by spray, on a cliff surrounded by dry pine forest.
- **Ponderosa pine bark**, which smells like vanilla or butterscotch if you put your nose in a furrow on a warm day.
- **Pacific Crest Trail hikers.** The trail crosses the park on its way from Mexico to Canada, and the camp store is a famous resupply stop.
- **Civilian Conservation Corps stonework** on the trail and the old cabins.
- **Ospreys and bald eagles** over Lake Britton.
