# Lava Beds

> **On your hike list:** Captain Jack's Stronghold Trail, Lava Beds National Monument (1.5 mile outer loop)

In the winter of 1872 to 1873, between fifty and sixty Modoc fighting men held a patch of broken lava against an American army that grew to nearly a thousand soldiers. It was the only major Indian war fought in California, and for the number of people involved, one of the most expensive wars the United States ever fought.

## The biggest volcano you have never heard of

Lava Beds sits on the north flank of Medicine Lake Volcano. It is a shield volcano, broad and low like the ones in Hawaii, so it does not look like much. By volume it is the largest volcano in the Cascade Range, bigger than Shasta. It has erupted many times in the last few thousand years, most recently about 950 years ago, when it produced the Glass Mountain obsidian flow.

Runny basalt lava builds tubes. The surface of a flow cools and crusts over while lava keeps running underneath, and when the eruption stops, the tube drains and leaves a cave. The monument has hundreds of them, the densest collection in North America. Some hold ice all year. Where the roofs fell in, they left trenches, pits and ridges. The Stronghold is a field of these.

## Why the Modoc fought

The Modoc lived around Tule Lake and Lost River, on what is now the California and Oregon line. In 1864 they signed a treaty and moved to a reservation in Oregon, which they had to share with the Klamath, who far outnumbered them and on whose land it sat. The Modoc were harassed and hungry. A leader named Kintpuash, known to settlers as Captain Jack, twice took his band home to Lost River and asked for a small reservation there.

On November 29, 1872, soldiers came to the Lost River village to force them back. Someone fired. The Modoc scattered, and one group, attacked by settlers, killed fourteen settler men in revenge. Then they all slipped across the lake to the lava.

## The Stronghold

About 150 people lived in the Stronghold for five months, most of them women and children. The men knew every crack. They piled rock walls across gaps, moved through the trenches unseen, and could cover any approach.

On January 17, 1873, more than 300 soldiers and volunteers attacked in thick fog. They never saw a single Modoc. By dark the Army had 37 dead and wounded, and the Modoc had lost nobody.

The government tried talking. Kintpuash wanted peace and a home on Lost River. Others in the band, wanted for the killings of the settlers, expected to be hanged whatever happened, and shamed him into a desperate plan. At a peace meeting in April 1873, Kintpuash drew a revolver and shot General Edward Canby. Canby was the only general of the regular Army killed in all the Indian wars.

After that there was no chance of terms. The Army shelled the Stronghold with mortars and cut it off from the lake. The Modoc got away once more, at night, through a gap in the lines, but they were out of water and food, and the band broke up. Kintpuash surrendered on June 1. He and three others were hanged at Fort Klamath that October. Their heads were cut off and sent to the Army Medical Museum in Washington. The 153 survivors were sent in cattle cars to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma.

Some Modoc were allowed back to Oregon in 1909. Kintpuash's skull was returned to his descendants in 1984.

## Look for on the trail

- **Rock walls and firing positions**, still where they were stacked in 1873.
- **Captain Jack's cave** and the smaller caves where families lived.
- **The dry lake bed.** Tule Lake came to the edge of the Stronghold then. Nearly all of it was drained for farmland in the twentieth century.
- **Medicine flags and offerings.** Modoc descendants come here. Leave everything as it is.
- **Petroglyph Point**, a short drive away, with thousands of carvings on a cliff that was once an island.
- **Nearby**, the remains of the Tule Lake Segregation Center, the largest of the World War II camps for Japanese Americans.
