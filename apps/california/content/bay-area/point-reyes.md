# Point Reyes

> **On your hike list:** Alamere Falls (13.2 miles round trip) · Point Reyes Lighthouse (1.3 miles, 313 steps) · Tomales Point Trail (9.5 miles round trip)

On a map, Point Reyes looks like it was glued onto California as an afterthought. That is close to the truth. The peninsula sits on a different tectonic plate from the rest of Marin, and it has traveled a long way to get here.

## A peninsula on the move

The long, straight valley that holds Tomales Bay, Olema and Bolinas Lagoon is the San Andreas Fault. East of it is the North American Plate. West of it, Point Reyes rides the Pacific Plate northwest at about two inches a year, roughly the speed your fingernails grow.

The granite under the lighthouse and Tomales Point is the giveaway. It matches rock in the Tehachapi Mountains and the southern end of the Sierra Nevada, more than 300 miles to the southeast. The peninsula has been sliding up the coast for millions of years.

It does not slide smoothly. The fault sticks, strain builds, and then it lets go. On April 18, 1906, it let go along nearly 300 miles. San Francisco burned, and at the head of Tomales Bay the ground west of the fault jumped as much as 20 feet north in under a minute. A fence near the Bear Valley Visitor Center was cut in two and offset 16 feet. A rebuilt version stands on the short Earthquake Trail.

## Drake and the Coast Miwok

Coast Miwok people lived in more than a hundred villages around these bays. In the summer of 1579 they met Francis Drake, who careened the *Golden Hind* somewhere on this coast for five weeks of repairs during his voyage around the world. Most historians put the landing at Drakes Bay, under white cliffs that reminded the English of Dover. The name Point Reyes came later, from Sebastián Vizcaíno, who passed on January 6, 1603, the Feast of the Three Kings.

## Butter, fog and a very bright light

After the Gold Rush the peninsula became one of the great dairy districts of the West. Its "alphabet ranches," lettered A through Z, shipped butter to San Francisco by schooner.

The headland is among the windiest and foggiest places on the Pacific coast, and ships kept hitting it. The lighthouse went up in 1870, and it was built low on the cliff on purpose so that the beam would shine under the fog. That is why you walk down 313 steps to reach it. The original first-order Fresnel lens is still inside, more than a thousand pieces of hand-ground glass.

## The elk come back

Tule elk live only in California. Hunting and farming reduced them from perhaps half a million to a handful by the 1870s, when a rancher near Bakersfield protected the last few. In 1978 ten elk were released on Tomales Point behind a fence. The herd grew into the hundreds, and the fence became a long-running fight, especially in drought years when animals died behind it. The Park Service began taking the fence down in December 2024, and a deal announced in January 2025 arranged for most of the seashore's cattle ranches to wind down.

The seashore exists at all because of timing. Developers had already begun subdividing above Limantour Beach when President Kennedy signed the bill creating it in 1962.

## Look for on the trail

- **Alamere Falls** is a tidefall, one of a handful of waterfalls in California that drop straight onto a beach.
- **The lakes on the Alamere hike**, such as Bass and Pelican, sit in hollows made by enormous old landslides.
- **Bull elk bugling** on Tomales Point from August into October.
- **Gray whales** from the lighthouse, heading south in January and north with calves in March and April.
- **Pierce Point Ranch** at the Tomales trailhead, a preserved dairy from the 1860s.
