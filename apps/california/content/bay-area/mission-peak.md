# Mission Peak

> **On your hike list:** Mission Peak, from the Stanford Avenue staging area (about 6.2 miles round trip, 2,100 feet of climbing)

Mission Peak is the steep green (or gold) wall behind Fremont, 2,517 feet high. Its hiking fame is recent. The reason it has a name is about 230 years old and sits at its foot.

## The mission

In June 1797 Father Fermín Lasuén founded Mission San José, the fourteenth of California's twenty-one missions, on the plain below the peak. The site was chosen because it sat among large Ohlone villages and commanded the pass that led inland to the Central Valley.

It became one of the most productive missions in the chain. At its height in the early 1830s nearly two thousand Native people lived there, Ohlone at first and then Miwok and Yokuts people brought from the interior, tending well over ten thousand cattle. It was also a place of terrible mortality. Crowding and European diseases killed people faster than babies were born, and thousands are buried in the mission cemeteries. In 1829 a Yokuts man named Estanislao, who had been an alcalde at the mission, led a revolt from the San Joaquin Valley and beat back two Mexican military expeditions before a third defeated him. Stanislaus County carries his name.

The adobe church collapsed in the great Hayward Fault earthquake of 1868. The one standing today is a careful reconstruction from the 1980s. The Ohlone are still here, too. The Muwekma Ohlone Tribe, made up of descendants of people from Mission San José and its neighbors, has spent decades seeking the return of federal recognition.

## A peak made of ocean and squeezed by faults

The summit ridge is built of sandstone and shale laid down on the seafloor, and hikers who look closely can find fossil shells. The mountain is high because the land east of San Francisco Bay is being squeezed between faults. The Hayward Fault runs along the base of the hills, right under the neighborhoods you drive through, and the Calaveras Fault runs behind the ridge. The 1868 Hayward quake was known as "the Great San Francisco Earthquake" until 1906 took the title. Geologists consider the Hayward Fault one of the most dangerous in the country because so many people now live on top of it.

The bumpy, stair-stepped slope you climb is one very large, very old landslide complex. Part of it moved again in the wet winter of 1998.

## Grass, cattle and the pole

The peak is almost treeless, and it has been grazed since mission days. The grass itself is an import. Wild oats and other Mediterranean annuals arrived with Spanish livestock and replaced California's native bunchgrasses across the state within a few decades. They are the reason the hills turn gold in summer.

The pole at the summit, known as "Mission Peeker," was put up in 1990 by a park ranger. Its sighting tubes point to landmarks around the Bay. It weighs several hundred pounds and has become the most photographed post in the East Bay.

## Look for on the trail

- **Sighting tubes** on the pole aimed at Mount Tamalpais, Mount Diablo, Mount Hamilton and more.
- **Mount Diablo** to the north. In 1851 surveyors made its summit the starting point for the land grid that divides most of California and all of Nevada.
- **Golden eagles and turkey vultures** riding the updraft on the west face. Hang gliders use the same lift.
- **The salt ponds** at the south end of the Bay, in shades of green and rust, now being turned back into tidal marsh.
