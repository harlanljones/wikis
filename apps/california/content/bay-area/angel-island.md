# Angel Island

> **On your hike list:** Angel Island: Mt. Livermore and the Immigration Station (about 5 miles, by ferry)

Angel Island is the largest natural island in San Francisco Bay, and nearly every chapter of California history has used it for something. Coast Miwok people paddled out in tule boats to hunt and fish. A Spanish ship hid in its cove. The Army fortified it against Confederates who never came. And for thirty years it was the door to America that was kept deliberately hard to open.

## Ayala's anchorage

In August 1775 Juan Manuel de Ayala brought the *San Carlos* through the Golden Gate, the first European ship to enter the Bay, and anchored in the sheltered cove where the ferry now docks. He named the island for Our Lady of the Angels. His pilot spent weeks in a longboat charting the Bay, and those charts opened the way for the mission and presidio at San Francisco the following year.

## The Immigration Station

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred Chinese laborers from entering the United States. It was the first American law to shut out a whole nationality, and it stayed on the books until 1943. Merchants, students and the children of citizens could still come, so the government built a station to sort out who was who. It opened on Angel Island in January 1910.

At Ellis Island most arrivals were through in hours. Here, Chinese immigrants were held for weeks, months, and in some cases close to two years, while inspectors grilled them on details like how many steps led to their family's front door. The 1906 earthquake had burned San Francisco's birth records, which let many men claim citizenship and then bring over "paper sons." The interrogations were designed to catch them, and families memorized coaching books to get through.

The men in the barracks carved poems into the wooden walls in classical Chinese. More than two hundred survive, full of anger, homesickness and waiting. They were nearly lost. The station closed after a fire in 1940, and the barracks were slated for demolition in 1970 when a park ranger named Alexander Weiss noticed the carvings and told people who understood what they were.

Estimates of how many people passed through vary a lot, but the usual figures are several hundred thousand immigrants from some eighty countries, including as many as 175,000 Chinese.

## The mountain that got its top back

Mount Livermore, the summit, is 788 feet high. For half a century it was sixteen feet shorter: the Army bulldozed the top flat in the 1950s for a Nike missile radar site. The state rebuilt the peak in 2002, which is why the summit trail feels newer than everything around it.

## Look for on the trail

- **Camp Reynolds** on the west side, a Civil War post from 1863 with a row of officers' houses still facing the parade ground.
- **Fort McDowell** on the east side, where soldiers shipped out to and came home from the Philippines and two world wars.
- **The poems** in the detention barracks, now a museum. Ask which wall carvings have been translated.
- **Blue-green rock** along the perimeter road. It is blueschist, formed deep in a subduction zone.
