# Mount Tamalpais

> **On your hike list:** Steep Ravine and Dipsea Loop, from Pantoll (about 3.9 miles, 1,200 feet of climbing)

Mount Tamalpais is only 2,571 feet high, but it rises straight from sea level and it has been a playground for San Francisco for more than a century. People here invented at least three ways to go up and down a mountain for fun.

## The name

Tamalpais comes from the Coast Miwok words usually given as *tamal pajis*, roughly "west hill" or "coast mountain." The popular story that the ridge is a "Sleeping Maiden" from a Miwok legend is a later invention by settlers, not a Miwok tradition.

## The Crookedest Railroad in the World

In 1896 a steam railroad opened from Mill Valley to a tavern just below the East Peak. It climbed 2,200 feet in a little over eight miles and needed 281 curves to do it, including a "Double Bow Knot" where the track ran parallel to itself five times. The ride down was the best part. Passengers sat in open "gravity cars" with a brakeman and coasted all the way to Mill Valley or down into [[bay-area/muir-woods|Muir Woods]], with nothing powering them but the hill. The automobile and a big fire in 1929 ended the line, and the tracks came up in 1930. The Old Railroad Grade is now a fire road, and its gentle slope gave the next invention a place to happen.

## The Dipsea

The stairs and trail you climb out of Steep Ravine are part of the Dipsea, a 7.4-mile route from Mill Valley over the shoulder of the mountain to Stinson Beach. It began in 1904 as a bet between members of the Olympic Club over who could get to the Dipsea Inn fastest. The race has been run nearly every year since 1905, which makes it the oldest trail race in the country. It is handicapped by age and sex, so the winner is as likely to be a nine-year-old or a seventy-year-old as a college runner.

## Clunkers

In the early 1970s a group of Marin cyclists started hauling heavy 1930s balloon-tire bikes up the mountain and racing them down a fire road so steep that the coaster brakes cooked their grease. They called the course Repack, because the hub had to be repacked after every run. Out of those races came purpose-built frames, and out of those came the mountain bike.

## Steep Ravine

The ravine itself is a small wet world. Webb Creek drops over a series of ledges under redwoods and Douglas firs, and the trail goes right up the middle of it, including the ten-foot wooden ladder beside a waterfall. Many of the bridges, steps and stonework in the park were built by the Civilian Conservation Corps in the 1930s. The redwoods survive on this exposed coast because the ravine funnels fog, and fog drip can give them a third or more of their yearly water.

## Look for on the trail

- **Green rock outcrops** of serpentinite, with thin soils that grow rare wildflowers and stunted cypress.
- **The Mountain Theater**, a stone amphitheater near Rock Spring that has staged a play every spring since 1913.
- **The change at the ridgeline.** Redwood canyon on one side, open grass and coyote brush on the other, sorted by wind and fog.
- **The Farallon Islands**, on a clear day, 30 miles out. They sit on the far side of the San Andreas Fault, on the same granite as [[bay-area/point-reyes|Point Reyes]].
