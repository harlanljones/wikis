# Armstrong Redwoods

> **On your hike list:** Armstrong Redwoods Pioneer Trail, Guerneville (1.5 miles round trip)

The town at the gate of this grove used to be called Stumptown. That tells you most of what happened to the redwoods of the Russian River, and it makes the survival of these 800 acres more surprising.

## Stumptown

The lower Russian River valley once held some of the densest timber anywhere. The flats where Guerneville stands were known as the Big Bottom, and loggers claimed single acres there yielded more board feet than any other ground they had seen. Mills arrived in the 1860s. A railroad followed in the 1870s to carry the lumber to the Bay, and within a generation the valley floor was a field of stumps. The town was renamed for George Guerne, who owned the biggest sawmill.

## The colonel

Colonel James Armstrong was one of those lumbermen. He came west from Ohio after the Civil War, ran a mill, and dealt in timber land. In the 1870s he decided that one grove should be left alone as a natural park and botanical garden. He put it in the name of his daughter Kate.

Armstrong died in 1900 before the plan was secure. His daughter Lizzie Armstrong Jones and a family friend, Harrison LeBaron, campaigned for years to keep the trees standing, and in 1917 the voters of Sonoma County approved buying the grove for $80,000. The state took it over in 1934.

## The trees

- The **Colonel Armstrong Tree** is the oldest in the grove, more than 1,400 years old. It was a sapling around the time the Maya were building Tikal.
- The **Parson Jones Tree** is the tallest, about 310 feet, longer than a football field. It is named for Lizzie's husband, a minister.
- The **Icicle Tree** shows off burls, the knobby growths packed with dormant buds that let a redwood sprout after injury.

Coast redwoods this far from the ocean depend on the river valley, which draws summer fog inland on most evenings. Climb the ridge behind the grove into the neighboring Austin Creek hills and the trees give way to oak and grass within a mile.

## Fire comes through

In August 2020 the Walbridge Fire, started by lightning, burned down into the reserve. Crews and the trees' own armor kept most of the old growth alive. Redwood bark can be a foot thick, it holds water, and it has no flammable resin. Look for blackened trunks with fresh green fuzz sprouting straight out of the bark, which is how a redwood regrows a scorched crown.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Forest Theater**, an outdoor amphitheater built in the 1930s where the acoustics come from the trees.
- **Redwood sorrel** carpeting the ground. Its clover-like leaves fold down like umbrellas when direct sun hits them.
- **Banana slugs** after rain, and **goosepens**, the fire-hollowed trunks that settlers used for keeping poultry.
- **Burn marks from 2020** alongside much older scars.
