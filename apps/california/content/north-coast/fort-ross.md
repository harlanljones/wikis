# Fort Ross

> **On your hike list:** Fort Ross Compound and Sandy Cove, Fort Ross State Historic Park (short walks on the coastal bluffs)

For twenty-nine years the flag of the Russian-American Company flew over a redwood stockade on the Sonoma coast. Fort Ross was the southern tip of an empire that reached from Siberia across Alaska, and it is the reason California has a Russian River.

## Why Russia came

Russian fur traders had followed the sea otter east across the Aleutians in the 1700s. Otter fur is the densest of any animal, up to a million hairs per square inch, and in China a single pelt could sell for more than a sailor earned in a year. By 1800 the company's Alaskan colonies had two problems. They were running out of otters, and they could not grow enough food to keep from starving.

In 1812 a company officer named Ivan Kuskov landed here with 25 Russians and about 80 Alaska Native hunters and built a fort. The name Ross is thought to be an old poetic form of *Rossiia*, Russia. Spain claimed this coast, and the nearest Spanish post was at San Francisco, less than a hundred miles south. The Spanish protested for years and never had the strength to do anything else.

## A very mixed town

Few of the people at Fort Ross were Russian. The hunters were Alutiiq and Unangan men from Kodiak and the Aleutians, who chased otters in skin kayaks called baidarkas. The land belonged to the Kashaya Pomo, who called the place Metini. Kashaya people worked at the fort, traded with it and married into it, and so did Coast Miwok from Bodega Bay.

The Kashaya remember the Russians as easier neighbors than those who came later. One reason is that the company came to trade, with no interest in converting anyone. The Kashaya were never taken into a mission, and their language and religion survived unusually intact.

## A colony that could not pay

The hunters were too good at their work. Within about a decade the otters along this coast were nearly gone. The company turned to farming and shipbuilding, and neither went well. Coastal fog rusted the wheat, gophers ate the gardens, and the ships built of green oak rotted in a few years. Fort Ross did manage some firsts for California: its first windmills, its first shipyard, and some of its first weather records and scientific collections.

In 1841 the company sold everything movable to John Sutter, the Swiss settler at Sacramento, for $30,000, mostly on credit. He hauled the cannon, livestock and lumber to his own fort. He was slow to pay, and by some accounts never paid in full. Seven years later gold turned up at [[gold-country/coloma|his sawmill]].

## What is original

Earthquakes and fires have been hard on the place. The chapel collapsed in 1906, when the San Andreas Fault, which comes ashore just south of the fort, slipped about twelve feet here. It was rebuilt, burned in 1970 and was rebuilt again. Only the Rotchev House, home of the last manager, is original. It is one of the few surviving buildings anywhere from Russia's time in North America south of Alaska.

## Look for on the trail

- **The corner blockhouses**, seven- and eight-sided, built to give cannon a clear field. They were never needed.
- **The cemetery** across the gulch, with Russian Orthodox three-barred crosses.
- **Sandy Cove**, where ships were built and launched.
- **The old orchard** inland, with trees descended from Russian-era plantings.
- **Offset fences and stream channels** along the road south, bent by the fault.
