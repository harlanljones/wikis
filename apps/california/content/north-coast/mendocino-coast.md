# The Mendocino Coast

> **On your hike list:** Fern Canyon Trail to Russian Gulch Falls, Russian Gulch State Park (5.4 to 6.3 miles round trip)

Mendocino looks like a New England fishing village that drifted to the wrong ocean. It was built by people from Maine, and it exists because of a shipwreck.

## The Frolic

In the summer of 1850 a fast Baltimore-built brig called the *Frolic*, a former opium runner, was sailing from China to Gold Rush San Francisco with a cargo of silk, porcelain, furniture and beer. On the night of July 25 she ran onto rocks just north of Point Cabrillo, a couple of miles from where Russian Gulch meets the sea. The crew got off and rowed to San Francisco.

Word of the cargo reached a lumberman named Harry Meiggs, who sent a salvage party north the next year. They found almost nothing, since the Pomo people living nearby had already salvaged the wreck. Archaeologists later dug up Pomo village sites with arrowheads chipped from green bottle glass and beads made from Chinese porcelain. What the salvagers did find was redwood, miles of it, growing right down to tidewater. Meiggs shipped a sawmill around Cape Horn, and it was running at the mouth of Big River by 1852. That was the beginning of Mendocino.

## Dogholes

The coast has almost no harbors. Lumber schooners had to work their way into tiny rock-bound coves, which sailors said were barely big enough for a dog to turn around in. Lumber went down to the ships on long wooden chutes from the cliff top, and later on wire cables, a sling load at a time. Passengers sometimes traveled the same way. Russian Gulch was one of dozens of these doghole ports between Bodega and Humboldt Bay. The steam schooners that served them were the main link to the outside world until the 1930s.

The name Russian Gulch probably honors hunters from [[north-coast/fort-ross|Fort Ross]], who worked this coast decades before the mills.

## A coast that is rising

The flat headlands you walk on are old sea floors. Waves cut a platform at the shoreline, the land rose, and the waves started on a new one below. In places along this coast there are five such terraces, like a flight of giant stairs, each roughly a hundred thousand years older than the one below it.

The oldest and highest have been leached by rain for so long that a hard, acid crust has formed under the soil. Trees that grow there are natural bonsai. In the "pygmy forests" inland from the coast, cypress and pine a century old may stand only a few feet tall.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Devil's Punchbowl**, a collapsed sea cave on the headland, 100 feet across and 60 deep, where the tide surges in through a tunnel.
- **The arch bridge** over the gulch, built in 1940 and named for Frederick Panhorst, the state's chief bridge engineer.
- **Second-growth redwoods** in Fern Canyon, with huge old stumps showing springboard notches, the slots where loggers stood on planks to saw above the swollen base.
- **The waterfall**, 36 feet, at the head of the canyon.
- **Point Cabrillo Light**, just north, built in 1909 near the spot where the *Frolic* went down.
