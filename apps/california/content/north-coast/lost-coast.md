# The Lost Coast

> **On your hike list:** Lost Coast Trail, north section, from Mattole Beach to Black Sands Beach (24.6 miles one way, about 3 days)

Highway 1 runs along the edge of California for hundreds of miles. Near the town of Leggett it turns sharply inland and does not see the ocean again for about 80 miles. The engineers of the 1930s took one look at the King Range and went around. What they left alone is the longest stretch of undeveloped coastline in California.

## Why there is no road

The King Range rises from the surf to the top of King Peak, 4,088 feet, in less than three miles. There is no coastal plain and hardly any terrace. The slopes are made of crumbly, sheared sandstone and mudstone that slides whenever it gets wet, and it gets very wet. Storms off the Pacific hit the range head-on and can drop more than 100 inches of rain in a year, among the highest totals in California.

## Three plates

A few miles offshore from the north end of the trail is the Mendocino Triple Junction, where three tectonic plates meet. To the south, the Pacific Plate slides past North America along the San Andreas Fault, which ends here. To the north, the small Gorda Plate dives under the continent, which is what feeds the volcanoes of the Cascades.

The junction is one of the most seismically active spots in the United States, and the land above it is rising fast. The King Range is going up about as quickly as any mountains in North America, a few millimeters a year, faster than rain can wear it down.

Sometimes it rises all at once. On April 25, 1992, a magnitude 7.2 earthquake struck near Petrolia, at the north end of the trail. Along about 15 miles of shore the coast jumped as much as four and a half feet. Tide pools full of mussels, urchins and seaweed were lifted clear of the water and died. You can still see the band of bleached rock.

## People of the coast

The Mattole and Sinkyone peoples lived here, moving between the beach in summer, for mussels, seals and surf fish, and the sheltered river valleys in winter. The shell heaps along the trail are theirs and are protected by law. In the 1850s and 1860s settlers and soldiers killed or drove out most of them in a few brutal years. In 1997 a group of tribes established the InterTribal Sinkyone Wilderness on logged-over land just south of the King Range, the first land trust of its kind in the country.

Later came ranchers, tanbark cutters and, at Petrolia in 1865, some of the first oil wells drilled in California. Congress made the King Range the nation's first National Conservation Area in 1970.

## The lighthouse nobody wanted

About three miles from the Mattole trailhead stands the Punta Gorda Lighthouse, a squat concrete tower lit in 1912 after a string of wrecks. Everything it needed came in by horse along the beach, and in winter the keepers were cut off for weeks. The Coast Guard called it the Alcatraz of lighthouses, a post for men who had annoyed their superiors. It was shut down in 1951.

## Look for on the trail

- **The uplifted platform** near Cape Mendocino and the Mattole, with its dead white band of tide-pool life.
- **Black sand**, ground from the dark sandstone of the range.
- **Elephant seals** on the beach near the lighthouse, a colony that started in the 1990s.
- **Impassable zones.** Three stretches of the trail are covered at high tide, because the mountains leave no room for a beach.
- **Bear tracks.** Black bears work the tideline here, which is why food canisters are required.
