# Humboldt Redwoods

> **On your hike list:** Humboldt Redwoods and the Avenue of the Giants, Founders Grove (32 mile scenic drive, 0.5 mile grove trail)

Humboldt Redwoods State Park holds the largest stretch of old-growth coast redwood left anywhere, about 17,000 acres. It exists because a road was built through the forest at just the right moment, and three men drove up it.

## The road trip of 1917

For most of the 1800s the redwoods of the Eel River were too remote to cut. Then the railroad arrived, and in the 1910s the new Redwood Highway was pushed through the best groves. Loggers followed the road, and so did tourists.

In August 1917 three prominent scientists drove north to see the trees: John C. Merriam, a paleontologist, Henry Fairfield Osborn, head of the American Museum of Natural History, and Madison Grant, a New York lawyer and zoologist. They found the highway lined with fresh stumps and learned that not one acre of the northern redwoods was in public ownership. The next year they founded the Save the Redwoods League.

The League's method was simple and has barely changed. It raises private money, buys groves from timber companies, and hands them to the state. Donors could have a grove named for them or for someone they loved, which is why the Avenue of the Giants is lined with hundreds of small signs. The first, the Bolling Grove, was dedicated in 1921. Much of the early work was done by women's clubs in Humboldt County, who lobbied, raised money and stood in front of loggers.

One uncomfortable footnote belongs here. Grant and Osborn were also leading promoters of eugenics and of "scientific" racism, and Grant's writing was praised by the Nazis. The League and the state parks have acknowledged this publicly, and in 2021 Grant's memorial was removed from Prairie Creek Redwoods.

## The Rockefeller Forest

The heart of the park is Bull Creek Flats. In the 1920s the League took John D. Rockefeller Jr. on a picnic there, and he gave two million dollars, which the state matched, to buy 10,000 acres from the Pacific Lumber Company. It is the largest unbroken old-growth redwood forest in the world.

It was nearly lost anyway. The slopes above it were logged bare after World War II. In December 1955 and again in December 1964, huge storms sent mud, gravel and logs down Bull Creek, which tore out its banks and toppled more than 500 ancient trees. The lesson reshaped conservation: you cannot save a grove without saving the hills that drain into it. The League went back and bought the whole watershed.

## The Dyerville Giant

Founders Grove, where you walk, is dedicated to the three men of 1917. Its most famous tree is on the ground. The Dyerville Giant stood about 362 feet when measured, and close to 370 counting its broken top, and was probably around 1,600 years old. In March 1991, after a week of rain, a neighbor fell against it and it went over. People a mile away thought they had heard a train wreck. The trunk is longer than a football field, and walking its length is the best way to grasp the scale of a redwood. It will take centuries to rot, feeding ferns, fungi and young trees the whole time.

## The floods

The Eel River is quiet in summer and violent in winter. In the flood of December 1964 it rose more than 30 feet over the highway in places and wiped out whole towns, including Dyerville and most of Weott. High-water marks along the Avenue stand far above your head. Redwoods on the flats are built for this. When silt buries their roots, they grow a new set higher up the trunk.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Founders Tree**, about 346 feet tall, with its lowest branch nearly 200 feet off the ground.
- **The root plate** of the Dyerville Giant. Redwoods have no taproot, only shallow roots that spread wide and knit together with their neighbors'.
- **Flood markers** at Weott.
- **Memorial grove signs**, a record of who paid for what you are looking at.
- **The Lolangkok Sinkyone**, whose country this is, are named on newer park signs.
