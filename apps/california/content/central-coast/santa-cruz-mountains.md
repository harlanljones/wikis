# The Santa Cruz Mountains

> **On your hike list:** Redwood Grove Loop, Henry Cowell (0.8 miles) · Bear Creek Redwoods Preserve (loops up to 7 miles) · Portola Redwoods State Park (Old Tree Trail and Tiptoe Falls) · Uvas Canyon Waterfall Loop (about 1 mile)

The Santa Cruz Mountains run from San Francisco to the Pajaro River, a low, wet, heavily forested range squeezed up along the San Andreas Fault. Four hikes on the list sit in four different corners of it, and together they tell one story: cut nearly everything, then spend a century getting it back.

## A range built by a bend

The San Andreas Fault runs the length of these mountains, and here it bends slightly to the left. Two plates trying to slide past each other at a bend get jammed together, and the crust has nowhere to go but up. The range is still rising. On October 17, 1989, the Loma Prieta earthquake began about eleven miles under the forest of Nisene Marks, not far from Bear Creek, and lifted parts of the range by more than a foot in fifteen seconds.

The mountains squeeze rain out of Pacific storms. The wet western side grows redwoods. The eastern slope, where Uvas Canyon sits, is drier, though Uvas is a wet pocket that catches enough rain to keep its string of waterfalls running through spring. *Uvas* is Spanish for grapes, after the wild grapevines along the creek.

## The first redwoods on record

Europeans first described redwoods here. In October 1769 the Portolá expedition, heading north along the coast, wrote of very tall trees with red wood and called them *palo colorado*. Portola Redwoods State Park is named for the expedition's leader. Its Old Tree is about twelve feet across and close to 300 feet tall.

## A resort instead of a sawmill

After 1850 the range became the lumberyard for San Francisco and San José. Flumes, oxen and narrow-gauge railroads moved the wood. Lime kilns, which burned redwood to cook limestone into mortar, ate even more forest. Henry Cowell, whose name is on the park in Felton, made his fortune in lime.

The grove at Henry Cowell survived because someone found a way to make money from standing trees. In 1867 Joseph Welch bought the Big Trees Grove and opened it as a resort, with a dance floor, a hotel and a railroad stop. Presidents Benjamin Harrison and Theodore Roosevelt both came to see it. One hollow tree is named for John C. Frémont, who was said to have camped inside it in 1846. Asked about that years later, Frémont reportedly said, "It's a good story, let it stand."

The trip that led to the founding of [[central-coast/big-basin|Big Basin]] began here too. In 1899 the photographer Andrew P. Hill was thrown out of the grove by its owner for taking pictures, and left angry enough to start a movement.

## A college in the woods

Bear Creek Redwoods was logged, then became a rich family's estate, and from 1934 to 1969 was Alma College, where Jesuit priests were trained. The site sits almost directly on the San Andreas Fault. The preserve opened to the public in 2019, and the old growth that remains there survived on slopes too awkward to log.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Frémont Tree** at Henry Cowell. You can stand inside it. Bring a light.
- **The tallest tree** at Henry Cowell, roughly 275 feet, and around 1,500 years old.
- **Albino redwoods.** Henry Cowell has several of these rare white-needled sprouts, which have no chlorophyll and live off their parent tree's roots.
- **Second-growth rings** everywhere, each one circling a stump from the 1800s.
- **Tiptoe Falls** at Portola, small and best after rain.
- **Sag ponds and straight valleys** near Bear Creek, the marks of the fault.
