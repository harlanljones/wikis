# Point Lobos

> **On your hike list:** Cypress Grove Trail and Whalers Cabin, Point Lobos State Natural Reserve (0.8 mile loop, plus other short trails)

The painter Francis McComas called Point Lobos "the greatest meeting of land and water in the world," and the line has stuck to it ever since. It is also a compact museum of how people have made a living from the California coast, one industry after another, until the place was finally left alone.

## Sea wolves

The name is short for *Punta de los Lobos Marinos*, Point of the Sea Wolves, which is what the Spanish called the barking sea lions on the offshore rocks. They are still there, and you will hear them before you see them.

The Rumsen Ohlone harvested abalone and mussels here for at least 2,500 years. Their shell middens are all over the reserve.

## A village of many trades

- **Chinese fishermen** arrived in the early 1850s, some of them sailing junks across the Pacific. They built the Whalers Cabin, which still stands on a foundation that includes whale vertebrae, and dried fish and abalone for shipment home. They were among the first commercial fishermen in California.
- **Portuguese whalers** from the Azores worked from the cove from 1862 into the 1880s. They rowed out after gray and humpback whales, towed them in and boiled the blubber on shore.
- **Japanese abalone divers** came in the 1890s. A marine biologist named Gennosuke Kodani set up a cannery with the landowner, A. M. Allan, and brought hard-hat divers from Japan. For a while they supplied most of the abalone sold in California.
- There was also a **granite quarry**, a **coal chute** at Coal Chute Point, a gravel business, and a wartime Army camp.

## Sold in lots, bought back

In the 1890s a developer mapped the point into a thousand house lots and called it Carmelito. Some sold. Allan, an engineer who bought the land in 1898, spent years buying the lots back because he thought the place too fine to build on. He also put up a toll gate, one of the first cases in California of charging the public to see scenery in order to protect it. His family sold it to the state in 1933.

In 1960 the waters off the point became the first underwater reserve in the United States.

## The cypress

Monterey cypress is planted all over the world. It grows wild in just two places, both within a few miles of each other: here at Point Lobos and across Carmel Bay at Cypress Point. These groves are leftovers from the ice ages, when cool, foggy forests covered much more of the coast. The trees hang on because the point is so foggy and windy that nothing else competes well. The orange fuzz on their branches is a green alga colored by carotene, the pigment in carrots, and does the tree no harm.

## Otters return

Sea otters were hunted for their fur until people assumed they were gone from California. In 1938 a raft of about fifty turned up near Bixby Creek, a few miles south of here. Every otter on the California coast descends from that group. You can usually find a few wrapped in the kelp of Whalers Cove.

## Look for on the trail

- **Two kinds of rock.** Pale granite about 80 million years old, and lying on top of it the Carmelo Formation, a lumpy conglomerate of pebbles dumped by underwater avalanches in a submarine canyon.
- **Whale bones** at the Whalers Cabin, and an iron try-pot used for rendering blubber.
- **Harbor seal pups** on the pocket beaches in April and May.
- **Kelp forest** just offshore, some of the tallest seaweed in the world, growing as much as a foot or two a day.
