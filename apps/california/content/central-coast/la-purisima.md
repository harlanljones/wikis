# La Purísima

> **On your hike list:** La Purísima Mission State Historic Park, Lompoc (about 1 mile around the grounds, with longer trails behind)

Most California missions today are a church and a gift shop surrounded by a city. La Purísima is the exception. It sits in open country with its workshops, barracks, water system and fields around it, which makes it the best place to understand what a mission was: less a church than a walled farming town run on forced labor.

## Twenty-one missions

Between 1769 and 1823 Franciscan priests founded twenty-one missions from San Diego to Sonoma, about a day's ride apart. Spain was short of colonists and worried about the Russians and the English. Its plan was to turn the people already living in California into Spanish-speaking Catholic farmers, and so into subjects of the crown.

Father Fermín Lasuén founded *La Purísima Concepción*, the eleventh, on December 8, 1787, in the country of the Chumash, a seagoing people with large towns along the Santa Barbara Channel. Once baptized, a person was not free to leave. Soldiers brought back runaways, and floggings were routine. People lived packed together, and measles, smallpox and other new diseases went through them again and again. About 1,500 Chumash lived at La Purísima at its height in 1804. Within a few years disease had killed about a third of them.

## The earthquake

On December 21, 1812, two big earthquakes hit the Santa Barbara coast in one morning. The first cracked the adobe walls. The second brought them down, and then heavy rains turned what was left to mud. The ruins of that first mission are still visible in the town of Lompoc.

The priests rebuilt four miles away, across the river. The new mission was laid out in a long line instead of the usual closed square, the only one built that way. One idea is that a line of buildings was easier to get out of in an earthquake.

## The revolt of 1824

By the 1820s Mexico had broken from Spain, the soldiers had not been paid in years, and the missions were expected to feed and supply them for nothing. In February 1824 a soldier at Mission Santa Inés flogged a Chumash man visiting from La Purísima. Fighting broke out that day and spread to three missions. It was the largest uprising in the history of the California missions.

At La Purísima the Chumash took control, sent the soldiers' families away unharmed, cut loopholes in the walls and mounted two old cannon. They held the mission for nearly a month. In March more than a hundred soldiers arrived from Monterey with artillery. Sixteen Chumash died in the battle. Afterward seven were executed and others sentenced to hard labor. Many families fled over the mountains to the San Joaquin Valley, and some never came back.

## Ruin and reconstruction

Mexico closed the missions in the 1830s and promised the land to the Native people who had worked it. In practice most of it went to ranchers. La Purísima was sold in 1845 for about $1,100 and used as a stable. By 1930 it was a few broken walls.

From 1934 to 1941 young men of the Civilian Conservation Corps rebuilt it. They made 110,000 adobe bricks by hand from the same clay, cut beams with period tools, and worked from the original foundations and old drawings. It is the most complete mission restoration in the state.

The Chumash are still here. The Santa Ynez Band has a reservation a short drive east.

## Look for on the trail

- **The water system**: a spring house, clay pipes, settling tanks and a fountain, built by Chumash workers. Water came by gravity from springs up the canyon.
- **The long row of rooms**: weavers, candle makers, leather workers and soldiers, furnished as they were.
- **Livestock** of the kinds the mission raised, including longhorn cattle and four-horned churro sheep.
- **El Camino Real**, the old royal road, which ran right through the grounds.
- **Burton Mesa chaparral** on the trails behind, a rare sandy-soil scrub found only in this corner of Santa Barbara County.
