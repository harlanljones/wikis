# Big Sur

> **On your hike list:** Ewoldsen Trail, Julia Pfeiffer Burns State Park (about 4.8 mile loop, 1,430 feet of climbing), with the McWay Falls overlook

The Spanish in Monterey called the wilderness down the coast *el país grande del sur*, the big country to the south, and mostly stayed out of it. It was one of the last parts of the California coast to get a road, and it still has no town to speak of.

## Mountains with their feet in the water

The Santa Lucia Range rises straight out of the Pacific. Cone Peak, a few miles south of the Ewoldsen Trail, is 5,155 feet high and stands just three miles from the surf. No coastal slope in the lower 48 states is steeper.

That steepness packs climates together. In a few miles you can go from kelp beds to redwood canyons to dry chaparral to pines. Coast redwoods reach the natural southern end of their range here, tucked into foggy creek bottoms like McWay Canyon. On the high ridges grows the Santa Lucia fir, which lives nowhere else in the world.

The rocks are a jumble: granite and marble carried north along the San Andreas system, shoved against sea-floor rocks. They are fractured and weak, and every wet winter pieces of the range fall into the sea.

## The people of the rock

The Esselen lived in these mountains for at least 6,000 years. They were one of the smallest tribes in California, and among the first to be swept into the missions, at Carmel and Soledad, in the 1770s. For a long time books described them as extinct. They were not. In 2020 the Esselen Tribe of Monterey County regained about 1,200 acres along the Little Sur River, its first land in 250 years.

## Julia Pfeiffer Burns

American and European homesteaders arrived in the 1860s and 1870s. They ran cattle, cut tanbark for tanneries and split redwood, and shipped it all out by sea from "doghole" landings, because the trail to Monterey took days.

Christopher McWay settled the canyon that has his name in the 1870s. Julia Pfeiffer, who grew up on her family's homestead up the coast, leased pasture here with her husband John Burns and ran cattle into her fifties. She was known up and down the coast as tough, funny and generous. In the 1920s a wealthy couple from the East, Lathrop and Hélène Brown, bought the McWay ranch and became her friends. When Hélène gave the land to the state in 1961, the gift came with the condition that the park be named for Julia. Hans Ewoldsen, whose name is on the trail, was the Browns' ranch foreman, and he built a small water-powered generator in the canyon that gave the ranch electricity years before power lines came.

## The road

Highway 1 through Big Sur took eighteen years to build and opened in 1937. Much of the labor came from convicts housed in camps along the route, who earned 35 cents a day and time off their sentences. Thirty-three bridges were needed, including the concrete arch at Bixby Creek.

The road has been trying to fall off the mountain ever since. In 2017 a slide at Mud Creek buried a quarter mile of highway under millions of tons of rock and added about fifteen acres of new land to California.

## McWay Falls

The 80-foot waterfall used to drop directly into the ocean. In 1983 a huge landslide just north of the cove dumped so much debris into the sea that the currents built a sandy beach under the falls. At high tide the water still reaches it.

## Look for on the trail

- **The southernmost redwoods** you are likely to hike through, with trunks blackened by the fires of 2008 and 2020.
- **Tanoak**, the tree the tanbark peelers were after. Its acorns were a preferred food across Native California.
- **Condors** soaring over the ridge. A flock has been based in Big Sur since 1997.
- **The terrace at the falls overlook**, where the Browns' house stood, with palm trees left over from the garden.
- **Gray whales** passing close to shore in winter and spring.
