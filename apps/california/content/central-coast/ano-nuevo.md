# Año Nuevo

> **On your hike list:** Año Nuevo Elephant Seal Walk, Año Nuevo State Park (3 to 4 miles round trip)

Every northern elephant seal alive is descended from a few dozen animals that hid on one Mexican island in the 1890s. Año Nuevo is where the species came back to the mainland, and it is one of the best places on Earth to stand near a two-ton wild animal that does not care that you are there.

## New Year's Point

The point got its name on January 3, 1603, when the Spanish explorer Sebastián Vizcaíno sailed past. His chaplain, Father Antonio de la Ascensión, named it *Punta de Año Nuevo* for the season. It is one of the oldest place names in the state.

For thousands of years before that it belonged to the Quiroste, an Ohlone people. Their village near here was the biggest the Portolá expedition saw on this coast in 1769. The Spanish called it Casa Grande for its huge round meeting house, and the Quiroste fed the sick and starving soldiers. The chert of Año Nuevo was valued for arrowheads and traded far inland. In 1793 a Quiroste leader named Charquin led one of the first organized attacks on a California mission, at Santa Cruz.

## Almost gone

Elephant seals carry a thick layer of blubber, and blubber boils down into excellent lamp oil. Once the whalers had thinned out the whales, they turned to the seals, which were easy to kill on the beach. By the 1880s the species was believed extinct. In 1892 a Smithsonian expedition found eight on Guadalupe Island, off Baja California, and killed seven of them for the museum's collection.

The low point may have been as few as 20 to 40 animals. Mexico protected the island in 1922, the United States followed, and the seals did the rest. The first ones turned up on Año Nuevo Island in 1955. The first pup was born on the mainland beach in 1975. Today there are more than 200,000, and their rookeries stretch from Baja to Point Reyes. Because they all come from so few ancestors, they are nearly identical genetically, which worries biologists.

## A year in the life

Elephant seals spend most of their lives alone at sea, and they are among the deepest-diving mammals known. They routinely feed 1,000 to 2,500 feet down, and tagged animals have gone below 5,000 feet, staying under for more than an hour. Females range across the open North Pacific. Males head for the Aleutians.

They come ashore twice a year, once to breed and once to molt.

| When | What is happening on the beach |
| --- | --- |
| December | Bulls arrive and fight for rank. A big one is 16 feet long and can weigh 5,000 pounds. |
| January to February | Females give birth and nurse. Pups go from about 75 pounds to 300 in four weeks. |
| March | Adults leave. Weaned pups teach themselves to swim. |
| April to August | Seals return in shifts to molt, shedding skin and fur in patches. |
| September to November | Young seals haul out to rest. |

Bulls and nursing mothers do not eat or drink the entire time they are ashore, which can be three months.

## Look for on the trail

- **The bull's nose**, the inflatable trunk that gives the species its name and works as an amplifier for his drumbeat threat call. Each bull's rhythm is his own.
- **Sand flipping.** Seals toss sand over their backs to stay cool.
- **The abandoned light station** on the island, now occupied by sea lions, with seals lounging in the old keeper's house.
- **The San Gregorio Fault**, which slices across the point and lifted the old sea floor into the flat terrace you walk on.
- **Dairy barns** near the entrance, left from the ranches that covered this coast after the Gold Rush.
