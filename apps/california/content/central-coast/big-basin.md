# Big Basin

> **On your hike list:** Big Basin Redwood Loop, Big Basin Redwoods State Park (0.6 mile loop)

Big Basin is where California first decided that a forest could be worth more standing than cut. It became the state's first state park in 1902. In 2020 nearly all of it burned in a single night, and it is now the best place in the state to watch a redwood forest put itself back together.

## A photographer gets thrown out

In 1899 a San José painter and photographer named Andrew P. Hill was hired to take pictures of redwoods for a British magazine. He went to the privately owned grove near Felton, now part of Henry Cowell, and the owner accused him of trespassing and demanded his negatives. Hill refused, and missed his train home fuming. The trees, he decided, should belong to everyone.

The following May, Hill and a small group of professors, writers and clubwomen camped at the foot of Slippery Rock in Big Basin. They passed a hat, collected $32, and founded the Sempervirens Club, named for *Sequoia sempervirens*, the coast redwood. Loggers were already working toward the basin. The club lobbied through the winter, with help from a Jesuit college president and the president of Stanford, and in 1901 the legislature voted $250,000 to buy the land. California Redwood Park opened in 1902 with 3,800 acres.

It was an early win for a new idea. Many of its most effective campaigners were women who could not yet vote.

## The night of August 18, 2020

In August 2020 a dry lightning storm swept in from the ocean during a heat wave and started hundreds of fires around the Bay Area. Several in the Santa Cruz Mountains merged into the CZU Lightning Complex. On the evening of August 18, driven by wind, it ran through Big Basin. It burned about 97 percent of the park and destroyed the 1930s headquarters, the lodge, the museum, the campgrounds and nearly every other building.

Early reports said the ancient redwoods were gone. They were not.

## What a redwood does next

Old redwoods are built for this. Their bark is thick and fibrous and contains almost no resin. Under it, all the way up the trunk, lie dormant buds. Within weeks of the fire the blackened columns began to turn fuzzy green as thousands of those buds opened, and new sprouts rose in rings from the roots. Researchers studying the regrowth found that some sprouts came from buds that had been waiting under the bark for centuries, fed by sugar the trees had stored decades earlier.

The Douglas firs and many of the tanoaks did not survive, so the forest is brighter and more open than it was. Most of the old-growth redwoods are alive. The park is being rebuilt on purpose with fewer buildings in the old grove.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Mother of the Forest**, historically the park's tallest tree at about 329 feet before a storm broke its top, and the **Father of the Forest**, roughly 2,000 years old.
- **Green fuzz on black trunks.** That is crown regrowth from buds under the bark.
- **Fire-following flowers and shrubs**, some from seeds that had waited in the soil since the last big burn.
- **Acorn woodpeckers and other birds** using the new openings.
- **Goosepens**, the hollowed bases of trees burned out by this fire and by many before it.
