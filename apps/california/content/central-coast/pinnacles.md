# Pinnacles

> **On your hike list:** High Peaks to Bear Gulch Loop, Pinnacles National Park (6.7 mile loop, 1,425 feet of climbing)

The spires of Pinnacles are the eroded remains of a volcano. The odd part is that the volcano erupted about 195 miles away, in what is now Los Angeles County, and the two halves have been drifting apart ever since.

## Half a volcano

About 23 million years ago a field of volcanoes erupted near the present-day town of Lancaster, building a pile of lava and ash perhaps 8,000 feet high. It happened to sit right on top of the San Andreas Fault. The fault split it. The western part, riding the Pacific Plate, started north.

In the 1950s and 1960s geologists noticed that the rocks at Pinnacles were a near-perfect match for an obscure patch of volcanic rock called the Neenach Volcanics, on the other side of the fault at the edge of the Mojave Desert. Same minerals, same age, same sequence of layers. It was one of the first solid measurements of how far the San Andreas has moved, and it helped persuade doubters that whole plates really do slide.

The trip is not over. Pinnacles is still moving northwest at an inch or so a year. The fault today runs a few miles east of the park, and this stretch creeps steadily rather than sticking. In the town of Hollister, on a branch of the same fault system, the creep bends curbs and walls out of line.

## Why it looks like this

The bumpy red rock of the High Peaks is volcanic breccia: broken chunks of lava welded together in ash. As the block traveled and tilted, water and frost widened its cracks into fins and towers.

The caves are a different trick. Bear Gulch and Balconies are talus caves, formed where boulders fell into narrow canyons and wedged overhead to make a roof. More than a dozen kinds of bats use them, and parts close in season for a colony of Townsend's big-eared bats.

## A homesteader's park

Schuyler Hain arrived from Michigan in 1891, took up a homestead and spent twenty years leading tours through the caves and writing letters to anyone who might protect them. Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed Pinnacles National Monument in 1908. In the 1930s the Civilian Conservation Corps cut the steep steps and handrails of the High Peaks trail straight into the rock. It became a national park in 2013, California's newest.

The Chalon and Mutsun people lived in and around these hills. Their descendants in the Amah Mutsun Tribal Band now work with the park on bringing back the careful burning their ancestors used to tend grasslands here.

## Condors

California condors are the largest land birds in North America, with wings nine and a half feet across. By 1982 only 22 were left in the world. The last wild one was captured in 1987 for a captive breeding program that many people thought would fail. Releases began in the 1990s, and Pinnacles became a release site in 2003. There are now several hundred condors flying free in California, Arizona, Utah and Baja. The park's flock mingles with the birds at [[central-coast/big-sur|Big Sur]]. Lead poisoning from bullet fragments in carcasses is still the main thing killing them.

## Look for on the trail

- **Condors versus vultures.** Condors hold their wings flat and steady, with white triangles under the leading edge on adults, and wear numbered tags. Turkey vultures tilt and wobble in a shallow V.
- **CCC steps** on the Steep and Narrow section, with footholds chipped out of the rock.
- **Green and orange lichen** painting the breccia. The park has hundreds of species.
- **Bees.** Pinnacles has around 500 species of bee, one of the densest collections known anywhere.
- **Bear Gulch Reservoir**, built by the CCC, home to the threatened red-legged frog.
