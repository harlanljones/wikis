# Channel Islands

> **On your hike list:** Cavern Point and Potato Harbor, Santa Cruz Island, Channel Islands National Park (5 mile loop, by boat from Ventura)

Santa Cruz Island is the largest island in California, about 96 square miles, with two mountain ranges, a central valley, and more than 70 miles of cliffs and sea caves. It is twenty miles from a metropolitan area of millions, and from the trail to Potato Harbor you will not see a building. People call the Channel Islands the Galápagos of North America, and the comparison is fair.

## Never connected

The northern Channel Islands were never joined to the mainland. Even at the height of the last ice age, with sea level 400 feet lower, a few miles of deep water remained. The four northern islands were joined to each other in one long island, which geologists call Santarosae.

Everything living here arrived by swimming, flying or rafting on driftwood, and then evolved in isolation. About 150 kinds of plants and animals in the islands live nowhere else.

- **Island fox.** A relative of the mainland gray fox that shrank to the size of a house cat. Each of six islands has its own subspecies.
- **Island scrub-jay.** Bigger and bluer than the mainland bird and found only on Santa Cruz, giving it the smallest range of any bird in North America.
- **Pygmy mammoth.** Full-sized Columbian mammoths, which are good swimmers, crossed to Santarosae and, over thousands of generations on a small island with limited food, shrank to about the size of a large horse. A nearly complete skeleton was found on Santa Rosa Island in 1994. They died out around 13,000 years ago.

The pattern is common on islands. Big animals get small and small ones sometimes get big.

## Thirteen thousand years

In 1959 an archaeologist found two human thigh bones eroding out of a gully at Arlington Springs on Santa Rosa Island. They are about 13,000 years old, among the oldest human remains in North America. Since the islands were never connected to the mainland, whoever that was came by boat. The find is a key piece of evidence that the first Americans traveled down the Pacific coast by sea.

Santa Cruz Island is *Limuw* to the Chumash, and in their tradition it is where people were created. About ten villages stood on the island. The Chumash built the *tomol*, a canoe of redwood driftwood planks split with whalebone wedges, sewn together with plant-fiber cord and sealed with natural tar that washes up on the beaches. It was one of the most advanced boats made in the Americas. Island Chumash also ran a mint of sorts. They drilled and ground olive-snail shells into beads that served as money across Southern California.

By 1822 the last islanders had been moved to the mainland missions. Since 2001 Chumash paddlers have made the crossing to Limuw in a tomol nearly every year.

## Ranch years

For 150 years the island was a ranch. Sheep by the tens of thousands stripped the hills, and pigs that escaped or were turned loose went wild and rooted up everything. The buildings at Scorpion Anchorage, where the boat lands, are from that time.

## The fox rescue

In the 1990s the foxes began to vanish. On Santa Cruz the count fell from about 2,000 to roughly a hundred in under a decade. Biologists fitted survivors with radio collars and found the cause, which turned out to be a chain reaction.

1. The pesticide DDT, dumped off Los Angeles for decades, had wiped out the islands' bald eagles by the 1960s. Bald eagles eat fish and chase off other eagles.
2. With them gone, golden eagles from the mainland moved in. They were drawn by a year-round supply of feral piglets.
3. Golden eagles also eat foxes, which had no instinct to look up.

The fix, in the early 2000s, took on all three links at once. Foxes were bred in pens. Golden eagles were trapped and moved to the far side of the Sierra. Bald eagles were brought back. And professional hunters removed more than 5,000 pigs in about a year.

It worked better than anyone hoped. The foxes went from the brink to more than a thousand in ten years, and in 2016 three island subspecies were taken off the endangered list, the fastest recovery of any mammal under the Endangered Species Act.

Today the Nature Conservancy owns the western three quarters of the island and the National Park Service the rest.

## Look for on the trail

- **Foxes** around the campground and picnic tables in broad daylight. They have no fear and will open a zipper.
- **Island scrub-jays** in the oaks of Scorpion Canyon.
- **Sea caves** under Cavern Point. The island has one of the largest in the world, Painted Cave, nearly a quarter mile long.
- **Dolphins.** The boat often meets common dolphins in pods of hundreds or thousands.
- **Giant coreopsis**, a tree-like sunflower that is a bare brown stump in summer and a mass of yellow in early spring.
- **Whales.** Blue and humpback whales feed in the channel in summer.
