# Torrey Pines

> **On your hike list:** Torrey Pines Reserve Trails, Torrey Pines State Natural Reserve, La Jolla (short loops, with a 300 foot descent to the beach)

The Torrey pine is the rarest pine in North America. It grows wild in two places in the world: on these bluffs and a few nearby canyons, and on Santa Rosa Island, 175 miles to the northwest. There are only a few thousand trees on the mainland.

## A leftover

Torrey pines are survivors of a cooler, wetter time, when pine forests grew along much of the Southern California coast. As the climate dried out after the ice ages, the forest shrank back to the one strip where it could hang on.

What saves it here is fog. This coast gets only about ten inches of rain a year, less than some deserts. But in late spring and summer, low clouds sit over the bluffs for weeks. The Torrey pine has long needles, in bundles of five, up to a foot long, and they comb moisture out of the fog so that it drips onto the tree's own roots.

Everything about the tree fits a hard place. Its taproot can go down 25 feet and its side roots spread more than 200. On the exposed cliff edge it grows low and twisted, and in a sheltered gully the same species stands straight and 60 feet tall. Its cones are heavy, take three years to ripen, and hold large seeds that scrub-jays carry off and bury.

Having only one small population is dangerous. During the drought of 2012 to 2016, bark beetles killed a large share of the trees in the reserve. The island trees, separated for thousands of years, have become slightly different, and some scientists have suggested crossing the two to give the mainland trees more genetic variety.

## Named for a teacher

The Kumeyaay gathered the pine nuts here for thousands of years, and the Spanish noted the grove on charts as *Punta de los Árboles*, Point of Trees, a landmark for ships.

In 1850 Charles Parry, a doctor and botanist with the survey marking the new border with Mexico, recognized the tree as an unknown species. He named it for his teacher, John Torrey of New York, the leading American botanist of the day.

## Ellen Browning Scripps

Parry came back in 1883 and was upset to find the grove unprotected, grazed by cattle and cut for firewood. He wrote a plea to save it. The city of San Diego set aside some of its land in 1899, but the best part of the grove, on the north side, was privately owned and about to be subdivided.

Ellen Browning Scripps had made a fortune as a partner in her family's newspaper chain, and she spent her last decades in La Jolla giving it away, to a college, a hospital, an aquarium and an oceanographic institution that all carry her name. Between 1908 and 1911 she bought the pine lands and held them as a free public park. She hired Guy Fleming, a self-taught naturalist, as caretaker. He lived on the grounds for decades and went on to run the state parks of Southern California. In the 1920s Scripps paid for the adobe lodge that is now the visitor center. The land became a state reserve in 1959.

"Reserve" is the strictest category in the state system. It is why there are no picnic tables, and why you may not bring food past the parking lot.

## The cliffs

The bluffs are a clean slice through about 50 million years of history, and the layers are easy to read from the beach.

| Layer | What it was |
| --- | --- |
| Greenish mudstone at beach level | A lagoon, about 48 million years ago, full of oyster and clam fossils |
| Pale, cave-pocked sandstone above it | A sandbar and beach at the lagoon's mouth |
| Red-brown cap on top | An ancient beach terrace, much younger, stained by iron |

The climate then was tropical, and the shoreline of that lagoon ran about where the cliffs are now. The cliffs are soft and retreat by sudden collapses. A fall at a nearby beach killed three people in 2019, which is why signs tell you to stay away from the base.

## Look for on the trail

- **Wind-shaped trees** on the Guy Fleming Trail, and the straight ones in the sheltered gullies.
- **Red Butte and the Razor Point badlands**, where rain has carved the sandstone into fins.
- **Flat Rock**, at the foot of the Beach Trail, with a rectangular hole cut in it. A man tried to mine it for coal in the 1800s.
- **Dolphins** just beyond the surf, and **gray whales** farther out in winter.
- **Hang gliders and paragliders** from the gliderport just south. People have been soaring on the cliff's updraft since the 1920s. Charles Lindbergh flew a glider here in 1930.
- **Los Peñasquitos Lagoon** to the north, one of the last undeveloped salt marshes in Southern California.
