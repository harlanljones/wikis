# Bodie

> **On your hike list:** Bodie Ghost Town, Bodie State Historic Park, near Bridgeport (self-guided walk, elevation 8,375 feet)

"Goodbye, God, I'm going to Bodie." A little girl is supposed to have written that in her diary when her family moved here in 1879. A Bodie newspaper insisted she had been misquoted, and that what she wrote was, "Good, by God, I'm going to Bodie." Both versions tell you something about the town.

## The man who missed it

In 1859 a prospector from Poughkeepsie named W. S. Bodey found gold in the hills north of Mono Lake. That November he was caught in a blizzard while fetching supplies and froze to death. He never saw the town. A sign painter later changed the spelling, supposedly so that nobody would pronounce it "body."

For years it was a minor camp. Then in 1875 a mine caved in and exposed a rich vein, and the Standard Company struck a bonanza the following year. By 1879 Bodie had around 8,000 people and some 2,000 buildings, and for a moment it was one of the largest towns in California.

## The bad man from Bodie

It had a main street a mile long with about 65 saloons, a red-light district, a Chinatown of several hundred people with its own temple, opium dens, three breweries, and a reputation. Newspapers across the West ran jokes about "the bad man from Bodie." Killings were common enough that a minister called it "a sea of sin, lashed by the tempests of lust and passion." There were also churches, a school, union halls, brass bands and baseball teams, and miners who worked ten-hour shifts and went home to their families.

The setting made everything harder. Bodie sits in a treeless bowl at almost 8,400 feet. It is one of the coldest places in the lower 48 states. It can freeze on any night of the year, winter temperatures drop to 20 or 30 below zero, and snow drifts over the roofs. Every stick of lumber and firewood had to be hauled in, at first by wagon and later by a narrow-gauge railroad from sawmills near Mono Lake.

## Electricity over the hill

The boom was over by 1882. The mines kept going on a smaller scale for decades, and that long afterlife produced Bodie's one real contribution to history. In 1892 the Standard mill, tired of paying for firewood, built a hydroelectric plant on a creek thirteen miles away and ran a power line over the hills to its motors. It was one of the first times anywhere that electricity was sent a long distance to do industrial work. The story goes that the builders strung the line dead straight because they were afraid the current might jump off at a corner.

## Arrested decay

A fire in 1932, started by a small boy playing with matches, burned most of the business district. The last mine shut in 1942, and the last residents drifted away. One family, the Cains, who had ended up owning much of the town, hired caretakers to keep out vandals for twenty years.

The state took over in 1962 and made an unusual choice. Bodie is kept in "arrested decay." Roofs are patched and walls propped so the buildings do not fall, but nothing is restored or prettied up. About 110 buildings stand, perhaps a twentieth of the town at its height. Look through the windows and you see what people left: tables set with dishes, a pool table with the balls out, shelves of canned goods, a schoolroom with lessons on the board.

Rangers receive packages every year from visitors returning nails, bottles and bits of glass they pocketed, often with a letter blaming a run of bad luck on the "curse of Bodie." The staff did not start the legend, and they have not tried very hard to stop it.

## Look for on the trail

- **The Standard Mill**, the gray building on the hill, where ore was crushed by stamps and the gold pulled out with mercury and, later, cyanide.
- **The Methodist church**, built in 1882, and the **Miners' Union Hall**, now the museum.
- **The cemetery**, with Bodey's reburied bones, and outside its fence the graves of those the town considered not respectable.
- **The leaning Swazey Hotel**, held up by a beam.
- **Sage grouse.** The hills around Bodie are one of the last strongholds in California of the greater sage grouse.
