# Devils Postpile

> **On your hike list:** Devils Postpile and Rainbow Falls, Devils Postpile National Monument, near Mammoth Lakes (5 miles round trip)

The Postpile is a cliff of basalt columns sixty feet high, so straight and regular that they look machined. On top, where a glacier sanded them flat, they fit together like floor tiles. The explanation is simple and has to do with how things shrink.

## Why six sides

A little less than 100,000 years ago, a vent a couple of miles upstream poured basalt lava into the valley of the Middle Fork of the San Joaquin River. The lava pooled behind a natural dam, in places 400 feet deep.

A thick, still pool of lava cools slowly and evenly. As it cools it shrinks, and the shrinking pulls it apart. Cracks start at points spaced evenly across the cooling surface, and the most efficient way for a surface to crack under even tension is in a pattern of hexagons, the same one you see in drying mud or a honeycomb. The cracks then grow straight inward as the cooling front advances, turning a flat pattern into long columns.

The Postpile is among the best examples in the world because the lava was unusually uniform and the cooling unusually steady. Count the sides: a little under half the columns are six-sided, most of the rest have five, and a few have four or seven. Some columns curve, where the cooling surface was not flat.

Then came ice. About 20,000 years ago a glacier more than a thousand feet thick moved down the valley. It quarried away most of the lava flow and left this one wall. On top it ground the column ends smooth and left scratches showing which way it was traveling.

## Almost blown up

When Yosemite National Park was created in 1890, the Postpile was inside it. In 1905, under pressure from miners and loggers, Congress cut 500 square miles off the park, including this valley. A few years later someone applied for a permit to dynamite the Postpile and use the rubble to build a dam on the river for a mining operation.

A Forest Service engineer named Walter Huber was sent the application, and instead of approving it he raised the alarm. The Sierra Club took it to Washington. In 1911 President Taft proclaimed Devils Postpile National Monument.

## Rainbow Falls

Two and a half miles downstream the river drops 101 feet over a cliff of volcanic rock. The lip is a hard layer and the rock under it is softer, so the falls undercut themselves and stay vertical, slowly working upstream. They have retreated about 500 feet since they formed. Around midday in summer the mist throws a strong rainbow.

Much of the forest on the way burned in the Rainbow Fire of 1992, which was started by lightning. Thirty years later the regrowth is still mostly brush and young pines, an honest look at how slowly high forests recover.

## The neighbors

The Postpile is a small feature of a very large volcanic system. Ten miles east is the Long Valley Caldera, a basin about 20 miles long and 10 wide. It formed in a single eruption about 760,000 years ago that threw out some 150 cubic miles of magma, on the order of two thousand times more than Mount St. Helens in 1980. Ash fell as far as Nebraska. The hot ash that stayed close to home welded itself into the Bishop Tuff, the pinkish cliffs along US 395.

Mammoth Mountain, where you catch the shuttle, is a younger volcano on the rim of the caldera. In the 1990s trees began dying on its slopes, killed by carbon dioxide seeping up through the soil from magma below. Warning signs near Horseshoe Lake still mark the area.

## Look for on the trail

- **The top of the Postpile.** Take the short, steep trail up. The polished hexagons are the best part.
- **The talus pile** of broken columns at the base, where frost and earthquakes have toppled them.
- **Soda Springs**, bubbly mineral springs on a gravel bar upstream.
- **Hikers with big packs.** The John Muir Trail and the Pacific Crest Trail both pass through the monument.
- **Red fir and lodgepole pine**, and the fire scar on the way to the falls.
