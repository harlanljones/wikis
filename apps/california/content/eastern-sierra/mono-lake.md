# Mono Lake

> **On your hike list:** South Tufa Trail, Mono Lake Tufa State Natural Reserve, near Lee Vining (1 mile, flat)

Mono Lake is at least 760,000 years old, which makes it one of the oldest lakes in North America. It has no outlet. Streams bring in fresh water and a little dissolved salt, the desert sun takes the water, and the salt stays. After three quarters of a million years the lake is more than twice as salty as the ocean and strongly alkaline, with a pH near 10. The water feels slippery, like soap. Mark Twain called it the "Dead Sea of California." He was wrong about the dead part.

## A simple, enormous food chain

No fish can live in Mono Lake. That leaves the water to two small animals.

- **Brine shrimp**, a species found only here, about the size of a fingernail clipping. In summer there are an estimated four to six trillion of them.
- **Alkali flies**, which cover the shore in a dark band and move away from your feet in a wave. They do not bite. They walk underwater inside a bubble of air to graze on algae.

Birds come from two continents to eat them. Most of California's nesting California gulls are hatched on the lake's islands. In late summer more than a million eared grebes stop to fatten up, along with tens of thousands of phalaropes, small shorebirds that double their weight here before flying nonstop to South America.

The Kutzadika'a, the Paiute people of the Mono Basin, harvested the fly pupae, which wash ashore in windrows, dried them and traded them across the Sierra. The pupae are rich in fat and taste, by most accounts, a bit like shrimp. The name Mono comes from the neighboring Yokuts' word for them, roughly "fly eaters."

## How tufa grows

Tufa is limestone, and it forms underwater. Fresh springs carrying calcium bubble up through the lake bed. The lake water is loaded with carbonates. Where the two mix, they make calcium carbonate, which builds up around the spring into a tower, a few inches a century.

So the towers at South Tufa should not be visible. You can walk among them because the lake has been lowered.

## Los Angeles comes for the water

By the 1930s Los Angeles had drained the [[eastern-sierra/manzanar|Owens Valley]] to the south and wanted more. The city bought up the streams that feed Mono Lake and, in 1941, began sending their water south through an eleven-mile tunnel bored under the Mono Craters.

Over the next forty years the lake fell 45 vertical feet. It lost half its volume, and its salinity doubled. Miles of white lake bed were exposed, and the wind raised clouds of alkali dust. In the late 1970s the falling water turned Negit Island, the main gull rookery, into a peninsula. Coyotes walked across and the gulls abandoned it. Scientists warned that at somewhat higher salinity the shrimp and flies would stop reproducing and the whole system would crash.

## The students

In 1976 a dozen college students spent a summer doing the first full ecological study of the lake. One of them, David Gaines, founded the Mono Lake Committee in 1978 with his partner Sally Judy, working from a kitchen table and then from an old dance hall in Lee Vining. Their bumper sticker read "Save Mono Lake." A later one said "Long Live Mono Lake."

They and the Audubon Society sued. In 1983 the California Supreme Court ruled that the state holds lakes and streams in trust for the public, and that this "public trust" applies even to water rights granted long ago. It was one of the most important water rulings in the country's history. In 1994 the state water board ordered Los Angeles to cut its diversions until the lake rises to 6,392 feet above sea level, about 20 feet above its low point.

Gaines did not see it. He was killed in a car crash in a snowstorm in 1988.

The lake was expected to reach its target in about twenty years. Droughts kept setting it back. In early 2025 it stood at about 6,383 feet, still nine feet short, and the argument about how much water the city may take is open again.

## Very young volcanoes

The gray hills south of the lake are the Mono Craters, the youngest mountain range in North America, a chain of volcanic domes that began erupting about 40,000 years ago. Panum Crater, by the road to South Tufa, erupted roughly 650 years ago. Paoha, the big white island, was pushed up from the lake bottom by magma only about 350 years ago. This is one of the most likely places in California for the next eruption.

## Look for on the trail

- **The bathtub ring.** Signs on the walk from the parking lot mark where the shore stood in past decades.
- **Sand tufa**, delicate tubes and columns formed inside the sand and uncovered by wind.
- **Flies moving like a carpet**, and gulls running through them with their beaks open.
- **Ospreys nesting on tufa towers** offshore. They fly to fresh water for fish.
- **Violet-green swallows** nesting in holes in the towers.
