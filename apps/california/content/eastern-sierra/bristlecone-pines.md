# The Bristlecone Pines

> **On your hike list:** Methuselah Trail, Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest, White Mountains (4.5 mile loop, 900 feet of climbing)

Somewhere along this trail is a tree that sprouted before the pyramids at Giza were built. It is unmarked on purpose. It looks much like its neighbors: short, twisted, mostly dead wood, with one strip of bark feeding a few green branches.

## Dr. Schulman's last discovery

Edmund Schulman was a tree-ring scientist from the University of Arizona who spent his summers in the 1930s through the 1950s looking for very old trees. He wanted long records of rainfall. He assumed, as everyone did, that the oldest trees would be the biggest, growing in the best conditions.

In 1953 a ranger told him about some gnarled pines high in the White Mountains. Schulman came to look and found something that turned the assumption upside down. The oldest trees were on the worst sites: dry, rocky, windy slopes where they barely grew at all. In 1957 he cored a tree with more than 4,600 rings and named it Methuselah. Its age is now put at about 4,850 years. For decades it was the oldest known living thing that is not a clone.

Schulman published his find in *National Geographic* in 1958 under the title "Bristlecone Pine, Oldest Known Living Thing." He had died of a heart attack two months earlier, at 49.

## How to live for five thousand years

Great Basin bristlecones do almost everything slowly, and that is the trick.

- **Location.** The White Mountains sit in the rain shadow of the Sierra and get about twelve inches of moisture a year, mostly as snow. The oldest trees grow on dolomite, a pale, alkaline rock so poor that almost nothing else can grow on it. There is no underbrush and trees stand far apart, so fire cannot spread.
- **Dense wood.** A tree may add an inch of thickness in a century. The wood is so tight and full of resin that insects and rot can hardly get in. In the cold, dry air, dead wood lasts for thousands of years more.
- **Dying back.** When drought or damage kills some roots, the tree lets the part of the trunk they fed die too. An ancient bristlecone may be nine-tenths dead wood, with a single ribbon of bark keeping a small crown alive.
- **Thrifty needles.** A bristlecone holds its needles for thirty or forty years, far longer than any other pine.

## The trees that corrected the clock

Because dead bristlecone wood survives so long, scientists were able to match ring patterns between living trees, dead snags and fallen logs, and build one continuous calendar of rings going back nearly 9,000 years. Each ring has an exact date.

In the 1960s that calendar was used to test radiocarbon dating, which was new and assumed to be accurate. Scientists measured the carbon in rings of known age and found that the method was off, by as much as several centuries for older samples. The corrections showed that Stonehenge and other stone monuments of prehistoric Europe were much older than anyone had thought, too old to have been copied from Egypt or Greece. The textbooks of European prehistory had to be rewritten because of a pine from Inyo County.

## Prometheus

In 1964 a graduate student studying glaciers on Wheeler Peak, in eastern Nevada, got permission from the Forest Service to cut down a bristlecone after his coring tool broke. When the rings were counted there were more than 4,800. He had cut down a tree older than Methuselah, the oldest ever known at the time. The tree, called Prometheus, became a cautionary tale, and it is the main reason nobody will tell you which tree here is the old one.

## Look for on the trail

- **The color line.** White dolomite slopes with scattered bristlecones, and right next to them brown sandstone covered in sagebrush with hardly a tree.
- **Cones with a bristle** on each scale. Young cones are deep purple.
- **Sculpted wood**, polished gold and black by blowing ice and sand.
- **The view west** to the Sierra crest across the Owens Valley, which is two miles deep at this point.
- **The thin air.** The trail is at 10,000 feet. White Mountain Peak, up the road, is 14,252 feet, the third highest summit in California.
