# Manzanar

> **On your hike list:** Manzanar National Historic Site, US 395 between Lone Pine and Independence (3.2 mile tour road, self-guided)

*Manzanar* is Spanish for apple orchard. The square mile of sagebrush beside the highway has been taken from people three times: from the Paiute, then from the farmers who planted the apples, and then, in 1942, from ten thousand people, most of them American citizens, who were put behind barbed wire here because of their ancestry.

## Paiute land

The Owens Valley Paiute had lived along the creeks coming down from the Sierra for many centuries. They dug ditches to spread the water over meadows of wild seed and root crops, one of the few cases of irrigation by people who did not otherwise farm. Ranchers and miners arrived in the early 1860s and their cattle ate the fields. After two years of fighting, the Army rounded up about a thousand Paiute in 1863 and marched them 175 miles over the mountains to Fort Tejon. Many slipped home, and their descendants live on four reservations in the valley today.

## The orchard town

In 1910 a developer laid out the town of Manzanar, with irrigation pipes, a school, a general store and thousands of apple, pear and peach trees. The fruit was good. But Los Angeles had opened its [[big-picture/water|aqueduct]] in 1913 and was buying land throughout the valley for the water that came with it. By the late 1920s the city owned Manzanar. It shut off the irrigation, and by the mid-1930s the town was empty.

## Executive Order 9066

On February 19, 1942, ten weeks after the attack on Pearl Harbor, President Franklin Roosevelt signed an order allowing the Army to remove anyone it chose from the West Coast. It was used against people of Japanese ancestry. About 120,000 were forced from their homes, and two thirds of them were American citizens. They were given days to sell or store what they owned and could bring only what they could carry. No one was charged with anything, and there were no hearings.

Manzanar was the first of ten camps to open, in March 1942. At its peak it held 10,046 people, and 11,070 passed through in all, most of them from Los Angeles.

## Life in Block after Block

The camp was 36 blocks of tarpaper barracks inside a fence with eight guard towers. Each family got one room, about 20 by 25 feet, with cots and a bare bulb. Wind drove dust through the knotholes and up through the floorboards. Latrines had no partitions. Summers passed 100 degrees and winter nights fell below freezing.

People made a town of it anyway. They ran schools, a hospital, a newspaper, churches and a cooperative store. They farmed several hundred acres and brought the old orchards back. They made camouflage nets for the Army. They built baseball diamonds and more than a hundred gardens, some with ponds, bridges and rock work, whose outlines you can still find in the brush.

Tension inside the camp, over informers, low pay and stolen food supplies, broke out on December 6, 1942. Military police fired into a crowd, killing two young men and wounding several more.

In 1943 the government handed every adult a "loyalty questionnaire." Those who answered wrong were sent to a harsher camp at Tule Lake. Others volunteered for the Army straight from the barracks. The Japanese American 442nd Regimental Combat Team became one of the most decorated units in American history.

## Three photographers

Dorothea Lange photographed the removal for the government in 1942, and her pictures were so plainly critical that the Army impounded them for the rest of the war. Ansel Adams came in 1943 as a friend of the camp director and made dignified portraits meant to show the incarcerated as loyal Americans. The third, Toyo Miyatake, was a prisoner. Cameras were forbidden, so he smuggled in a lens and had a carpenter build a wooden box around it. He photographed daily life from the inside, and the director eventually let him work in the open.

## Remembering

The camp closed in November 1945. Many people had nothing to go back to. The barracks were sold for scrap lumber.

In December 1969 about 150 people, most of them students, drove to Manzanar for what they called a pilgrimage. It has been held every April since. The campaign that grew from it led Congress, in 1988, to formally apologize and pay $20,000 to each surviving detainee. A federal commission had found that the incarceration was caused by "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership." Manzanar became a National Historic Site in 1992.

## Look for on the trail

- **The cemetery monument**, a white obelisk built by stonemason Ryozo Kado in 1943. The characters on the front read "soul consoling tower."
- **Two stone sentry posts** at the entrance, also Kado's work.
- **The auditorium**, the one large original building, now the visitor center.
- **Garden ruins**, especially Merritt Park and the mess hall gardens.
- **Old fruit trees**, survivors of the orchard town.
- **Mount Williamson**, 14,379 feet, filling the western sky. It is in half the photographs.
